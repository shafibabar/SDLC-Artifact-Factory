# Competitive Alternatives and the Buyer's Evaluation Criteria

Self-contained. Read this when listing the alternatives (step 2), writing the buyer's evaluation criteria (step 3), choosing the positioning map's axes, or decomposing a composite matrix cell. It holds the taxonomy, the prompts for finding alternatives a team overlooks, the method for deriving and weighting criteria, the category lens, and Moore's whole product.

---

## 1. The alternatives taxonomy

List in this order. The order matters: the first rows are the ones teams skip, and the ones most deals are lost to.

| Type | Definition | How to find it |
|---|---|---|
| **Doing nothing / non-consumption** | The customer tolerates the problem, or nobody does the job at all until something forces it (Christensen et al., *Competing Against Luck*: non-consumption is often the largest market) | "What happens today if nobody does anything?" "What forces it: an audit, a deadline, a complaint?" |
| **No decision** (B2B only) | The buying group cannot agree on the problem and drifts back to the status quo (Adamson and Dixon, *The Challenger Customer*) | Count the roles in the stakeholder map's buying committee: the more roles, the likelier this outcome. Name the role most likely to defend the status quo |
| **Manual workaround / substitute** | Same need met by different means: a spreadsheet, email reminders, a consultant, a hire, an in-house script | "What would they fire if they hired us?" (Christensen's hiring/firing) |
| **Direct competitors** | Products that solve the same problem for the same buyer | Named vendors the buyer would shortlist |
| **Indirect competitors** | Products that solve part of the problem, or a related one, and so absorb the budget | Modules bundled in a suite the buyer already pays for; adjacent tools |
| **Future entrants** | Players not in the space with the means to enter: platforms that could bundle the capability, adjacent vendors | "Who could ship this as a free checkbox in something the buyer already owns?" |

Dunford (*Obviously Awesome*) makes the alternatives the first step of positioning: you cannot know your unique attributes until you know what you are being compared against. Doing nothing is always on the list.

**How many:** lean 2–4 in total; standard 2–3 substitutes plus 3–6 vendors; enterprise the same per segment. Never more than you can score honestly.

**Which alternatives a buyer actually considers** is evidence, not assumption: the best source is the user's interview and lost-deal notes ("what were you using before?", "what else did you look at?"). Where there is none, mark the list `hypothesis` and say which design-partner conversation will confirm it.

## 2. Deriving the buyer's evaluation criteria

The criteria are the questions the buyer asks when choosing, not the product's feature list. Derive them **before** scoring anyone, from these sources in order of strength:

1. **What buyers said or did**: interview notes, trial exit surveys, lost-deal reasons, RFP sections. Quote their words.
2. **The buying committee** (`stakeholder-mapping`): each role brings its own criteria. A finance approver weighs cost and switching risk; a daily user weighs effort; security weighs data handling. At enterprise, write criteria per role.
3. **The market category's criteria.** The category the buyer places the product in brings a set of expectations with it (Dunford's shelf analogy: the aisle sets the evaluation criteria before anyone reads the label). If you are filed under "project management tool", the buyer will ask about Gantt charts whether or not you care about them.
4. **The job** (`jtbd-analysis`, if written): the desired outcomes behind the job.

Then **weight** them (high / medium / low, or a 1–5 weight), from the same evidence. A criterion with no evidence behind its weight is marked `hypothesis`.

**Table stakes vs differentiators.** A criterion where every alternative is strong is table stakes: the buyer will not consider a product without it, but it differentiates nothing. Hand table stakes to `requirements-analysis`; never make one a differentiation point.

**The axes test.** The positioning map's two axes are the two heaviest buyer criteria (or, where two heavy criteria correlate, the heaviest pair that does not). Do not choose the axes and then justify them. If the product shares its space with an alternative on the buyer's axes, record it: it tells the kernel the advantage must come from elsewhere, or that a narrower segment weighs the criteria differently.

## 3. The category lens (enterprise, or whenever the category is open)

The market category is chosen in `gtm-strategy` (Dunford's three options: fit an existing category, create a subcategory, create a new category). This analysis supplies the evidence for that choice by showing how the criteria and the competitive set change under each candidate:

| Candidate category | Criteria it brings | Who the buyer compares us with | Where we stand |
|---|---|---|---|
| [existing category] | [criteria buyers in that category expect] | [the category's incumbents] | [usually: behind on breadth] |
| [subcategory: "the only X for Y"] | [the category's criteria, re-weighted for Y] | [a smaller set] | [where the differentiation points count] |

Do not decide the category here; record the comparison and hand it on.

## 4. Whole-product decomposition (Moore)

Moore (*Crossing the Chasm*, after Levitt) separates the **generic product** (what ships) from the **whole product** (everything the target buyer needs to get the result: integrations, onboarding and support, documentation, partners, proof such as references and certifications). Pragmatist buyers buy the whole product.

Use it to split any composite cell before scoring. "Fit for small firms" becomes:

| Ring | Example sub-criterion |
|---|---|
| Generic product | Does the core capability work at their scale? |
| Expected product | Setup effort; price within their budget authority |
| Augmented product | Integrations with the tools they already use; support hours |
| Proof | References from firms like them; certifications they are asked for |

A substitute often wins on the augmented and proof rings (a consultant comes with references), which is exactly where a flattering matrix hides it.

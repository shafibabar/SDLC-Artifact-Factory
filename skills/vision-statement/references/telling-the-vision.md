# Telling the Vision — From Logline to Spoken Story

Self-contained. Use this when a vision statement must be **told** rather than read: onboarding a new team member, opening an investor or board conversation, or winning over a sceptical stakeholder. The ≤ 60-word vision artifact stays as it is. This produces a companion version, never a replacement.

## Why a told version exists

A vision statement is deliberately compressed: one logline, five components in miniature. That compression makes it durable and checkable, but it rarely moves a room. *Business Storytelling for Dummies* (Dietz & Silverman) builds a core story once and then **resizes it for the medium** (Ch. 9): shorter for an elevator, longer for a keynote, with the same underlying claim. Luhn (*The Best Story Wins*) adds the craft rules: the customer is the hero, the product is the enabler, and a short form compresses the story's beats rather than dropping them.

## The technique

1. **Keep the logline's claim intact.** The told version may add colour; it may not add a new promise.
2. **Turn the "today's need" component into one scene.** Replace the category-level pain ("compliance teams can't see their data") with a single concrete moment: a person, a day, a consequence. Dietz & Silverman call this a "hip pocket" story (Ch. 4): one held ready for a recurring situation.
3. **Keep the protagonist the hero.** The scene is about *them*; the product enters only as what makes the future state possible.
4. **Land on the future state and why it matters.** End on the changed world, not on a feature.
5. **Close with the durable bet,** in one sentence.
6. **Size it to the room:** 120–180 words (about one spoken minute) by default; 30–40 words for an elevator, in which case you are back near the logline.

## Check before using it

- Does the listener know whose world changes, and how, after hearing it? (Dietz & Silverman test a story by its effect on the audience, not by its plot.)
- Is every claim also in, or implied by, the ≤ 60-word artifact?
- Would it survive a competitor's name being mentioned? If it only works by comparison, it's positioning (see `gtm-strategy`), not vision.

## Shape of a told vision (illustrative)

> *Scene.* Last March, Priya, the one-person compliance team at a 200-person clinic group, spent nine days before an audit opening shared drives one folder at a time, still unsure she'd found every file with patient data.
> *Future.* We're building toward a world where someone in Priya's seat can answer "what sensitive data do we hold, and where?" in minutes, on any day, not just before an audit.
> *Why it matters.* Smaller regulated organisations shouldn't carry more compliance risk than enterprises simply because they can't afford a team to look.
> *Durable bet.* And they shouldn't have to hand their data to anyone to get that answer: it never leaves their own boundary.

# Pricing — Reference

Self-contained. Read this when writing the GTM's Pricing Model, gathering or recording willingness-to-pay evidence, choosing a charging unit or tiers, or checking a pricing section before hand-off. Source: Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke, *Monetizing Innovation* (Simon-Kucher & Partners), reconciled with Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*. A practitioner book: actionable, not proven; its case studies are illustrations, and no statistic from it is quoted here.

**Ownership.** The GTM's Pricing Model owns price levels, tiers, the price strategy and (at enterprise) fences and the discount policy. The business model canvas records the revenue type, charging unit, value anchor and WTP evidence and points here. A planned `pricing-and-packaging` skill will own the full enterprise tier matrix and pricing business case; until it exists, they live in this section.

---

## 1. Price first, not last

The book's central claim: products fail to make money mostly because pricing is the last step (build, then ask "what can we charge?"). Invert it: the willingness-to-pay conversation happens with the target segment **before** the offer is configured, and it decides what goes in the box, for whom, under which model and at what price. In this plugin the GTM is written before Ideate, so the GTM's pricing can and should shape what is built.

### The four monetisation failures

| Failure | What happens | The check |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature shock** | Everything goes in; the product is confusing and priced above what any one segment values | Does any tier carry fillers or killers that add cost or confusion? An agent-built product is especially exposed: adding a feature costs an agent little |
| **Minivation** | The right product priced too low, or aimed too small; it sells but leaves value uncaptured. It looks like success | Is the price far below the value anchor and the top of the stated WTP range, with no penetration rationale? |
| **Hidden gem** | A valuable offer nobody pursues | Is a high-WTP segment left unserved? |
| **Undead** | Launched with no demand check; it lingers | Is there any evidence anyone will pay? |

Run all four before hand-off.

## 2. Willingness-to-pay evidence

Ask, for a described offer, rather than "how much would you pay?":
- the price that would be **acceptable**, the price that would be **expensive** but still considered, and the price that would be **prohibitively expensive**: a range, not a point;
- a **forced trade-off** among features or benefits (rank them, or split a fixed budget), so "everything matters" is not an answer;
- purchase likelihood at one or two stated prices, and what would have to change to buy.

**Reconciling with the Mom Test.** Fitzpatrick warns against hypothetical "would you buy" questions. Treat stated WTP as a **range hypothesis**, labelled *stated*, and upgrade it to *committed* only on a commitment: a pre-order, a letter of intent, a paid pilot, a signed price. (This reconciliation is a synthesis, not a quotation of either book.)

**Record:** who was asked, how many, stated vs committed, and the range per segment. The agent cannot hold these conversations; it drafts the questions and the user (or a design-partner session) asks them.

**Segment by needs and WTP, then describe by firmographics.** Customers differ in what they need and what they will pay; one price for everyone overcharges some, undercharges others and loses the rest. The beachhead (Moore) picks *which* segment to win first; WTP segmentation decides *what to sell them and at what price*.

## 3. Three decisions: model, charging unit, packaging

**(a) Monetisation model** — how the product charges:

| Model | Fits when | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription | Ongoing value, predictable for both sides | Churn |
| Usage-based | Value scales with consumption or outcomes | Unpredictable bills |
| Freemium | The free tier is a genuine funnel and the fence to paid is clear | Trains buyers that the product is free; free-tier cost to serve |
| Dynamic | Demand or capacity varies | Perceived unfairness |
| Indirect / outcome-based | A third party pays, or price tracks a measurable result | Measuring the result |

The book treats *how* you charge as often worth more than *how much*.

**(b) Charging unit** — the "per what" (the book's price metric). Three tests: does it **track the value** the customer receives; can the customer **predict their bill**; does it **grow as the customer succeeds**? Record the units rejected and why. A unit that grows with something the customer wants to shrink (data volume when they want less data) fails the first test.

**(c) Packaging** — one offer, or tiers:
- **Leaders / fillers / killers**, per segment: leaders the segment values and pays for (they define the offer and justify the price); fillers with little stand-alone WTP (they round out a package and must not drive cost); killers that make the segment *less* likely to buy (complexity, unwanted commitments): keep them out of that segment's package even if another segment wants them.
- **Good-better-best**: each tier aimed at one segment, its leaders inside, separated by **fences** (a usage limit, a feature, a contract term) that stop a high-WTP customer from buying down.
- Bundle when it raises total WTP; never when it forces a killer on someone.

## 4. Value anchor, price strategy, price range

- **Value anchor**: the quantified economic value to the customer (hours saved × loaded cost, losses avoided, revenue recovered). It is the **ceiling**. Label the inputs: an assumption until Stage 1 evidence confirms it.
- **WTP**: what the segment will actually pay; the **evidence** for where the price sits under the ceiling.
- **Cost** is a floor for viability, never the basis: cost-plus pricing donates the gap between cost and value to the customer permanently.
- **Price strategy**: **maximisation** (the default for differentiated and most B2B products); **penetration** only with a named network effect, scale economy or lock-in, otherwise it is planned minivation, and raising a price later is far harder than lowering it; **skimming** only with a named premium early segment and a planned step-down. The strategy must match the positioning: a premium differentiation claim cannot sell at a penetration price.
- **State a price range per tier**, derived from the WTP range and the value anchor. List prices are refined in Stage 2 against acceptance. Never defer to a "commercial plan" no artifact produces.

**Behavioural presentation** (enterprise, after the economics are right): the first or highest price seen anchors the rest; with three options many buyers choose the middle, so the intended best-seller goes in the middle; how a price is partitioned (per month vs per year, per user vs flat) changes how it feels; a price that is too low can signal low quality to a risk-averse buyer. These amplify a good configuration; they do not rescue a bad one.

## 5. Price integrity at launch

Everyone who sells carries the value story; the price is defended by value, not by discount. Discounts only in exchange for something (term, volume, a reference) — a **give-get** — with a named limit on who can grant how much. Unsustainable introductory prices and ad-hoc discounts train buyers to wait. After launch, win/loss outcomes and price objections feed back into configuration and price.

## 6. What the pricing section holds, by scale

| Scale | Minimum content |
|---|---|
| **lean** (GTM skipped) | In the Lean Canvas Revenue block: "not monetised" with the reason, or model, charging unit and one price point with its WTP basis ("3 conversations, stated"). No tiers |
| **standard** | WTP evidence summary (who, how many, stated / committed, range per segment); model; charging unit with the three tests; packaging (single offer or tiers, leaders per tier); value anchor; price strategy with justification; a price range per tier; the Stage 2 price-acceptance exit; the four-failure check |
| **enterprise** | Standard, plus: leaders / fillers / killers per segment; good-better-best tiers with explicit fences; bundling and add-on decisions; behavioural presentation choices; a discount and price-integrity policy; price review triggers (win/loss, price objections). A bottom-up business case (segment size × adoption at the price × price, minus cost to serve, with sensitivity on price and adoption) belongs to `pricing-and-packaging` once it exists |

Never quote a conversion-rate or pricing benchmark that has no source; set targets from the product's own evidence.

# Lean Analytics
**Authors:** Alistair Croll and Benjamin Yoskovitz
**Domain:** Data & Analytics — Metric Selection and Validation

## Core Concepts

- **Actionable metrics vs. vanity metrics — the book's central distinction.** A metric is actionable if it ties to a specific, repeatable action a team can take, and the number changing tells you whether that action worked. A vanity metric moves in a reassuring direction (usually up and to the right) and makes everyone feel good, but no one changes behavior because of it — registered users, page views, and total downloads are the book's stock examples, because they accumulate monotonically regardless of whether the product is actually succeeding. The book gives four concrete tests for whether a metric is good, not vanity: it should be **comparative** (compare against another time period, cohort, or competitor, so a bare number becomes a trend or a ranking), **understandable** (simple enough that people across the organization can remember it and argue about it, not just the analyst who computed it), **a ratio or a rate** (rates resist gaming and reveal the real story better than raw counts — "conversion rate" tells you more than "signups," because a rate has a denominator baked in), and it should **change how you behave** — if you can't say what you'd do differently at a different value, it isn't actionable, full stop.
- **One Metric That Matters (OMTM).** The book's second major device: at any given moment, a team should identify a single metric — the one number that, more than any other, reflects whether the biggest current risk to the business is being resolved — and organize attention around it, deliberately ignoring dashboards full of secondary numbers until that risk is resolved. The OMTM is explicitly **temporary and stage-relative**: it is expected to change, often every few weeks or months, as the biggest open question changes (from "will anyone use this" to "will they come back" to "will they pay"). Croll and Yoskovitz frame picking an OMTM as an act of focus and prioritization discipline as much as a measurement choice — it exists to stop a team from quietly optimizing five different numbers that pull in different directions, and to force a single, falsifiable bet about what matters most right now.
- **The Lean Analytics stages.** The book organizes what's worth measuring by which stage a business is in, typically summarized as **Empathy** (do people actually have the problem you think they have, and will they engage with a proposed solution — largely qualitative at this stage), **Stickiness** (do early users come back and engage repeatedly — the retention question), **Virality** (does usage spread from user to user without paid acquisition), **Revenue** (can the business extract sustainable value per customer), and **Scale** (can growth be pushed hard once the model is proven). A later, more elaborate part of the book cross-references these stages against different business-model archetypes (e-commerce, SaaS, mobile apps, media/content sites, two-sided marketplaces, user-generated-content platforms), each with its own suggested OMTM candidates per stage — the book's implicit claim is that "what to measure" is never a fixed list, it's a function of *both* stage and business-model shape.
- **Qualitative validation before quantitative commitment.** Consistent with its position in the Lean Startup lineage (the book is part of Eric Ries's Lean Series and explicitly extends Build-Measure-Learn into a metrics discipline), the book repeatedly insists that qualitative work — customer interviews, "get out of the building" conversations, watching someone actually use the product — comes first, to generate a hypothesis about what will matter. Quantitative metrics then validate or kill that hypothesis at a scale a handful of interviews can't reach. A number without a preceding qualitative hypothesis is exploration, not validation; a hypothesis never checked against a number is just an opinion.
- **Cohort analysis as the antidote to misleading aggregate trends.** A specific, recurring technique: cumulative totals (total signups, total users, total files processed) almost always trend up and to the right regardless of whether the product is actually improving, because they never subtract departed or disengaged users. The book's fix is to segment users into **cohorts** by when they started, and compare each cohort's behavior (typically retention or engagement) over the same elapsed time since signup — if the cohort that joined this month retains better at day 30 than the cohort that joined three months ago, the product is genuinely improving; the cumulative-total graph would show growth in both cases and couldn't tell the difference. This is the book's primary mechanism for deciding a metric — usually a retention or stickiness metric — is genuinely decision-worthy rather than a comforting illusion produced by an ever-growing denominator.
- **The "if you could only know one thing" test.** A lightweight heuristic the book offers for finding the OMTM: imagine you could see only a single number about the business tomorrow morning — what would it need to be for you to know whether you're winning or losing? Whatever number answers that question is the current OMTM candidate; if the honest answer is "I'd need to see three or four numbers together," the team hasn't yet resolved what its single biggest risk actually is.

## Actionable Techniques

- Before accepting any proposed metric, run it against the book's four-part test: is it comparative, is it understandable to a non-analyst, is it expressed as a ratio/rate rather than a raw count, and does a different value change what anyone does.
- At any point in a build, be able to name the current One Metric That Matters and the specific risk it's resolving; treat a team (or agent-driven backlog) tracking five "important" metrics with equal weight as a sign the biggest risk hasn't actually been identified yet.
- Expect the OMTM to expire. Revisit it deliberately — don't let it become permanent by default once the question it was answering is settled.
- Before committing to build a retention-, engagement-, or stickiness-flavored metric, validate it with a cohort comparison (segment by start date, compare like-elapsed-time behavior) rather than trusting a cumulative or point-in-time aggregate — a rising cumulative count can coexist with declining per-cohort retention.
- Generate the hypothesis for a metric qualitatively (a direct conversation with the person who'll act on it) before instrumenting it quantitatively; a metric nobody hypothesized about first is a fishing expedition, not a validated requirement.
- When assessing whether a business-model-specific stage framework applies, check the fit against the actual product shape (funded/unfunded, viral/non-viral, one product/many, solo-operator/team) before importing the framework wholesale — the book's own stage-metric tables are explicitly conditioned on business-model archetype, so a mismatched archetype should not be force-applied.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

**Not proposed.** No new skill or agent clears the bar this repo's prior research files have set for a genuine, non-duplicative gap.

- A "lean startup stage framework" or "OMTM" skill was the most natural-sounding candidate and is explicitly declined. The book's Empathy/Stickiness/Virality/Revenue/Scale progression and its business-model-specific metric tables are built for a team actively searching for product-market fit through iterative pivots, typically with a viral or paid-acquisition growth loop and multiple people who need a rotating focal metric to stay coordinated. This repo's first product is a single B2B compliance/data-estate-mapping platform with named personas, a design-partner validation motion, and an OKR set already defined at Strategy (`okr-authoring`) — there is no viral loop to measure (Virality has no plausible instantiation here), "Scale" in the book's growth-hacking sense is not the current or likely-next concern, and the team-coordination problem OMTM is built to solve (stopping multiple people from silently optimizing different numbers) is structurally weaker with one operator directing one agent per domain. Empathy and Stickiness loosely echo the product's current MVP-validation and early-retention questions, but that overlap is coincidental, not a reason to import the full five-stage apparatus as a standing skill.
- A standalone "cohort analysis" skill was considered and also declined — cohort analysis is a technique, not a discipline with its own elicitation method, template, or quality criteria; it belongs as an addition inside `analytics-requirements` (as a vanity-metric-check technique) and `metrics-instrumentation-plan` (as a computation-logic requirement), the same way `data-warehouse-toolkit-kimball-ross.md` folded SCD types and grain declaration into existing skills rather than proposing a new one.
- The right size of change, if any of this is adopted, is a few paragraphs inside `analytics-requirements` (citation, the "understandable" criterion, the OMTM/North-Star clarification, the cohort-analysis technique) — not a new component.

## Key Terminology

- **Vanity Metric** — already in active, correct use in this repo (`analytics-requirements`, `okr-authoring`'s Common Failure Modes table) prior to this research, independently matching the book's own definition. Not a new proposal; noted here only to confirm the repo's existing usage is accurate and consistent with the term's primary source.
- **One Metric That Matters (OMTM)** — the book's precise term for a stage-relative, deliberately rotating single-focus metric. Genuinely distinct from this repo's North Star Metric (see the `okr-authoring` row above) and worth a precise glossary note *only if* the repo ever formally adopts stage-based rotating focus as a practice — premature to add to the Ubiquitous Language glossary on the strength of one skill's cross-reference alone.
- **Cohort Analysis** — a specific, well-defined technique (segment by start date, compare like-elapsed-time behavior) rather than a loose synonym for "segmentation." Precise enough to be useful shorthand in `analytics-requirements` or `metrics-instrumentation-plan` if the cohort technique is adopted for retention-style metrics; not currently used anywhere in the repo's skills.
- **Lean Analytics Stages** (Empathy / Stickiness / Virality / Revenue / Scale) — not recommended as a glossary or skill concept at this time; see Caveats below for why this framework is a probable mismatch for this repo's actual product shape.

None of these rise to the level of the DDD/event-driven/testing/platform/security categories already in `CLAUDE.md`'s Ubiquitous Language — recommend leaving the core glossary untouched for this book; only `Vanity Metric`'s existing informal use is confirmed as already-correct.

## Caveats

- **The Lean Analytics stage framework is a plausible poor fit for this repo's actual product** (see Candidate New Skills/Agents above for why it isn't proposed as a skill) — judgment about product stage already belongs to `okr-authoring`'s existing Objective-setting process, and importing a second, competing framework for the same question would be redundant rather than additive.
- **OMTM's focus discipline is still worth keeping even though its team-coordination rationale is weaker here.** The underlying advice — don't track five metrics with equal weight, know which one currently matters most — remains valid at solo-operator scale even though the specific failure mode OMTM prevents (multiple people silently optimizing different numbers) is structurally less likely with one operator directing one agent per domain.
- **Cohort analysis assumes a metrics pipeline capable of segmenting by cohort start date and computing per-cohort time series** — a real instrumentation requirement, not just an analytical technique. If adopted, it belongs in `metrics-instrumentation-plan`'s computation logic, not only as a review-time check in `analytics-requirements`; this file does not assess whether the current pipeline architecture (per `data-pipeline-design`) already supports the query shape cohort analysis needs.
- **The book is a decade-plus old (2013) and written for a venture-funded startup audience** — its examples, tooling references, and implicit assumption of investor-facing "innovation accounting" reporting don't map onto this repo's frugal, self-funded, solo-operator context. Treat it as a source of metric-selection *discipline* (the four-part good-metric test, OMTM's focus principle, cohort analysis as validation) rather than of stage-progression or fundraising-narrative guidance.
- **This file's scope is metric selection and validation, not metric presentation** — it does not overlap with `research/data-and-analytics/storytelling-with-data-knaflic.md`'s territory (chart type, decluttering, exploratory-vs-explanatory visual design). A metric that passes every test in this file still needs Knaflic's discipline before it's shown to anyone.

# Measure What Matters: How Google, Bono, and the Gates Foundation Rock the World with OKRs
**Authors:** John Doerr (with supporting material from Christina Wodtke, *Radical Focus*, and Andy Grove, *High Output Management*, where noted)
**Domain:** Goal-Setting and Strategic Alignment — Objectives and Key Results (within Product Strategy and Go-to-Market)

## Core Concepts

- **OKRs come from Andy Grove's Intel, not from Google.** Doerr learned the method at Intel in the 1970s, where Grove ran it as a variant of management by objectives (MBO). Grove's *High Output Management* (1983) sets out its two questions: *where do I want to go?* (the Objective) and *how will I pace myself to see whether I am getting there?* (the Key Results). Grove's own claim is that the system's main value is **focus**: a few Objectives, chosen by saying no to everything else. Doerr brought the method to Google in 1999, and *Measure What Matters* (2018) is his account of it, built around case studies (Intel's Operation Crush, Google, YouTube, Chrome, the Gates Foundation, Bono's ONE campaign, Adobe and others). This matters for the repo because `okr-authoring/references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:3` cites "Google/Doerr" conventions with no research file behind them. This file is that source.
- **Objective versus Key Result.** An **Objective** is *what* is to be achieved. It is significant, concrete, action-oriented and, ideally, inspiring. A **Key Result** shows *how* you will know you got there. It is specific, time-bound, measurable and verifiable, and either it is met or it is not; nobody has to argue about it. Doerr gives the pairing as a sentence pattern, roughly "I will [Objective] as measured by [these Key Results]". He also says that an Objective without Key Results is just a wish, and that a Key Result with no number in it is not a Key Result. The skill's structure (`okr-authoring/SKILL.md:38-44`) and its Objective/KR tests (`references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:7-39`) already match this closely.
- **Doerr is less strict than this repo on outputs.** Doerr and Grove allow a Key Result to be a measurable **milestone**. As I recall, Intel's Operation Crush KRs included shippable things such as publishing benchmarks and producing sales material, alongside counts of customer design wins. The repo's rule that a KR is "never a shipped feature, launch, or roadmap item" (`SKILL.md:5-7`, `:50-62`) comes from **Cagan and Wodtke**, not from Doerr. That is a defensible choice for product teams, but the attribution should be honest: the outcome-only rule is Cagan's stance, and Doerr does not require it.
- **The four "superpowers".** Doerr organises the first half of the book around four benefits. (1) **Focus and commit to priorities.** Few Objectives (he suggests three to five per cycle), each with up to about five KRs, and leaders commit to them visibly. (2) **Align and connect for teamwork.** OKRs are public across the organisation, so anyone can see how their work connects to the top-level goals. (3) **Track for accountability.** Progress is checked regularly, not just at the end, and an OKR can be updated, changed or dropped mid-cycle if it stops making sense. (4) **Stretch for amazing.** Some goals are set beyond what seems achievable, because aiming high produces more than aiming safe.
- **Committed versus aspirational OKRs.** This is the most load-bearing idea missing from the skill (finding W1-24). Doerr reproduces Google's internal OKR guidance in the book. It divides OKRs into two types:
  - **Committed OKRs** are things the organisation agrees *will* get done. The expected grade is **1.0**. Anything less needs an explanation and a recovery plan, because other teams are relying on it.
  - **Aspirational (stretch, "moonshot") OKRs** describe a world the team would like to see without yet knowing how to get there. The expected average grade is around **0.6–0.7**, and a team that keeps scoring 1.0 on them was not stretching.
  - Google lists **not telling the two types apart** as a classic OKR mistake. If they are mixed, a committed promise gets graded like a stretch goal and treated as a success at 0.7, or a stretch goal gets graded like a promise and the team is punished for aiming high. The skill applies the 0.7 sweet spot to every KR (`SKILL.md:70-73`; `references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:87-97`). Its "binary KR" rule (`:99-101`) is really a committed KR without the name.
- **Grading on 0.0–1.0, red / yellow / green.** Google grades each KR from 0.0 to 1.0 at the end of the cycle. In the colour scheme Doerr describes, 0.0–0.3 is red (little progress), 0.4–0.6 is yellow (progress but short of the target) and 0.7–1.0 is green (delivered). The Objective's grade is the average of its KRs. Grading is meant to be quick and honest, and it is a way to learn, not a judgement on the people. The skill's bands (`references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:83-89`) match this. Its linear-interpolation formula (`:75-79`) is the repo's own refinement; Doerr presents grading as simpler and more judgement-based than a formula.
- **Cadence: quarterly OKRs inside an annual frame.** Doerr's default is **quarterly** OKRs for near-term work, with **annual** OKRs for longer goals. Shorter cycles let a team adapt; longer ones give room for big goals. He suggests the rhythm should fit the organisation and does not insist on a quarter. The skill has only the quarter (`SKILL.md:81`; `references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:123-129`). An annual layer is the natural place for the roadmap's Next/Later columns to point to.
- **Cascading versus alignment.** Doerr warns against **strict top-down cascading**, where every level copies and narrows the level above. It is slow, it turns each level into a translation exercise and it stops ideas coming from the front line. He favours **alignment**: top-level OKRs are public, teams and individuals write many of their own OKRs (he suggests roughly half should come from below; treat that figure as approximate) and link them to the top level. Teams also align *sideways* with the teams they depend on. The skill's heading and description say "cascade" (`SKILL.md:9`, `:82`), but its reference already describes alignment "by reference, not literal copy" (`references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:140`). The wording should follow the reference.
- **CFRs: conversations, feedback, recognition.** The second half of the book argues that OKRs need a matching way of managing people, which Doerr calls **CFRs**. *Conversations* are regular one-to-ones between manager and contributor about goals and progress. *Feedback* flows both ways and happens all the time, not once a year. *Recognition* means acknowledging contributions often and specifically. Doerr presents CFRs as a replacement for the annual performance review (Adobe's "check-in" is his main case). For this repo's solo operator, CFRs mostly collapse. What survives is the weekly check-in and the quarter-end retro.
- **Separate OKRs from compensation.** Doerr is firm that OKR grades should *not* drive bonuses or ratings. Once they do, people set safe targets and the stretch disappears. Performance conversations can mention OKRs as context, but the grade is not the pay formula. The skill already says this (`references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:119`). It only needs the source.
- **Health metrics and paired Key Results.** Grove's idea of **paired indicators** carries through Doerr: measure an effect *and* its counter-effect, so that pushing one number does not quietly damage another. For example, pair a volume KR with a quality KR, or pair speed with defect rate. Wodtke makes this explicit as **health metrics**. These are things you are not trying to improve this quarter but must not break while you chase the OKR, such as customer satisfaction, system stability or team wellbeing. The skill has a "Not gameable" test (`references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:39`) but no paired KR and no health-metric section in the template (`references/okr-template-and-examples.md:11-69`).
- **Common failure modes (Google's list, as Doerr reproduces it).** The book's guidance names several traps. The ones I recall with confidence are:
  - not separating committed from aspirational OKRs;
  - "business as usual" OKRs that describe work that would happen anyway;
  - timid aspirational OKRs;
  - sandbagging;
  - low-value Objectives that fail the "who cares?" test;
  - committed Objectives whose KRs, even if all met, would not deliver the Objective.

  Doerr's own failure stories add three more: too many OKRs, OKRs written once and never revisited, and OKRs treated as a to-do list of tasks. The skill covers sandbagging, too many OKRs, set-and-forget, outputs and vanity KRs (`SKILL.md:114-123`; `references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:145-157`). It misses business-as-usual, "who cares?", the failure to separate committed from aspirational, and insufficient KRs for a committed Objective.
- **Wodtke's *Radical Focus*: one OKR, and a weekly rhythm that keeps it alive.** Wodtke tells the method as a business fable about a struggling start-up and adds three things Doerr does not stress:
  - **One OKR.** A small company or team should have **a single Objective with about three KRs per quarter**. More than one splits the focus of a team that cannot afford it.
  - **Confidence set lower than you might expect.** She suggests setting each KR so the team's confidence of hitting it starts at about **5 out of 10**, a coin flip, and updating that score every week.
  - **A weekly rhythm.** On Monday there is a commitments meeting: priorities for the week, updated confidence in each KR, and a look ahead at the next few weeks. On Friday there is a "wins" session to celebrate what got done.

  She captures this in a one-page **four-square** status report. The four quadrants are: this week's priorities (P1/P2); the OKR with current confidence for each KR; a forecast of the next few weeks; and health metrics shown green, yellow or red. This is the concrete answer to the gap the findings name for the skill's cadence (`SKILL.md:81-83`): who runs the check-in, and where confidence is recorded.
- **What the books say about the order: vision → OKRs → roadmap.** All three support it, though none of them discusses a "product roadmap" in this plugin's sense.
  - **Doerr** says a mission or vision is the long-term direction, and OKRs are the concrete, time-bound way to move toward it. OKRs come *from* that direction; they are not derived from a plan of work.
  - **Wodtke's** chain is mission/strategy, then OKRs, then weekly priorities and tasks. Work is chosen *because* it moves a KR.
  - **Grove** treats the activities below an Objective as the means of hitting its KRs.

  Taken together, roadmap items are **initiatives**: bets on how to move a KR, sequenced over time, and free to change mid-cycle while the KR stays fixed. That matches Cagan (`inspired-cagan.md:16-17`) and `roadmap-authoring/SKILL.md:8`, `:80`, which already read the OKR set first. The skill that has the order backwards is `okr-authoring` (`SKILL.md:28`, `:89`, `:96`), along with the agent's sequence (`agents/product-strategist.md:163-164`, roadmap at 7, OKRs at 8). One nuance the books add is that the dependency is not purely one-way. If no plausible initiative could move a KR, the target is fantasy, so the roadmap step should be able to send an OKR back for revision. But that is a feedback check, not a reason for the OKRs to read the roadmap first.
- **The North Star belongs upstream of the OKRs.** None of the three books uses "North Star Metric" as a formal term. Doerr's closest idea is a long-lived top-level goal (YouTube's daily watch-time goal is his best-known example). That fits the repo's decision (W1-3) that the vision derives the North Star (`vision-statement/SKILL.md:65`, `:92`) and each OKR cycle *consumes* it. In practice, the cycle's Objectives should say which part of the North Star they move, rather than naming a new one (`okr-authoring/SKILL.md:95`, `:137`; template `:21`, `:26-29`).

## Actionable Techniques

- Tag every Objective **committed** or **aspirational** at authoring time. Grade committed KRs against 1.0, where a shortfall needs a written explanation and a recovery plan. Grade aspirational KRs against an expected average of about 0.7. Certifications, contractual deliverables and compliance deadlines (the SOC 2 example at `references/okr-template-and-examples.md:142`) are committed by default.
- Stop using "~70% confidence" and "~0.7 expected grade" as if they were the same thing. A KR you are 70% sure of hitting *in full* should usually grade well above 0.7. Google's 0.7 sweet spot and Wodtke's 5/10 starting confidence both describe a target set *beyond* the likely result. For aspirational KRs, start confidence near 50%. For committed KRs, start near 90% or above. The skill currently says 70% at `SKILL.md:83`, `:93`, `:136`, at `references/okr-rules-and-grading.md:17`, `:117`, and in the template at `:72`.
- Run a "business as usual?" test and a "who cares?" test on every Objective before running the existing health check. If the work would happen without the OKR, or nobody outside the team would notice it succeeding, it is not an Objective.
- Pair every volume or speed KR with a quality or counter-metric KR, or list the counter-metric as a health metric. Add a **Health metrics** block to the OKR template: two to four green/yellow/red signals that must not degrade while the OKR is pursued.
- Record the weekly check-in on one page (Wodtke's four-square): this week's priorities, KR confidence, the next few weeks, and health metrics. For a solo operator, keep it as a dated log appended to the OKR artifact. Each entry is one line per KR with confidence and a note, so the operator, or the product-strategist agent in a later session, can read the trend without a meeting.
- Size the set to the product's profile. **Lean:** one Objective, three KRs (Wodtke's one OKR, which also matches `phase-requirements.json`'s "lean: one objective"). **Standard:** up to three Objectives. **Enterprise:** three to five Objectives per level, aligned by reference with a cross-team dependency check, never strictly cascaded.
- Add an optional annual layer. One or two annual Objectives give the quarterly set its context and give the roadmap's Next/Later columns something to link to, other than the vision itself.
- Keep grading separate from any reward, and say so in the artifact. For a solo operator the risk is self-flattery, not gaming a bonus. Grade before rereading the narrative, and write the "what we learned" line before the grade is discussed.

If an enterprise-profile product ever needs team-operating artifacts, CFRs would belong in a people-process skill, not in OKR authoring.

## Key Terminology

- **Objective** — a significant, concrete, ideally inspiring statement of what is to be achieved in a cycle. Qualitative; no metric inside it.
- **Key Result** — a measurable, time-bound, verifiable marker of progress toward an Objective; either met or not, with a number.
- **Committed OKR** — an OKR the organisation agrees will be delivered. The expected grade is 1.0, and a shortfall must be explained.
- **Aspirational OKR** (stretch, moonshot) — an OKR set beyond the currently known path. The expected average grade is about 0.6–0.7.
- **Grading** — end-of-cycle scoring of each KR from 0.0 to 1.0, banded red (0.0–0.3) / yellow (0.4–0.6) / green (0.7–1.0); used to learn, not to judge people.
- **Alignment** (versus **cascading**) — linking a team's own OKRs to higher-level and neighbouring OKRs by reference, as opposed to copying and narrowing them from the top down.
- **CFRs** — Conversations, Feedback, Recognition: Doerr's continuous performance-management practice paired with OKRs.
- **Health metric** (Wodtke) / **paired indicator** (Grove) — a signal that must not degrade while the OKR is pursued, or a counter-metric that stops one KR being hit at another's expense.
- **Four-square** (Wodtke) — a one-page weekly status with four quadrants: this week's priorities, OKR confidence, the next few weeks, and health metrics.
- **Confidence** — a KR's running, weekly-updated likelihood of being hit (Wodtke uses 1–10). It is the leading indicator; the grade is the lagging one.
- **Initiative** — a bet on how to move a KR. It may change mid-cycle while the KR stays fixed. In this plugin, roadmap items are Initiatives.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** Grove and Intel as the origin, and Doerr's 1999 introduction at Google; the Objective/KR definitions and the "I will ... as measured by ..." pattern (paraphrased, not quoted); the four superpowers by name; committed versus aspirational, with 1.0 versus about 0.7; the 0.0–1.0 grading and its colour bands; CFRs; keeping OKRs separate from compensation; Doerr's preference for alignment over strict cascading; Wodtke's one-OKR focus, Monday commitments and Friday wins, the four-square and health metrics.
- **Less certain:** the exact list and wording of Google's "classic OKR mistakes" (I am sure of the themes, not the count or the labels); the "roughly half bottom-up" figure; the specific Operation Crush KRs (the point that Grove's KRs included milestone-type deliverables is what matters, not the details); whether Wodtke's 5/10 starting confidence applies to every KR or is mainly illustrated in the fable; which material belongs to *Radical Focus*'s first edition (2016) and which to its second (2021); and how much of Grove's paired-indicator discussion is about OKRs specifically rather than indicators in general. No page numbers, direct quotations or statistics are given here, deliberately.
- **The repo is stricter than Doerr on outputs, on purpose.** The outcome-only KR rule comes from Cagan (and Wodtke's spirit), not from *Measure What Matters*. A reader citing Doerr for "a KR is never a deliverable" would be misattributing it. The skill's prose should credit the outcome rule to `inspired-cagan.md` and the grading and cadence conventions to this file.
- **These are practitioner books, not empirical research.** Doerr is an investor and advocate. His evidence is case stories from companies he backed or advised, and he does not weigh the failures. Grove writes from running Intel. Wodtke writes from consulting and teaching. Treat their conventions (0.7, quarterly, three to five Objectives) as calibrated defaults, not as findings.
- **Scale mismatch.** All three books assume an organisation with managers and teams. For this plugin's lean, solo profile, most of the alignment and CFR machinery does not apply. What carries over is focus (one Objective), honest grading, the committed/aspirational split, health metrics and a lightweight, recorded weekly check-in.
- **Overlap with sibling files.** `inspired-cagan.md` covers OKRs as the hand-off to empowered teams and the anti-pattern of a feature roadmap. `product-management-in-practice-lemay.md` covers the hypothesis framing of a KR. `research/data-and-analytics/lean-analytics-croll-yoskovitz.md` covers metric quality tests and why the OMTM and the North Star differ. Wodtke's "one OKR" is close in spirit to the OMTM, but it is a quarterly commitment, not a rotating tactical metric, so the Lean Analytics distinction still holds. Rumelt's *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* (not yet in `research/`) would supply the missing "strategy" link between vision and OKRs that none of these three books defines rigorously.

# Obviously Awesome: How to Nail Product Positioning so Customers Get It, Buy It, Love It
**Authors:** April Dunford
**Domain:** Product Positioning & Go-to-Market Strategy

## Core Concepts

- **Positioning is invisible when done right, painfully obvious when done wrong.** Dunford's central thesis: when positioning is correct, customers instantly "get it" — the product feels obviously suited to them, and the work behind that feeling disappears. When positioning is wrong (or absent), everything downstream — sales conversations, marketing copy, pricing objections, even product roadmap debates — becomes harder, and the product looks like it's trying to be everything to everyone. She illustrates this with the retail-shelf analogy: a product placed on the wrong grocery-store aisle confuses shoppers no matter how good the product is, because context (the aisle) sets the customer's evaluation criteria before they ever read the label. This repo's `skills/gtm-strategy/SKILL.md` already warns against a symptom of bad positioning ("Positioning by adjective" — claims with no named alternative, no measurable dimension) but treats it as one anti-pattern among several rather than naming the underlying invisible/obvious mechanism that explains *why* vague positioning fails structurally.
- **Positioning is built from a small set of components, worked in a fixed sequence, not authored as a single sentence.** Dunford runs positioning as a structured, roughly ten-step workshop, built around six core components: (1) **competitive alternatives** — what the customer would do or use if your product did not exist; (2) **unique attributes** — the capabilities you have that those alternatives lack; (3) **value and themes** — the customer-facing benefit each unique attribute actually produces, grouped into 2-4 themes; (4) **target market characteristics** — the "best-fit customers" who care the most about that value; (5) **market category** — the frame of reference customers use to make sense of the offering; and (6) **relevant trends** — market or industry shifts that add urgency, used sparingly. The sequence matters: each component constrains the next (you cannot know your unique attributes until you know the true alternatives; you cannot pick a market category until you know which value themes and which customers matter most). This repo has no artifact that runs this sequence — see Applies To.
- **Competitive alternatives are broader than "competitors" — and must include doing nothing.** Dunford insists the first positioning question is not "who competes with us" but "what would the customer do if we didn't exist" — which is often a spreadsheet, a manual process, an internal hack, or simply tolerating the pain. Only once the true alternative set is named can you isolate what is genuinely unique. This maps closely onto — but is not identical to — `skills/competitive-analysis/SKILL.md`'s "Substitutes" row in its competitive-landscape taxonomy ("Hiring a Data Privacy Officer, engaging a compliance consultant"). The skill already captures the *concept*; Dunford's framework goes further by making "list competitive alternatives" the mandatory first step of a positioning exercise specifically, not an optional row in a broader competitor inventory that a team could skip.
- **Unique attributes only matter if they survive two filters: real differentiation and customer-perceived value.** An attribute earns a place in positioning only if (a) true alternatives genuinely lack it, and (b) the target customer actually cares about it — attributes that are real but unvalued, or valued but not actually differentiating, are cut. This is structurally identical to `skills/competitive-analysis/SKILL.md`'s Step 6 three-part test for differentiation points ("Valued," "Real," "Defensible"). Dunford's filter and this skill's filter converge almost exactly — this is the strongest existing coverage in the repo for any single Dunford component.
- **Market category is a deliberate strategic lever with three named options, not a label you fill in after the fact.** This is the book's most distinctive and least-replicated idea. A team can (1) **fit the product into an existing, well-understood category** — fastest path, borrows the customer's existing mental model and evaluation criteria, but you inherit that category's rules even where they don't favor you; (2) **create a brand-new category** — highest potential reward ("category king/queen" strategy) but requires heavy, sustained market-education investment before anyone will evaluate you on your own terms; or (3) **create a subcategory inside an existing category** — a hybrid ("the only X built for Y"), cheaper than inventing a category outright while still letting you set new evaluation criteria within a frame customers already trust. Dunford is explicit that this choice is made deliberately, tested against alternative framings, and revisited — not defaulted to whatever category the product happens to resemble.
- **Target market characteristics should be built from customers who already love the product, not from an aspirational persona.** Dunford's method for finding the target market starts by studying the segment of *existing* customers who are most enthusiastic and win-rate-favorable, then reverse-engineering what they have in common — company profile, trigger event, workflow — rather than starting from a top-down guess about who "should" want the product. This is a narrower, evidence-first move than `skills/gtm-strategy/SKILL.md`'s Ideal Customer Profile, which is built from company-profile attributes (size, industry, geography, technical maturity, regulatory exposure, buying triggers, disqualifiers) without a required step of anchoring any of those attributes in observed best-fit customers.
- **Trends are a garnish, not a foundation.** Relevant trends (a new regulation, a shift in buyer behavior, a technology wave) can be layered onto positioning to add urgency — but Dunford warns against positioning built primarily on a trend ("the AI-powered X for the era of Y"), because trend-led positioning ages quickly and collapses into generic category noise once the trend is no longer novel. None of the repo's strategy skills currently distinguish "trend-based urgency" as a separate, deliberately-limited component from the core value proposition.
- **Positioning is a cross-functional workshop output, tested against real sales and customer conversations, and re-run when context shifts.** The book frames positioning as a structured exercise done in a room with product, sales, marketing, and leadership together — not a document one person drafts — and insists it be validated against actual deal outcomes (win/loss reasons) and revisited whenever the competitive landscape, the product, or the target market changes materially. This mirrors `skills/business-model-canvas/SKILL.md`'s "Frozen canvas" anti-pattern ("re-validate at every launch stage transition") and `skills/gtm-strategy/SKILL.md`'s three-stage launch sequencing — the repo already has the *cadence* discipline for re-validating strategy artifacts; it has no positioning-specific artifact to apply that cadence to.

## Actionable Techniques

- Before `skills/competitive-analysis/SKILL.md`'s Step 2 ("List competitors across all four types"), insert an explicit, mandatory sub-step: "list what the customer would do if the product did not exist," scored equally against direct/indirect competitors rather than appearing as a single optional row (as in the current Worked Example's one "compliance consultant" line) — Dunford treats this as the load-bearing first step, not an afterthought.
- Before `skills/gtm-strategy/SKILL.md`'s Step 2 ("Positioning Statement"), insert a market-category decision point: for the product's candidate category (or 2-3 candidates), explicitly name the evaluation criteria that category imports (what a buyer expects a product "in this aisle" to do), and test whether the differentiation points already produced by `competitive-analysis`'s Step 6 still hold up under those imported criteria. Currently the skill's positioning-statement template has a `[category]` blank filled in without ever asking whether that category choice helps or hurts the stated differentiator.
- When building the ICP in `skills/gtm-strategy/SKILL.md` Section 1, require at least one ICP attribute per segment to be sourced from an actual best-fit existing customer (a Stage 1 design partner, per the skill's own Launch Sequencing) rather than allow the full ICP table to be populated from assumption alone.
- Add a "relevant trends" line to `skills/gtm-strategy/SKILL.md`'s Messaging Framework, explicitly capped at one supporting proof point (not a headline), to prevent trend language from crowding out the value-theme-based headline and value proposition.
- When more than one plausible market category exists for a product, produce a short category-comparison table (category option | evaluation criteria it imports | fit with current differentiation points | market-education cost) before locking the `[category]` value used downstream in both `vision-statement`'s Format 1 and `gtm-strategy`'s positioning statement — both currently consume "category" as a given input, never as a choice to be compared.
- Treat the positioning statement produced by a future positioning exercise as upstream of, not identical to, `vision-statement`'s Moore's Elevator Test format and `gtm-strategy`'s Positioning Statement format — both are compressions of positioning work into a single sentence; neither currently documents the multi-step derivation Dunford insists must precede that sentence.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **New skill: `positioning-canvas`** (phase: strategy, owner: product-strategist). This is a genuine structural gap, not a restatement of existing coverage. Both `competitive-analysis` and `gtm-strategy` currently treat positioning as a byproduct — a differentiation filter embedded in one skill, a fill-in-the-blank sentence template in the other — rather than as its own deliberate, sequenced exercise with a market-category decision as a first-class step. A dedicated `positioning-canvas` skill would: (1) run competitive-alternatives → unique-attributes → value-themes → best-fit-customer → market-category → relevant-trends in Dunford's order; (2) produce a Positioning Canvas artifact that sits, in the Strategy-phase sequence, after `competitive-analysis` (which supplies the alternatives and differentiation points) and before `gtm-strategy` (whose Positioning Statement section would then consume the canvas's output instead of being derived ad hoc); and (3) own the market-category decision (fit / create / subdivide) explicitly, feeding the chosen category into both `gtm-strategy`'s positioning statement and `vision-statement`'s Format 1 "product category" component, so the same category choice is not independently — and possibly inconsistently — guessed at in two places.
- No new agent is warranted. `product-strategist` already owns the Strategy phase and every skill this book touches; a `positioning-canvas` skill would simply extend that agent's existing skill list, the same way `competitive-analysis`, `business-model-canvas`, `vision-statement`, and `gtm-strategy` already do.

## Key Terminology

Candidates for `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language, none of which currently appear there or in the four skills read for this research:

- **Positioning** — the deliberate framing of a product against a specific market category and set of alternatives so that a target customer immediately understands its relevance and value. Distinct from a "positioning statement" (the compressed sentence), which the repo already has two near-duplicate templates for (`vision-statement` Format 1, `gtm-strategy` Positioning Statement) without ever naming the broader process those templates compress.
- **Market Category** — the frame of reference a customer uses to evaluate an offering (an existing category, a new category, or a subcategory). Currently used as an unexamined blank (`[category]`) in both `vision-statement` and `gtm-strategy`, never as a named strategic choice.
- **Competitive Alternative** — what a customer would do or use in the product's absence, including "do nothing" and manual/DIY workarounds; broader than "competitor" or "substitute" as currently scoped in `competitive-analysis`'s taxonomy.
- **Best-Fit Customer** — the subset of existing customers who most enthusiastically adopt and win with the product, used as the evidence base for defining the target market, rather than a top-down assumed ICP.
- **Category King/Queen** — the outcome sought when a team deliberately creates a new market category and successfully becomes the reference point other entrants are compared against; the high-risk, high-reward end of the market-category choice.

## Caveats

- **This is a workshop-based, qualitative method — it fits the repo's frugal, solo-operator constraint well.** Dunford's process requires no paid research tooling, no market-research vendor, and no data science; it is a structured conversation (or, for this repo, a structured agent-run exercise) using inputs the team already has. This aligns cleanly with the "Budget and Frugality" section of `CLAUDE.md`.
- **The framework assumes an existing product with real customers, or at least a working prototype and early adopters.** Her "best-fit customer" method explicitly starts from people who already use and love the product. This fits less naturally at the pure pre-launch stage `vision-statement` is used at (before any customer has touched the product) and fits best once `gtm-strategy`'s Stage 1 (closed beta / design partners) has produced real usage signal. A `positioning-canvas` skill should be sequenced to run once initial competitive-analysis exists and, ideally, be revisited once design-partner feedback exists — not treated as a one-time pre-launch artifact only.
- **Overlap with sibling files in this cluster — check them once written.** This research cluster (`research/product-strategy-and-gtm/`) is being populated in parallel with files on *Crossing the Chasm* (Moore), *Loved* (Lauchengco), and *The Lean Product Playbook* (Olsen), none of which existed at the time this file was written and none of which were read. Likely overlaps to reconcile once all four exist: Moore's technology-adoption-lifecycle and "bowling alley" niche-sequencing strategy intersects directly with Dunford's "create a new category" option (both address how a team wins a beachhead before broader market categories settle) — a future reader should check whether `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` proposes a category-adoption-stage skill that should be merged with, rather than duplicate, the `positioning-canvas` candidate proposed here. Olsen's Product-Market Fit Pyramid (target market → underserved needs → value proposition → feature set → UX) sits conceptually *upstream* of Dunford's positioning work (which assumes a value proposition and product already exist) — the two should not be collapsed into one skill; Dunford refines how an existing value proposition is framed and communicated, Olsen validates that the value proposition is right in the first place. Lauchengco's *Loved* is product-management-craft-focused (how PMs work day to day) and may not overlap with either — but should still be checked before finalizing the `positioning-canvas` skill proposal, in case it already covers positioning from a PM-workflow angle that would change where in the phase sequence the new skill should sit.
- **Dunford's book is B2B/B2C SaaS-oriented and written from a startup go-to-market consultant's practice, not an academic framework.** Its evidence base is Dunford's own consulting engagements and case studies (recounted anecdotally, not cited to independent research), which is consistent with how this repo already treats `gtm-strategy` and `business-model-canvas` (both practitioner frameworks, not peer-reviewed research) — no special caution beyond the repo's existing norm for treating practitioner books as actionable-but-not-empirically-proven guidance.

# Business Model Generation and Value Proposition Design
**Authors:** Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur (*Business Model Generation*, 2010, co-created with a large community of practitioners); Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, Gregory Bernarda and Alan Smith (*Value Proposition Design*, 2014)
**Domain:** Business Model Design and Value Proposition Design (within Product Strategy and Go-to-Market)

## Core Concepts

- **A business model describes how an organization creates, delivers and captures value, and it fits on one page as nine building blocks.** *Business Model Generation* (BMG) defines the Business Model Canvas as a shared language for describing, visualizing, assessing and changing business models. The nine blocks are **Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships and Cost Structure**. The book's core claim is that the canvas is a *thinking and conversation tool*. It is used on a large printed poster with sticky notes, by a group, so that ideas can be moved, removed and recombined. It is not a form to be completed once. This repo's `skills/business-model-canvas/SKILL.md:27-31` states the "fit between the blocks" idea correctly. It does not carry the "movable sticky notes, many drafts" working style that the book treats as essential.

- **The canvas has a right side (value) and a left side (efficiency), with a financial rail at the bottom.** The right-hand blocks (Segments, Relationships, Channels) plus the central Value Proposition describe value creation for the customer. The left-hand blocks (Activities, Resources, Partnerships) describe the infrastructure that produces it. Revenue Streams and Cost Structure close the loop. The book likens the two sides to emotion versus logic. The skill covers this split (`SKILL.md:64-66`, `references/canvas-blocks.md:15-24`).

- **Each block has a typology in the book, and these typologies are much of its practical value.** As I recall them:
  - **Customer Segments:** five segment types: *mass market*, *niche market*, *segmented* (slightly different needs), *diversified* (unrelated segments) and *multi-sided platforms* (two or more interdependent segments, all needed for the model to work).
  - **Value Propositions:** a list of value elements: *newness, performance, customization, "getting the job done", design, brand/status, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, accessibility, convenience/usability*. These can be quantitative (price, speed) or qualitative (design, experience).
  - **Channels:** five channel phases (*awareness, evaluation, purchase, delivery, after sales*). Channels can be *own vs partner* and *direct vs indirect*. The trade-off is that own channels give higher margins and more control, while partner channels give reach at lower margins.
  - **Customer Relationships:** the motivations are *customer acquisition, customer retention and boosting sales (upselling)*. The types are *personal assistance, dedicated personal assistance, self-service, automated services, communities and co-creation*.
  - **Revenue Streams:** a split between *transaction revenue* (one-time) and *recurring revenue*. The ways to generate revenue are *asset sale, usage fee, subscription fees, lending/renting/leasing, licensing, brokerage fees and advertising*. Pricing mechanisms are either **fixed** (list price, product-feature dependent, customer-segment dependent, volume dependent) or **dynamic** (negotiation, yield management, real-time market/auction).
  - **Key Resources:** *physical, intellectual, human, financial*.
  - **Key Activities:** *production, problem solving, platform/network*.
  - **Key Partnerships:** four partnership types: *strategic alliances between non-competitors, coopetition (strategic partnerships between competitors), joint ventures, and buyer-supplier relationships*. Three motivations: *optimization and economy of scale; reduction of risk and uncertainty; acquisition of particular resources and activities*.
  - **Cost Structure:** a *cost-driven* to *value-driven* spectrum. Cost characteristics are *fixed costs, variable costs, economies of scale and economies of scope*.

  The skill reproduces the resources, activities and partnership-motivation typologies faithfully, and the relationship types partially. It omits the segment types, the value-element list, the own/partner channel trade-off, the relationship motivations, the revenue-type list, the fixed/dynamic pricing split, the partnership types and economies of scale/scope (see Applies To).

- **Business model patterns are recurring arrangements of the blocks, and naming one tells a team which blocks must interlock.** BMG describes five:
  1. **Unbundling.** Drawing on Hagel and Singer, a firm holds three fundamentally different businesses: *customer relationship*, *product innovation* and *infrastructure*. Each has different economic, competitive and cultural imperatives, so they are often better separated. The book's examples include private banking and mobile telecoms outsourcing their networks.
  2. **The Long Tail.** Selling many niche products in small volumes each, made possible by low inventory cost and strong platforms. Often paired with user-generated content.
  3. **Multi-Sided Platforms.** Bringing together two or more distinct but interdependent customer groups. The platform creates value by facilitating interaction, and one side is frequently subsidized to attract the other. Examples are credit cards, search engines and game consoles.
  4. **Free as a business model.** At least one substantial segment continuously benefits from a free offer, financed by another part of the model. There are three sub-patterns: *advertising-based* (a multi-sided platform), *freemium* (a free basic tier with a small share of paying premium users, so cost-to-serve the free base is the key number) and *bait and hook* (a cheap or free initial product with recurring follow-up purchases, such as razors and blades or printers and ink).
  5. **Open business models.** Creating and capturing value by collaborating with outside partners. This is *outside-in* (bringing in external ideas or IP) or *inside-out* (licensing out unused internal IP or assets), building on Chesbrough's open innovation.

  The skill names none of these patterns. Each one has a specific coherence signature that a generic seven-question check does not catch. For example, freemium lives or dies on cost-to-serve the free users versus the conversion rate, and a multi-sided platform needs a value proposition, channel and revenue stream *per side*.

- **Design techniques are what make the canvas generative rather than descriptive.** BMG gives six:
  1. **Customer insights.** Design from the customer's perspective, notably with the **Empathy Map**: what the customer *thinks and feels, hears, sees, says and does*, plus their *pains* and *gains*. The book cautions against listening only to existing customers, because new models often come from non-customers or new segments.
  2. **Ideation.** Generate many options before choosing. The book names innovation **epicenters**, the block a new model starts from: *resource-driven, offer-driven, customer-driven, finance-driven, or multiple-epicenter*. It also uses **"what if" questions** to break assumptions, and a divergent-then-convergent process (team composition, immersion, expanding, criteria setting, prototyping).
  3. **Visual thinking.** Sticky notes and drawings on the canvas, used to make the model tangible and to tell its story.
  4. **Prototyping.** Treat each canvas as a prototype at different fidelities, from a napkin sketch to an elaborated canvas, a business case and a field-tested model. Explore many before committing.
  5. **Storytelling.** Introduce a new model through a story, from the company's or the customer's perspective, to overcome resistance to the unfamiliar.
  6. **Scenarios.** Make the design concrete by describing customer-usage situations and future environments, then design models that would work in each.

  The skill has none of these techniques. Its procedure (`SKILL.md:137-149`) goes straight from choosing a variant to filling blocks. There is no step that generates more than one model.

- **Strategy around the canvas covers the environment, evaluation and managing several models.** BMG places the model inside a **business model environment** with four areas: *market forces, industry forces, key trends, macro-economic forces*. It evaluates a model with a **SWOT run block by block**, asking structured questions about each block's strengths and weaknesses. It combines the canvas with **Blue Ocean Strategy's** Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create grid: the value-proposition change is tested against its effect on the cost side and the customer side together. It also discusses **managing multiple business models** in one organization, such as a disruptive model kept separate from the core. The book's overall process has five phases: **Mobilize, Understand, Design, Implement, Manage**. The skill has a "re-validate at each launch stage" rule (`SKILL.md:147-149`). It has no environment scan and no structured strengths/weaknesses assessment per block.

- **The Value Proposition Canvas zooms into two blocks, Customer Segment and Value Proposition, and the companion book *Value Proposition Design* (VPD) is about getting their fit right.**
  - **Customer Profile** (the circle, the right side):
    - **Customer Jobs:** *functional*, *social* and *emotional* jobs. Also *supporting jobs*, in the roles of *buyer of value*, *co-creator of value* and *transferrer of value*. The book notes that job importance varies with context.
    - **Pains:** *undesired outcomes/problems/characteristics*, *obstacles* (things that prevent or slow getting a job done) and *risks* (things that could go wrong). Ranked by *severity*, from extreme to moderate.
    - **Gains:** *required, expected, desired and unexpected* gains. Ranked by *relevance*, from essential to nice-to-have.

    VPD insists the profile is sketched from the customer's view and then *verified with evidence*. It is not to be filled in from the team's assumptions about what the product does.
  - **Value Map** (the square, the left side):
    - **Products and Services:** what the proposition is built around.
    - **Pain Relievers:** how those products and services relieve specific, ranked pains.
    - **Gain Creators:** how they produce specific, ranked gains.

    A value proposition should not address every pain and gain. It should address the few that matter most, and do it extremely well.
  - **Fit:** achieved when pain relievers and gain creators map to the profile's most important jobs, pains and gains. VPD separates three successive kinds of fit:
    1. **Problem-solution fit**, on paper: evidence that customers care about these jobs, pains and gains.
    2. **Product-market fit**, in the market: evidence that customers actually want the value proposition.
    3. **Business model fit**, in the bank: evidence that the proposition can be embedded in a profitable, scalable business model.
  - **Multiple customers in B2B.** VPD notes that a value proposition may need a separate profile for each stakeholder: influencers, recommenders, economic buyers, decision makers, end users and even saboteurs. I recall the list but not its exact wording.

  The skill gestures at this ("pain relieved + gain created", `SKILL.md:85-86`; `canvas-blocks.md:61-63`). It does not decompose the customer into jobs, pains and gains. It does not rank them. It does not show the mapping from pain relievers and gain creators to specific pains and gains, which is how fit is demonstrated. W1 already flags this (`wave-1-findings.md`, business-model-canvas § 2).

- **VPD makes testing the centre of design: every canvas is a set of hypotheses, and evidence moves them.** VPD's later chapters (and, more fully, the follow-on book *Testing Business Ideas* by David Bland and Osterwalder, 2019, which is not covered here) teach the following:
  - Extract the hypotheses underlying a canvas.
  - Prioritize them by importance and lack of evidence, testing first the ones that would kill the idea.
  - Design small experiments, recorded on a **Test Card** (*we believe… / to verify that, we will… / and measure… / we are right if…*).
  - Capture results on a **Learning Card** (*we believed… / we observed… / from that we learned… / therefore we will…*).

  Evidence varies in strength. What people *say* in interviews is weaker than what they *do*, such as clicking a call to action, paying a deposit or pre-ordering. Experiments progress from cheap, fast and weak toward costlier and stronger. VPD also shows a progress board for tracking which hypotheses are tested. This is the mechanism behind W1-32 (per-block evidence status). The skill marks the Lean Canvas Unfair Advantage as allowed to be blank (`canvas-blocks.md:267`) but has no general hypothesis/tested/validated state for any block.

- **The value proposition can be stated in a fixed sentence shape ("ad-lib").** VPD offers a fill-in template, roughly: *Our [products and services] help [customer segment] who want to [jobs to be done] by [verb] [customer pain] and [verb] [customer gain], unlike [competing value proposition].* I am confident of the structure but not certain of the exact wording. It overlaps closely with `gtm-strategy`'s Moore-style positioning statement (`gtm-strategy/SKILL.md:84`).

## Actionable Techniques

- **Build a Value Proposition Canvas before writing Block 2.** For each segment:
  1. List customer jobs (functional, social, emotional, supporting).
  2. List pains (undesired outcomes, obstacles, risks), ranked by severity.
  3. List gains (required, expected, desired, unexpected), ranked by relevance.
  4. Write the value map: products and services, then pain relievers and gain creators.
  5. Draw an explicit line from each reliever or creator to the pain or gain it addresses.

  Only the top-ranked pains and gains that the map addresses go into the BMC's Value Propositions block. Any pain reliever that points at no ranked pain is cut.
- **Classify the segment type first** (mass, niche, segmented, diversified, multi-sided). If it is multi-sided, fill Value Propositions, Channels, Relationships and Revenue *per side*, and name which side is subsidized.
- **Name the pattern if one applies** (unbundled, long tail, multi-sided, free/freemium/bait-and-hook, open), then run that pattern's extra checks:
  - *Freemium:* cost-to-serve a free user, expected conversion rate, and what makes the premium tier worth paying for.
  - *Multi-sided:* which side is subsidized, and the chicken-and-egg launch plan.
  - *Unbundled:* which of the three business types (customer relationship, product innovation, infrastructure) this firm actually is.
- **Pick an epicenter and generate at least two or three alternative canvases** (resource-, offer-, customer- or finance-driven) before choosing one. Use "what if" questions to challenge each block's default. Keep rejected canvases as recorded alternatives.
- **Run the Empathy Map for the primary segment's buyer and user** (thinks and feels, hears, sees, says and does, pains, gains) when no interview evidence exists yet. Label it as an assumption, consistent with `user-persona`'s honesty rule.
- **Tag every block entry with an evidence state** (hypothesis / tested / validated) and the cheapest test that would move it. Use a Test Card for the riskiest two or three. Prefer behavioural evidence (sign-up, deposit, letter of intent) over interview statements.
- **Assess the finished canvas block by block**: one strength and one weakness per block (BMG's structured SWOT), plus an environment scan (market forces, industry forces, key trends, macro-economic forces) for the two or three forces most likely to break the model.
- **Use the fixed/dynamic pricing typology** when filling Revenue Streams: name the revenue type (subscription, usage, licensing…) *and* the pricing mechanism (fixed: list, feature-, segment- or volume-dependent; dynamic: negotiation, yield, auction).
- **Use cost characteristics**: state where economies of scale or scope apply. This is often the honest answer to "why does margin improve as we grow?"

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **No new skill is clearly warranted, though one is a borderline case.** The Value Proposition Canvas could be a standalone `value-proposition-canvas` skill: it has its own elicitation, its own artifact and its own fit criteria. Against that, it zooms into two blocks of a canvas this skill already owns. It also overlaps heavily with `jtbd-analysis` (jobs) and `user-persona` (pains). A separate skill would create a three-way restatement of jobs, pains and gains. The recommended path is a `references/value-proposition-canvas.md` plus a VPC section in the canvas template. Revisit if the VPC starts to be produced independently of a BMC (for example, by a feature-level discovery skill).
- **The testing material (hypotheses, Test Cards, Learning Cards, evidence strength) is cross-cutting.** It applies to `business-model-canvas`, `beta-program-design`, `jtbd-analysis` and `impact-mapping` alike. It is a candidate for a shared reference or a small `assumption-testing` skill rather than living only in this one. It overlaps with Blank's Customer Development, Cagan's prototype and concierge techniques and Fitzpatrick's interview rules, already in this cluster. A research file on *Testing Business Ideas* (Bland and Osterwalder) would be the proper grounding; it is not in `research/` and this file does not cover it in depth.
- **No new agent.** Everything here is knowledge that `product-strategist` applies.

## Key Terminology

Candidates for `skills/glossary-management/`. These are distinct from the existing DDD terms; note that **Value Proposition** here is a strategy term, not a domain-model term.

- **Business Model Canvas**: the nine-block, one-page description of how an organization creates, delivers and captures value.
- **Value Proposition Canvas**: the two-part zoom on one Customer Segment (Customer Profile) and its Value Proposition (Value Map).
- **Customer Profile**: Customer Jobs, Pains and Gains for one segment, ranked.
- **Value Map**: Products and Services, Pain Relievers and Gain Creators.
- **Pain Reliever / Gain Creator**: a specific way the offer reduces a named pain or produces a named gain. Not a feature.
- **Fit (Problem-Solution, Product-Market, Business Model)**: three successive levels of evidence that the value proposition matters, sells and makes money.
- **Business Model Pattern**: a recurring arrangement of blocks (Unbundling, Long Tail, Multi-Sided Platform, Free/Freemium/Bait and Hook, Open).
- **Multi-Sided Platform**: a model serving two or more interdependent segments, where the value comes from their interaction.
- **Epicenter**: the block from which a new business model design starts (resource, offer, customer, finance, multiple).
- **Empathy Map**: a quick customer-insight sketch (thinks and feels, hears, sees, says and does, pains, gains).
- **Test Card / Learning Card**: a pre-registered experiment and its recorded outcome.

## Caveats

- **High confidence.** I am confident of: the nine blocks and the left/right split; the value/efficiency framing; the five patterns and the three free sub-patterns; the six design techniques; the Empathy Map; the Value Proposition Canvas structure (jobs/pains/gains; products and services, pain relievers, gain creators); the job types (functional/social/emotional plus supporting); the gain types (required/expected/desired/unexpected); the three kinds of fit; and the hypothesis → experiment → evidence emphasis in VPD. All of these are the books' most repeated content and Strategyzer's public material.
- **Medium confidence.** These lists are from memory and may differ slightly in wording or membership from the printed text: the per-block typologies (value elements, relationship types and motivations, revenue types, fixed/dynamic pricing mechanisms, partnership types, cost characteristics, segment types); the epicenter names; the five-phase process (Mobilize, Understand, Design, Implement, Manage); the environment map's four areas; the B2B stakeholder roles; and the exact wording of the Test Card, Learning Card and ad-lib templates. Check them against the books before quoting any of them as a closed list in a skill.
- **Not covered or uncertain.** I give no page numbers, chapter numbers or direct quotations, because I cannot reproduce them reliably. The case studies in each pattern chapter are recalled only as examples and should not be cited as evidence. *Testing Business Ideas* (2019), *The Invincible Company* (2020) and the Strategyzer "Business Model Environment" and other tools are separate works; I mention them only where they extend VPD.
- **Lean Canvas is Maurya's, not Osterwalder's.** The four-block substitution in `canvas-blocks.md:262-267` is grounded in *Running Lean*, which still has no research file (W1 coverage). Positionally, the Lean Canvas places Problem where Key Partners sits, Solution and Key Metrics where Key Activities and Key Resources sit, and Unfair Advantage where Customer Relationships sits. That matches the skill. Maurya's own write-ups describe the Activities/Resources swap somewhat differently, and I am not certain which pairing he states. A *Running Lean* research file should settle this.
- **Practitioner frameworks.** Both books are design-practice books, not empirical research. BMG grew from Osterwalder's PhD work on a business-model ontology, but the canvas's effectiveness is asserted through cases, not measured. Treat them, as this cluster treats Dunford and Cagan, as actionable but not proven.
- **Fit with this repo's context.** The workshop format (a large poster, a team with sticky notes) assumes a group. Here a single agent drafts for one reviewer, so "visual thinking" becomes a structured, multi-draft artifact rather than a wall session. Generating alternatives and recording evidence state are what carry over directly. The lean scale profile skips this artifact (`phase-requirements.json`, business-model-canvas entry), so any VPC addition must stay optional at that profile.

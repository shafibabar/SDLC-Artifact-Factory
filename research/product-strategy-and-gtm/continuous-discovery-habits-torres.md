# Continuous Discovery Habits: Discover Products that Create Customer Value and Business Value
**Authors:** Teresa Torres (2021)
**Domain:** Product discovery practice: outcomes, interviewing cadence, opportunity mapping, assumption testing

## Core Concepts

- **Continuous discovery is a cadence, not a phase.** Torres defines it, in substance, as weekly touchpoints with customers, by the team building the product, where they run small research activities in pursuit of a desired outcome. Each part of that definition carries weight. *Weekly* means discovery never stops for delivery; it is a habit that keeps running, not a project with an end date. *The team building the product* means the people who decide also hear the customer directly, with no researcher handing over a report. *Small research activities* means one interview, one quick test, one look at data, not a quarterly study. *In pursuit of a desired outcome* means the research has a direction; it is not open-ended curiosity. This is the book's sharpest difference from the plugin's model. The plugin runs eight gated phases, and discovery happens once, in Ideate (`jtbd-analysis`, `impact-mapping`), and once more after Deploy, in Customer Validation (`feedback-template`, `beta-program-design`). Torres would say the two should be one loop that never closes.
- **The product trio owns discovery together.** The product manager, the designer and a tech lead make discovery decisions as a group, so value, usability and feasibility are weighed at the same time. `inspired-cagan.md:8` already covers the trio and why this repo cannot staff one literally. Torres adds the working practice: the trio interviews together, synthesises together (the interview snapshot, below) and builds the opportunity solution tree together. In this plugin the "trio" is Shafi plus agents that are reset between phases. The nearest practical version: Shafi conducts the conversation, `requirements-analyst` holds the customer view and the tree, and an engineering agent is asked for feasibility notes only when assumptions are tested. That is an adaptation, not Torres's practice, and should be named as one.
- **Start from a desired outcome, and tell the three kinds of outcome apart.** A **business outcome** measures the business's progress (revenue, retention, cost). It lags and is shaped by many teams. A **product outcome** measures a change in what customers do in the product that the team believes drives the business outcome. A team can move it directly, and it responds quickly to what the team ships. A **traction metric** measures use of one specific feature. It is close to an output, and assigning one to a team decides the solution for them. Torres recommends giving a team a product outcome. The outcome is negotiated between leader and team, not handed down. When the right metric is not yet known, the first goal can be a *learning* goal (find the metric that matters) before it becomes a *performance* goal (move it by X). The repo already insists on outcome over output (`impact-mapping/SKILL.md:30-35`). What it lacks is the three-way split, so it cannot catch a Key Result that is really a business outcome (too far away to steer by) or really a traction metric (a feature in disguise).
- **The opportunity solution tree (OST) is the book's central artifact.** It is a visual tree. The **desired outcome** sits at the root. Below it is the **opportunity space**: customer needs, pain points and desires found in research, written from the customer's point of view. Below each opportunity are the **solutions** that might address it, and below each solution the **assumption tests** that check it. Three rules make it work. (1) An opportunity is not a solution in disguise. "I wish I had a dashboard" is a solution, and the opportunity is whatever the dashboard is for. The test: could more than one solution address it? (2) Opportunities nest. Big opportunities break down into smaller, more solvable child opportunities. A useful top level is the steps of the customer's experience, taken from an **experience map** the team draws of what it currently knows. (3) The tree makes the team's thinking visible. It shows *why* this solution, for *this* opportunity, toward *this* outcome, and that makes it a stakeholder communication tool as well as a planning tool. The plugin has no artifact that holds opportunities. Job stories (`jtbd-analysis`) and impact-map behaviour changes (`impact-mapping`) are both close, but neither keeps a structured, growing set of customer needs that new evidence can be added to.
- **Story-based interviewing: collect specific stories, not opinions.** Torres's interview asks for the story of a specific past instance: "tell me about the last time you…". The scope of the prompt sets which part of the experience you hear about. A broad prompt gives the whole journey, a narrow one gives one moment, and the scope is chosen to match the outcome being pursued. The interviewer then *excavates* the story (what happened first, then what, who else was involved, what was hard) and gently redirects generalisations ("I usually…") back to the specific instance. Interviews are for discovering **opportunities**, not for testing solutions. Showing a concept in an interview turns it back into an opinion poll. This agrees with the Mom Test (`the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md:7-11`, already taught in `jtbd-interviewing.md:18-76`) and with the switch interview (`competing-against-luck-christensen.md:31`). What Torres adds is not new question rules. It is the *purpose* (opportunities for a named outcome), the *scoping* of the story prompt, and the synthesis and cadence around the interview.
- **The interview snapshot is the unit of synthesis.** After each interview, the trio fills in a one-page snapshot together, while memory is fresh. As I recall its fields: who the participant was, a few quick facts that set context, a memorable quote, a sketch of the story as an experience map, the opportunities heard, and insights that don't yet fit anywhere. Snapshots are what stop weekly interviewing from piling up as unread notes. They are the input to **opportunity mapping**: each new snapshot's opportunities are added to the tree, merged with ones already there, and the tree is restructured as patterns appear. This is the missing middle step in the repo. `feedback-capture-and-triage.md:13-40` captures *items*, one complaint or request at a time, and `jtbd-analysis` goes straight from "gather input" (`SKILL.md:129`) to written job stories. Nothing in between turns one conversation into structured, comparable evidence about needs.
- **Automate recruiting, or the weekly habit will not last.** Torres's practical claim: teams stop interviewing because recruiting is a chore, not because they stop caring. The fix is a standing pipeline that puts an interview on the calendar every week with no extra effort. Examples are an in-product or on-site prompt asking customers if they will talk for a short session, a scheduling link, referrals from customer-facing colleagues (support, sales, account management), a customer advisory panel, and an incentive where it suits. This is the part that matters most to a solo operator. Shafi cannot keep a weekly touchpoint going by hand alongside everything else. A recruiting pipeline set up once (a sign-up prompt in the product, design partners who have agreed to a standing slot) is what makes "continuous" realistic. `beta-program-design/SKILL.md:109-117` sets a *bi-weekly* structured check-in for the closed-beta cohort only, and nothing keeps discovery going outside a beta.
- **Prioritise opportunities by comparing them, one level of the tree at a time.** Torres warns against "whether or not" decisions (should we build X, yes or no) and argues for "compare and contrast" decisions (which of these is best). For opportunities, compare siblings under the same parent, choose one, and only then go a level deeper. The final choice is a **target opportunity**. The comparison is qualitative and uses a few lenses: **opportunity sizing** (how many customers it affects, how often), **market factors** (is it table stakes, does it set us apart), **company factors** (strategy, strengths), and **customer factors** (how important it is to the customer, how badly it is served today). She treats these choices as reversible "two-way door" decisions that should not be over-analysed, because the tree will be revisited. At small n this is a better fit than a numeric Opportunity Score (`job-statements-and-dimensions.md:150-187`), whose scaling caveat (`:180-187`) and the Christensen file (`competing-against-luck-christensen.md:106`) both admit it does not hold up with 3–5 design partners.
- **Generate several solutions for one target opportunity, and carry a few forward together.** The first idea is rarely the best, and a team with one idea falls in love with it. Torres's practice: generate many solutions for the target opportunity. She cites research suggesting people produce better ideas working alone before pooling them than in a group brainstorm. Then narrow to a small set, around three, and carry those forward together, so every test is a comparison and not a vote on a favourite. This independently supports W1-41 (`impact-mapping/SKILL.md:58-66` has no candidate-spread step) from a discovery source, not only from the AI-creativity source (`unleash-creativity-with-ai-bier.md:19`).
- **Test assumptions, not whole ideas.** Every solution rests on assumptions, and the cheapest learning comes from testing the riskiest assumption, not building the idea. Torres surfaces assumptions by story-mapping each solution (the steps a user would take through it, and what must be true at each), by pre-mortems, and by walking the tree's lines (why would this solution address this opportunity, and why would that move the outcome). She sorts assumptions into five types: **desirability** (do they want it), **viability** (should the business do it), **feasibility** (can it be built), **usability** (can they use it) and **ethical** (could it do harm, and should it be done). Assumptions are then mapped on two axes, how important the assumption is to the idea's success and how much evidence already supports it, an approach she credits to David Bland. The important, low-evidence ones are tested first. Each test is small and fast, and its **success criteria are written before it runs** ("at least N of M participants do X"), so a result cannot be reinterpreted after the fact. Testing is comparative across the solution set, and tests start small and only get bigger as confidence grows. The four-risk annotation already in `impact-map-levels.md:191-195` is Cagan's set. Torres adds the **ethical** category, which matters for a data-estate and compliance product, plus the prioritisation axes and the pre-set success criteria.
- **Measure impact, and close the loop back to the tree.** Shipping is not the end. The team checks whether the shipped solution actually moved the product outcome, and that evidence changes the tree: an opportunity is closed, a solution is pruned, or the team learns the opportunity-to-outcome link was wrong. Over time, teams move from measuring activity (it shipped, it is used) to measuring impact (it moved the outcome). This is the conceptual bridge between the plugin's Customer Validation phase and its Ideate artifacts. Today `feedback-template` routes items to agents (`feedback-capture-and-triage.md:75-88`) but has no path back to the opportunity space that the next cycle's discovery would draw on.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Name the interviewer and the synthesiser separately.** Shafi (or a design-partner session he runs) holds the conversation. The agent writes the story-based interview guide beforehand, and afterwards turns Shafi's raw notes into an interview snapshot. This settles W1-23 with a positive rule ("the human interviews, the agent synthesises"), where the current fix only says what the agent may not do. A snapshot made from notes the agent did not witness is marked as such.
- **Write the story prompt from the outcome.** Each interview guide opens with one "tell me about the last time you…" prompt, scoped to the part of the experience the current outcome touches. The guide then lists excavation probes (what happened next, who else, what was hardest) and redirects for generalisations. This goes before, and does not replace, the Mom-Test question bank at `jtbd-interviewing.md:150-166`.
- **One snapshot per conversation, filed by date.** Add a `templates/interview-snapshot.md` (participant, context facts, memorable quote, the story as an experience-map sketch, opportunities heard, unplaced insights, source = which conversation, interviewer, date). Snapshots are evidence, not approved artifacts. Store them the way feedback is stored, one file per date (`phase-requirements.json:794-796` already uses `customer-validation/feedback/*.md`), so the approve-and-reopen cycle does not fire every week.
- **Phrase every "underlying need" as an opportunity.** In `feedback-template`, fill the Underlying need field (`feedback-capture-and-triage.md:32-33`) in the customer's voice, at a level where more than one solution could address it. Tag it with the moment in the experience it belongs to. That makes each feedback item ready to place on the tree with no rewording.
- **Aggregate by opportunity, not only by symptom.** Add a second rollup to the feedback log's Aggregation Summary (`feedback-capture-and-triage.md:184-186`): opportunities heard, how many independent stories mention each, and which parent opportunity each sits under. Symptom-level dedupe still decides defect handling, and the opportunity rollup feeds the next discovery cycle.
- **Classify the Key Result before mapping it.** Before an impact map or tree is built, label the linked KR as a business outcome, a product outcome or a traction metric. Build the map from a product outcome. If the KR is a business outcome, ask `okr-authoring`'s owner which product outcome the team believes drives it. If it is a traction metric, treat it as a solution already chosen, and challenge it.
- **Compare three, test assumptions, set the bar first.** For a target opportunity (or an impact-map impact), keep three candidate solutions. List each one's assumptions under the five types, rank them by importance against evidence, and write a test with a numeric success threshold for the top one or two before any result exists. Record the result against the threshold, not a judgement made afterwards.
- **Keep a weekly touchpoint even when there is nothing to "validate".** For the solo operator: one conversation a week, recruited through a standing pipeline (an in-product sign-up prompt, design partners with a standing slot, introductions from current customers). Recruiting is a setup task done once, and it belongs on the beta and launch plans, not left to effort.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **New skill: `opportunity-solution-tree`** (phase: ideate, owner: requirements-analyst, domain: discovery). This is a real structural gap. No current skill holds a growing, structured set of customer opportunities under an outcome, with solutions and assumption tests hanging off them. Job stories describe jobs. Impact maps describe actors and behaviour changes. Neither is a place to add next week's evidence. The skill would own: the outcome check (product outcome, not business or traction), the experience-map top level, opportunity-framing rules (customer voice, more than one possible solution, parent/child nesting), compare-and-contrast prioritisation to a target opportunity, three-solution comparison, and the assumption list with the five types. It would consume interview snapshots and the feedback log's opportunity rollup, and feed `impact-mapping` (opportunities between HOW and WHAT) and `epic-definition`. Scale: **lean**, no tree artifact; one ranked opportunity list kept inside the persona or feedback log (`phase-requirements.json` skips `jtbd-analysis` and `impact-map` at lean). **Standard**, one tree for the one product outcome in play. **Enterprise**, one tree per product outcome, with opportunities tagged by segment or tenant role so that one segment's need is not averaged into another's. Lifecycle: version the tree per cycle (or regenerate it from dated snapshots) rather than editing one approved file weekly, because every edit reopens an approved artifact (CLAUDE.md § Phase Gate Rules, #1288).
- **Strengthen, don't duplicate, the candidate `customer-discovery-interviewing` skill** (`the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md:37`, extended by `competing-against-luck-christensen.md:80`). Torres supplies the parts it lacks: the story prompt scoped to an outcome, excavation, the interview snapshot template, the standing recruiting pipeline, and the weekly cadence. With those, the candidate skill becomes the one interviewing home that `jtbd-analysis`, `user-persona` and `feedback-template` point to.
- **Assumption testing: merge with Cagan's `discovery-prototyping` candidate** (`inspired-cagan.md:47`) rather than proposing a second skill. Torres's assumption types, importance × evidence map and pre-set thresholds are the "what to test and when it counts" half. Cagan's prototype types are the "how to test" half. One skill, `assumption-testing` or `discovery-prototyping`, should hold both.
- **No new agent.** The trio is a working pattern, not a role this plugin can staff. Opportunity mapping and assumption ranking are knowledge `requirements-analyst` applies. A recurring "log this week's conversation" step could later become a command (raw notes → snapshot → tree update), since commands are the workflow tier. That is a later option, not a requirement of the book.

## Key Terminology

Candidates for `skills/glossary-management/`. Check each against existing entries first. *Product Trio* and *Continuous Discovery* are already proposed in `inspired-cagan.md:56,61`. Reuse those definitions, and refine *Continuous Discovery* with Torres's weekly-touchpoint definition rather than adding a second entry.

- **Desired Outcome**: the metric at the root of an opportunity solution tree. Preferably a product outcome.
- **Business Outcome / Product Outcome / Traction Metric**: three kinds of metric. A business outcome measures the business's progress and lags. A product outcome measures a customer behaviour change the team can drive directly. A traction metric measures use of one feature, and assigning it decides the solution.
- **Opportunity**: a customer need, pain point or desire, written from the customer's point of view and answerable by more than one solution. Distinct from a *job* (`jtbd-analysis`), which sits higher and is solution-free by definition, and from an impact-map *impact*, which is a behaviour change.
- **Opportunity Solution Tree**: the outcome → opportunities → solutions → assumption tests tree used to plan and show discovery decisions.
- **Target Opportunity**: the one opportunity chosen, by comparison with its siblings, for solution work.
- **Experience Map**: the team's drawing of the customer's experience as currently understood. Used to structure the top of the opportunity space.
- **Story-Based Interview**: an interview built around one specific past instance ("tell me about the last time…"), excavated in detail, used to find opportunities and not to test solutions.
- **Interview Snapshot**: the one-page synthesis made after each interview, the unit that feeds opportunity mapping.
- **Compare-and-Contrast Decision**: choosing among several options at once, as opposed to a "whether or not" decision on one option.
- **Assumption Test**: a small, fast test of one assumption behind a solution, with success criteria set before it runs. Assumption types: desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, ethical.

## Caveats

- **Confident:** the weekly-touchpoint definition of continuous discovery, in substance, not as a verbatim quote. The product trio. Business vs product outcomes and traction metrics, with the advice to assign product outcomes. The opportunity solution tree's structure, and the rule that opportunities are not solutions in disguise. Story-based interviewing ("the last time you…") and its use for opportunities, not solution testing. The interview snapshot as per-interview synthesis. Automating recruiting as the habit's foundation. Compare-and-contrast over whether-or-not decisions. Generating several solutions and carrying a few forward. Testing assumptions rather than ideas, with the five types including ethical. Setting success criteria before a test.
- **Less certain:** the exact fields of the interview snapshot (the list above is my reconstruction). The exact four-lens list for opportunity prioritisation, and whether the book uses "two-way door" in those words. Whether "around three" solutions is the book's stated number or my summary. The precise research Torres cites for solo-then-pooled ideation. That the importance × evidence assumption map is credited to David Bland (*Testing Business Ideas*) and not original to Torres. How fully the book treats learning goals vs performance goals. Whether the book itself suggests framing top-level opportunities as jobs to be done.
- **No page numbers, quotes or statistics are given, deliberately.** Nothing above is a direct quotation. Phrases in quotation marks are illustrative prompts, not citations.
- **The book assumes a cross-functional team with direct customer access. This plugin has neither in the literal sense.** Agents cannot interview, and the "trio" is one human plus agents reset between phases. Every adaptation above (the human interviews and the agent synthesises; feasibility notes from an engineering agent; snapshots made from notes the agent didn't witness) is this repo's, and the skills should say so rather than attribute it to Torres. Torres does address teams without a designer, or with limited access to customers, near the end of the book, but I don't recall that guidance well enough to cite it.
- **It conflicts with phase gating.** Torres treats discovery as continuous and parallel to delivery. The plugin gates phases and reopens approved artifacts on edit. The tree and snapshots therefore need a storage and versioning rule (dated evidence files plus a per-cycle tree) before they can live in the manifest without constant re-approval.
- **It overlaps three sibling files. Use it for its own contribution only.** Question rules belong to Fitzpatrick (`the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md`), the switch interview and job formats to Christensen, Ulwick and Klement (`competing-against-luck-christensen.md`), the four risks and the trio's rationale to Cagan (`inspired-cagan.md`), and goal measurement and the path-and-assumption columns to Adzic (`impact-mapping-adzic.md`). Torres's own contribution is the outcome typology, the opportunity solution tree, the snapshot and synthesis loop, the recruiting and cadence habit, compare-and-contrast prioritisation, and the assumption-test discipline.
- **Practitioner source with a commercial context.** Torres teaches this method commercially (Product Talk). The evidence base is her coaching practice, not controlled research, which is the same norm the repo applies to its other practitioner books.
- **It grounds `feedback-template` only partly.** It grounds capture style, need-to-opportunity interpretation and opportunity-level aggregation. It does not ground severity, routing or SLAs, and the skill must not cite it for them.

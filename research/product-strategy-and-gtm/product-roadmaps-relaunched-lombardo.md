# Product Roadmaps Relaunched: How to Set Direction while Embracing Uncertainty
**Authors:** C. Todd Lombardo, Bruce McCarthy, Evan Ryan, Michael Connors
**Domain:** Product roadmapping: what a roadmap is, what goes on it, how it is prioritised, agreed, shared and kept current (within Product Strategy and Go-to-Market)

## Core Concepts

- **A roadmap is a strategic communication tool: a statement of intent and direction.** The book (O'Reilly, 2017) opens by arguing that most roadmaps fail because they are really release plans: lists of features with dates, made before anyone knows whether those features solve anything. The authors redefine the roadmap as a document that says *where the product is going and why*, in terms of the value it will deliver to customers and the business, and that is honest about how much is still unknown. It is a promise about direction and value, not about specific features on specific days. This is the stance `roadmap-authoring/SKILL.md:24-28` already takes. Until now that stance had only `inspired-cagan.md:17` behind it, which supports the anti-feature-list half and not the structure of the replacement. This book is the dedicated source the findings asked for (`wave-1-findings.md:783`).
- **A roadmap is not a release plan, a backlog or a project plan.** The book draws a firm line between strategic and tactical artifacts:
  - The **roadmap** says *why* and *what problems, in what order*. Its timeframes are broad. Its audience is everyone, from executives to customers.
  - The **release plan** (or delivery plan) says *which solutions ship when*. It has dates, and it changes often. Its audience is the delivery team.
  - The **backlog** is the list of work items that carries out the near-term part of the roadmap.
  - A **Gantt-style project plan** tracks tasks and dependencies to a fixed date. Put on a roadmap, it creates false precision.

  The roadmap *feeds* the release plan; it should never be generated *from* one. The skill names "Release plan in disguise" as an anti-pattern (`SKILL.md:137`) and says "dates belong to release planning", but it never says where release planning lives in this plugin. No skill owns it today.
- **Five primary components.** The book's central structure is a short list of things every roadmap needs. As I recall it:
  1. **Product vision.** The roadmap opens by restating the destination, so every theme can be read against it. In this plugin, that means a one-line reference to `vision-statement`, not a second vision.
  2. **Business objectives.** The measurable outcomes the roadmap is meant to deliver. The book points to OKRs and KPIs as the usual form. Every theme should serve at least one objective, and an item that serves none is a candidate to drop.
  3. **Timeframes.** Broad, not dates: Now / Next / Later, or near-term / mid-term / future, or quarters near in and halves or years further out. The further out, the broader the timeframe.
  4. **Themes.** The heart of the roadmap (see below).
  5. **Disclaimer.** A plain statement that the roadmap reflects current understanding and will change. The book treats this as a required part of the artifact, not a footnote, because it sets expectations with every audience that sees it.
- **Five secondary components, added when the audience or the product needs them.**
  - **Features or solutions.** Candidate solutions sit *under* a theme, mostly in the near term. They are examples of how the problem might be solved, not commitments.
  - **Stage of development.** Where each theme stands: being researched, in discovery, in development, in beta, released. This shows progress without dates.
  - **Confidence.** How sure the team is about each item's scope and timing. Confidence should fall as the timeframe gets further out.
  - **Target customer.** Which segment or persona a theme serves, when the product serves more than one.
  - **Product areas.** Swimlanes for parts of the product, teams or product lines, when one product is large or several share a roadmap.
- **Themes are customer needs or problems, not features.** A theme names a problem worth solving or a job the customer is trying to do, phrased so that more than one solution could answer it. The book's test is roughly: would a customer recognise this as their problem, and could the team discover a different solution and still satisfy the theme? Themes can nest (a broad theme with narrower sub-themes) and can span more than one timeframe. Grouping work under themes is what lets a team change the solution without breaking the promise. This is close to, but not the same as, the skill's "outcome" rows (`SKILL.md:84`, `:98-105`). An outcome says what will be true. A theme says which customer problem is being worked on. The book uses both: the theme names the problem, and the business objective says how success is measured.
- **Broad timeframes and the Now / Next / Later shape.** The book favours timeframes that get looser with distance and is wary of dates anywhere past the near term. It discusses the Now / Next / Later layout, which I recall it crediting to Janna Bastow of ProdPad, alongside "near / mid / future" and quarter/half variants. When an organisation insists on dates, the advice is to keep them coarse and to attach confidence to them, rather than to fake precision.
- **Getting the facts first: the inputs before the roadmap.** Before any themes are drafted, the book has the team gather the strategic context: the vision, the business objectives, the customer research and the market. The order in the book runs vision → objectives → customer needs (themes) → prioritisation → alignment → presentation → keeping it current. That supports the owner's order **vision → OKRs → roadmap**: objectives come first, and themes are chosen because they serve them. The book also expects movement the other way. If no theme can credibly move an objective, the objective is revisited. That is the same feedback check proposed in `measure-what-matters-doerr.md:41` and `good-strategy-bad-strategy-rumelt.md` (the kernel mapping table).
- **Prioritise with an explicit framework, and pick the one that fits.** The book surveys several methods rather than prescribing one. The ones I recall with reasonable confidence:
  - **Critical path.** Follow the steps a customer must go through to get value. Anything that blocks that path comes first. This is the simplest method and suits early products.
  - **Kano model.** Sort needs into basic expectations, performance needs and delighters. Basic expectations must be met before delighters earn anything.
  - **Desirability / feasibility / viability.** Score each candidate on whether customers want it, whether it can be built and whether it serves the business.
  - **A value-versus-effort ROI scorecard.** Score each theme's value (against the objectives) and its effort, and include a confidence factor so that a high-value guess does not beat a well-understood item on paper alone.

  The book's point is that the *choice* of framework, and the scores, should be visible, so stakeholders argue about the inputs rather than the outcome. It also stresses that a framework supports judgement and does not replace it.
- **Alignment and buy-in are worked for before the roadmap is shown, not after.** The book spends real time on getting stakeholders to support the roadmap. Two techniques stand out:
  - **Shuttle diplomacy.** Meet key stakeholders one at a time before any group review. Learn their concerns, show them how their input shaped the draft, and resolve objections privately.
  - **Nemawashi.** A Japanese term for preparing the ground: talking a change through informally with everyone it affects before the formal decision, so the meeting confirms a consensus rather than creating a fight.

  The book separates **alignment** (everyone understands and supports the direction) from **consensus** (everyone agrees with every item). Alignment is the goal. It also treats **saying no** as a core skill: declining a request by tying the answer to the objectives and the vision, and recording it, rather than burying it in "Later".
- **Present and share: one roadmap, several views.** Different audiences need different detail. Executives want objectives and themes. Sales wants to know what to tell customers and what not to promise. Engineering wants the near-term detail. Customers get an external version with less detail and no internal dates. The book is wary of roadmaps used as sales collateral, because promises made to close a deal turn themes into dated feature commitments. The disclaimer goes on every version.
- **Keep it alive.** A roadmap is reviewed on a regular cadence (the book suggests something like monthly for the near term and quarterly for the whole) and whenever something material changes: a new objective, a market shift, discovery results. Each update should say what changed and why, and the people who saw the old version should hear about the change. A roadmap that is not updated becomes a fiction people stop trusting. The skill's "Silent re-planning" anti-pattern (`SKILL.md:141`) already says this, but the Output Format has nowhere to record a change.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Restructure each roadmap row around a theme.** Each row is: *Theme* (the customer problem, in the customer's words) → *Objective / KR it serves* → *Candidate solutions* (Now only, marked as examples) → *Stage* → *Confidence* → *Dependencies*. Later rows carry only the theme, the objective and the confidence. This keeps the theme constant across horizons, so an item moving from Later to Next gains detail without being rewritten.
- **Write every theme as a problem with more than one possible solution.** Test it: if the theme can only be met by one specific feature, it is a feature in disguise. "Compliance officers find out about new Restricted data too late to act" is a theme. "Recurring scans and alerts" is a solution to it.
- **Make the disclaimer a required section, not a quoted line.** The skill's preamble (`SKILL.md:119`, `:162-163`) already does the job. Name it the **disclaimer**, require it on every audience version, and fail the artifact without it.
- **Name the prioritisation framework in the artifact.** Add a short "How this was prioritised" line: the framework used and, at standard and enterprise, the scores. Default to **critical path** at lean, and to a **value / effort / confidence scorecard** at standard and enterprise, with Kano used when the question is "which needs are table stakes?". This turns the skill's "Sequence by value and dependency" (`SKILL.md:86`) into a stated method. It does not replace MoSCoW, which works at release-scope level in Ideate (`moscow-prioritization`).
- **Pre-wire the roadmap before review (scaled).** For a solo operator, nemawashi collapses to one move: before asking for approval, list the decisions the owner is most likely to push back on and give the reason for each. At standard and enterprise, read the `stakeholder-mapping` engagement plan (`stakeholder-mapping/SKILL.md:80-86`) and record who was consulted, one at a time, before the group review.
- **Record every "no".** Requests that were declined go in "Explicitly Not On This Roadmap" with the objective or vision reason (and, per Rumelt, the guiding-policy reason). This is the book's "saying no" practice, and it is what keeps Later from becoming landfill (`SKILL.md:139`).
- **Add a revision log and named triggers.** A "Revision history" table (date, what moved, what was dropped, the learning that caused it). Revise at minimum: at each OKR cycle, when discovery results arrive, and when the Ideate story map exists (its release slices are the first concrete test of the Now horizon, per `story-mapping/SKILL.md:124` and `wave-1-findings.md:284`).
- **Separate the roadmap from the release plan explicitly.** Add a short "Roadmap vs release plan" table to the skill. State that dated delivery plans are produced later, from the Now horizon and the story map's slices, and are not part of the strategic roadmap.
- **Produce audience views only when there is an audience.** Lean: one view. Standard and enterprise: an internal view plus an external view (themes and stages only, no dates, disclaimer on top).

## Key Terminology

These are not yet in `glossary-management`. Adding any of them to an artifact template needs a glossary entry first (CLAUDE.md § Ubiquitous Language).

- **Product roadmap**: a strategic communication tool; a statement of intent and direction showing the customer problems the product will address, in what order, toward which objectives, with honest uncertainty.
- **Theme**: a customer need, problem or job the roadmap commits to addressing, phrased so more than one solution could satisfy it. The unit of a roadmap.
- **Timeframe** (or **horizon**): a broad period on the roadmap (Now / Next / Later, or near / mid / future) that grows wider the further out it is.
- **Disclaimer**: the required statement that the roadmap reflects current understanding and will change.
- **Stage of development**: where a theme stands (research, discovery, development, beta, released), shown instead of a date.
- **Confidence**: the stated certainty of a roadmap item's scope and timing, which falls with distance.
- **Release plan**: the tactical, dated plan of which solutions ship when. Downstream of the roadmap; not part of it.
- **Shuttle diplomacy**: meeting stakeholders individually before a group review to surface and resolve objections.
- **Nemawashi**: preparing agreement informally with everyone a decision affects before the formal decision is made.
- **Alignment** (versus **consensus**): shared understanding of and support for the direction, without requiring agreement on every item.
- **Critical path** (prioritisation): ordering work by what blocks a customer from reaching value.
- **Kano model**: classifying needs as basic expectations, performance needs or delighters.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** the redefinition of the roadmap as a strategic communication tool and statement of intent and direction; the warning against feature-and-date roadmaps; the split between primary components (vision, business objectives, timeframes, themes, disclaimer) and secondary ones (features, stage of development, confidence, target customer, product areas); themes as customer needs or problems; broad timeframes and wariness of dates; the chapters on prioritisation, alignment and buy-in, presenting and sharing, and keeping the roadmap fresh; shuttle diplomacy and nemawashi as alignment techniques; saying no as a skill; the roadmap-versus-release-plan distinction.
- **Less certain:** the exact list and order of prioritisation frameworks (critical path, Kano and desirability/feasibility/viability I am fairly sure of; the exact shape of the ROI scorecard and its confidence factor less so); whether the book gives numeric confidence bands (I do not recall it doing so, so treat the skill's >80% / 50–80% / <50% as unsourced); the precise review cadence it suggests; the exact credit for Now/Next/Later to Janna Bastow; exact chapter titles and order. The book includes survey and interview material from practitioners. None of its figures are reproduced here. No page numbers, direct quotations or statistics are given, deliberately.
- **The book assumes a product organisation.** Alignment, audience views and sales pressure matter most with stakeholders, sales teams and several product teams. For the lean, solo profile, what carries over is the structure (vision line, objectives, broad timeframes, problem themes, disclaimer), the honesty about confidence, and the habit of recording "no" and every revision. Alignment reduces to anticipating the owner's objections.
- **Themes versus outcomes is a real difference, not a rename.** The skill's rows are outcomes ("what will be true"). The book's rows are themes ("which problem we are working on"), measured by objectives. Adopting the book means rows carry both: the theme, plus the KR it moves. Do not replace outcome statements with vague theme labels. Keep a theme name sharp enough that a customer would recognise the problem.
- **This is practitioner guidance, not empirical research.** The authors are consultants, product leaders and tool builders, and the evidence is interviews, surveys and case stories. Treat the component lists and frameworks as well-tested defaults.
- **Overlap with sibling files.** `inspired-cagan.md` holds the anti-feature-roadmap stance and the outcome roadmap; this book supplies the structure that replaces the feature list. `measure-what-matters-doerr.md` holds the OKR mechanics and the order argument from the OKR side, including roadmap items as Initiatives. `good-strategy-bad-strategy-rumelt.md` holds coherence, the weakest-link sequencing rule and guiding-policy exclusions; those are complementary to this book's prioritisation frameworks, not replaced by them. `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` holds bowling-alley sequencing, which this book's "target customer" component gives a place on the roadmap. `user-story-mapping-patton-economy.md` holds the release slices that test the Now horizon later.

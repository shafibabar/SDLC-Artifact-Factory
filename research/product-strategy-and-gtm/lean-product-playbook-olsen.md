# The Lean Product Playbook
**Authors:** Dan Olsen
**Domain:** Product Strategy and Go-To-Market — Product-Market Fit Discovery Process

## Core Concepts

- **The Product-Market Fit Pyramid: five layers, bottom to top, each built on the one below it.** Olsen's central device is a pyramid with five stacked layers: **1. Target Market** (who the customers are) → **2. Underserved Needs** (what needs those customers have that aren't well met today) → **3. Value Proposition** (the product concept — the story of how the product uniquely satisfies the underserved needs, relative to alternatives) → **4. Feature Set** (the specific features that deliver the value proposition) → **5. User Experience** (the actual design, flow, and look-and-feel the customer touches). Product-market fit exists when the top four layers are genuinely built on a correctly-identified bottom layer — the pyramid is not five independent checklists, it is a dependency chain: an error in target market invalidates everything stacked above it, and a feature set that doesn't trace back to an underserved need is waste, no matter how well-executed the UX.
- **The bottom two layers are "problem space," the top three are "solution space."** Target Market and Underserved Needs describe the customer's world as it exists independent of your product — they should be researchable and describable without ever mentioning what you're building. Value Proposition, Feature Set, and UX describe your specific answer to that problem. Olsen is emphatic that teams collapse this distinction constantly — writing a "need" that is secretly a feature already decided upon ("customers need a dashboard" is a solution disguised as a need; "customers need to know at a glance whether they're at risk before a meeting" is the actual need a dashboard might satisfy). Confirming problem-space work is done *before* solution-space work begins is the pyramid's main discipline.
- **The Opportunity Score: importance and satisfaction, surveyed separately, combined into a single ranking.** To decide which underserved needs are the biggest opportunity, Olsen (building on Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation) has customers rate each candidate need on two 1–10 scales in a survey: how *important* is this to you, and how *satisfied* are you with how well it's met today. A need that is very important and poorly satisfied is a high-opportunity need; a need that is important but already well satisfied is not worth building for (customers are already served); a need that is unimportant is not worth building for regardless of satisfaction. The formula Olsen popularizes: **Opportunity Score = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)**. This produces a single ranked list of needs, rather than a qualitative sense that "customers seem to care about X."
- **The Lean Product Process: six steps that operationalize the pyramid, bottom to top.** (1) Determine your target customer, (2) identify underserved needs, (3) define your value proposition, (4) specify your minimum viable product feature set, (5) create your MVP prototype, (6) test the MVP with customers — then iterate steps 4–6 based on what's learned. This is Olsen's Lean/Agile-flavored operationalization of the pyramid: it is explicitly iterative at the top (feature set → prototype → test → revise) while treating the bottom two layers (market, needs) as comparatively more stable once validated, since re-litigating "who is this for" on every sprint is expensive and usually unnecessary once genuinely validated.
- **The "hi-fi MVP": validate the UX at high fidelity, before writing production code.** Olsen distinguishes a coded, functional MVP from what he calls a hi-fi (high-fidelity) MVP — a realistic-looking, clickable prototype (mockups, wireframes advanced to pixel-accurate visual design, prototyping tools) that a target customer can react to as if it were real, without a single line of production code behind it. His argument: the cost of being wrong about the UX or feature set is far higher after engineering has built it than before; a hi-fi prototype lets you run steps 5–6 of the Lean Product Process (test the MVP, learn, iterate) at prototype speed, days instead of sprints, and iterate the feature set and UX layers cheaply before committing the (expensive, hard-to-reverse) engineering layer. This reframes "MVP" away from "smallest set of features we ship" and toward "smallest artifact that lets us validate before we build" — a coded MVP is downstream of a validated hi-fi MVP, not a synonym for it.
- **The Kano Model: features don't all convert satisfaction the same way — three (or five) response curves, not one.** Borrowed from Noriaki Kano, this is Olsen's prioritization technique for the Feature Set layer once candidate features exist. Each feature is classified by how its presence/absence and quality affect customer satisfaction: **Basic (Threshold/Must-be) features** — expected table stakes; their absence causes strong dissatisfaction, but their presence beyond baseline does not increase satisfaction (a flat, diminishing-returns curve — customers simply expect them and stop noticing once met). **Performance (One-dimensional) features** — satisfaction scales roughly linearly with how well the feature is delivered; more/better genuinely produces more satisfaction, and less genuinely produces dissatisfaction. **Delighter (Attractive/Excitement) features** — unexpected; their absence does not cause dissatisfaction (customers weren't expecting them), but their presence produces disproportionate delight (an exponential curve). Two secondary categories round out the model: **Indifferent** (customers don't care either way — investment here is waste) and **Reverse** (some customer segment actually prefers the feature's absence). Olsen's strategic point: a product needs to clear the bar on all Basic features (or it will be rejected regardless of anything else), invest incrementally in Performance features (diminishing but real returns), and include at least one or two genuine Delighters (differentiation and word-of-mouth come disproportionately from these, not from Basic features, no matter how well executed).
- **Kano classification uses a paired functional/dysfunctional survey question, not a single rating.** For each candidate feature, customers answer two questions: the *functional* form ("How would you feel if the product had this feature?") and the *dysfunctional* form ("How would you feel if the product did NOT have this feature?") — each on a 5-point scale (I like it / I expect it / I am neutral / I can tolerate it / I dislike it). The two answers are cross-referenced in a standard Kano evaluation table to classify the feature (e.g., "like" on functional + "tolerate" on dysfunctional typically classifies as Attractive/Delighter; "expect" on functional + "dislike" on dysfunctional typically classifies as Must-be/Basic). This paired-question structure is what distinguishes Kano from a single "how important is this" rating — it specifically separates the cost of *absence* from the value of *presence*, which a single importance score conflates.
- **Segmentation matters because needs, and their Kano classification, differ by customer segment.** Olsen repeatedly stresses that "the market" is rarely one homogeneous group with identical needs — the same feature can be a Delighter for one segment and Indifferent (or even Reverse) for another. He recommends running the needs-importance/satisfaction survey and the Kano survey per meaningful segment, not once across an undifferentiated respondent pool, and treating a single blended average as a trap that can hide the fact that no real segment is actually well served.

## Actionable Techniques

- Before finalizing a value proposition, walk the pyramid bottom-up explicitly: confirm the target market is defined, confirm underserved needs within that market are identified and scored, and only then draft the value proposition as "the specific way we satisfy the highest-opportunity needs better than the current alternatives." Treat a value proposition drafted before needs are identified as invalid regardless of how compelling it reads.
- Run an Opportunity Score exercise on candidate underserved needs — survey (or, absent survey capacity, structured stakeholder/design-partner interviews scored the same way) importance and satisfaction per need on a 1–10 scale, compute `Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)`, and rank. Use the ranked list, not team intuition, to decide which needs the value proposition and feature set actually target.
- Before committing engineering time to a feature set, build a hi-fi (non-functional, high-fidelity) prototype of the proposed UX and test it with 5–8 target-market users, watching for comprehension and reaction rather than asking "would you use this" (a notoriously unreliable question). Treat this as the real MVP checkpoint; a coded MVP that has skipped this step is testing engineering feasibility, not product-market fit.
- For the finalist feature set, run a Kano classification pass (functional/dysfunctional paired questions per feature, per meaningful segment) before binning features into a delivery-window prioritization method. Use the classification to check that: every Basic feature clears its bar (non-negotiable, regardless of stated priority elsewhere), Performance features get incremental investment proportional to available capacity, and at least one Delighter survives the cut — a feature set with zero Delighters is differentiation-free by construction.
- Treat a "need" statement that names a UI element, a button, or a screen as a smell: rewrite it in the customer's problem-space language until it could be satisfied by more than one possible feature. This is the pyramid's problem-space/solution-space discipline applied as a rewrite test.
- When a lower pyramid layer changes (a new target-market insight, a needs re-scoring), explicitly re-check every layer above it rather than patching only the layer that prompted the change — a changed underserved-need ranking can silently invalidate a value proposition that was tuned to the old ranking.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **Candidate new skill: `kano-prioritization`.** This clears the bar the repo has applied in prior research files (most recently in `lean-analytics-croll-yoskovitz.md`, which declined a standalone "cohort analysis" skill specifically because cohort analysis lacks "its own elicitation method, template, or quality criteria" and folds cleanly into an existing skill as a technique). Kano does not fold as cleanly: it has its own elicitation method (the paired functional/dysfunctional survey, per feature, per segment), its own output artifact (a Kano classification table distinct from a MoSCoW table), and its own quality criteria (segment-specific sampling, avoiding a blended average, re-running when the feature set changes materially). As analyzed above, it answers a materially different question from `moscow-prioritization` (satisfaction-curve shape versus capacity-constrained inclusion), so it is not a rename or a restatement of existing content. Proposed shape: owned by `requirements-analyst`, used in the Ideate phase after `jtbd-analysis` (needs must be known before candidate features can be classified) and before or alongside `moscow-prioritization` (Kano classification informs, but does not replace, the Must/Should/Could/Won't binning).
- **Not proposed as a standalone skill: a "PMF Pyramid" or "product-market-fit-pyramid" skill.** Four of the pyramid's five layers are already substantively covered by existing skills under different names — Target Market by `user-persona` (plus `business-model-canvas`'s Customer Segments block), Underserved Needs by `jtbd-analysis`, Value Proposition by `business-model-canvas`'s Value Propositions block, and Feature Set by `moscow-prioritization` (plus, if adopted, `kano-prioritization`). The UX layer is likely owned by a ux-architect skill this file did not read (see Caveats) and so cannot be confirmed either way. Building a whole new skill to re-describe a five-layer stack that is already, layer-by-layer, someone else's job would violate this repo's own anti-pattern guidance against components that overlap another's domain. The two genuinely new, non-duplicative pieces of Olsen's content — the Opportunity Score technique and the Kano Model — are proposed above as additions to `jtbd-analysis` and as the standalone `kano-prioritization` skill respectively, which is the right-sized adoption per this repo's established pattern (see `lean-analytics-croll-yoskovitz.md`'s own "right size of change" conclusion, reached the same way).
- **Not proposed: a hi-fi MVP / prototyping skill**, pending confirmation against the unread `ux-flow-design` skill (see Caveats) — this concept may already live there under different terminology (e.g., "low/mid/high-fidelity wireframes" is common UX vocabulary independent of Olsen), and proposing a new skill without checking for that overlap first would risk exactly the kind of undiscovered duplication this file's honesty standard is meant to prevent.
- No case for a new agent. Every genuinely new technique above (Opportunity Score, Kano classification) is knowledge — a scoring method and a survey-question structure — not reasoning distinct from what `requirements-analyst` already performs; both belong in skills the existing agent consumes, consistent with `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md`'s identical conclusion for that book's material.

## Key Terminology

Worth considering for `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language, if this cluster's concepts are adopted into a skill:

- **Product-Market Fit Pyramid** — Olsen's five-layer dependency stack (Target Market → Underserved Needs → Value Proposition → Feature Set → User Experience). Distinct from the generic phrase "product-market fit" (already used once in this repo, in `business-model-canvas`'s Lean Canvas section, without this specific five-layer structure behind it) — naming the pyramid precisely would let a future skill reference the layer-dependency discipline rather than the vaguer phrase alone.
- **Problem Space / Solution Space** — Olsen's split between the bottom two pyramid layers (customer's world, describable without reference to the product) and the top three (the product's specific answer). Functionally identical in spirit to `jtbd-analysis`'s existing "solution-shaped jobs" anti-pattern, arrived at independently — if adopted as formal terminology, cross-reference rather than treat as a new concept.
- **Underserved Need** — a customer need that is important but not well satisfied by current alternatives, as distinct from a need generally. The qualifier ("underserved") is load-bearing: a well-served important need is not an opportunity, however important it remains.
- **Opportunity Score** — Olsen's formula (`Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)`), built on Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation methodology, for ranking underserved needs from paired importance/satisfaction survey ratings. Not currently used anywhere in this repo's skills.
- **Kano Model / Basic (Must-be) / Performance (One-dimensional) / Delighter (Attractive) / Indifferent / Reverse** — Noriaki Kano's feature-classification framework by satisfaction-response curve shape. Not currently used anywhere in this repo's skills; distinct from, and not a synonym for, MoSCoW's Must/Should/Could/Won't (see the `moscow-prioritization` row above for the precise distinction — one is a satisfaction-curve classification, the other is a capacity-constrained inclusion decision).
- **Hi-Fi MVP** — Olsen's term for a high-fidelity, non-functional, clickable prototype used to validate the feature set and UX layers before committing engineering resources to a coded MVP. Distinct from "MVP" used loosely to mean "the smallest shippable version of the real product" — in Olsen's usage the hi-fi MVP is explicitly not shipped and not coded.

## Caveats

- **Check the sibling files in this cluster once all exist.** This file was written alongside `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` (already present in `research/product-strategy-and-gtm/` at the time of writing and read directly for this file) but before `obviously-awesome-dunford.md` and `loved-lauchengco.md` exist. Moore's own caveats section already anticipates the overlap addressed here: Moore's beachhead-segment-selection (a strategic, criteria-driven choice of *which* narrow segment to attack first, with whole-product economics and sales-force sequencing in view) and Olsen's Target Market layer (an iterative-validation question of *who the underserved needs belong to*) are related-but-distinct lenses on "who first" — Moore's is a sequencing and defensibility question, Olsen's is a discovery and validation question. Neither supersedes the other; a future reader should not merge them into one "target market" step without preserving both concerns. Dunford's positioning methodology (not yet read) is very likely the more detailed treatment of Olsen's Value Proposition layer specifically (positioning and value proposition are adjacent but not identical: positioning is how the value proposition is framed against competitive alternatives for a buyer, the value proposition is the underlying pain-relieved/gain-created claim) — reconcile rather than duplicate once that file exists, most likely keeping Olsen's pyramid for needs-to-value-prop derivation and Dunford's for the competitive framing of the resulting value proposition.
- **No overlap with `research/data-and-analytics/lean-analytics-croll-yoskovitz.md` was found, and this was checked directly.** That file's domain is metric selection and validation for an already-built or already-launched product (vanity vs. actionable metrics, OMTM, cohort analysis) — a measurement discipline. This file's domain is the pre-build discovery process for deciding *what to build in the first place* (target market, needs, value proposition, feature set, UX) — a discovery/validation discipline that precedes metrics entirely. The two are sequential, not competing: Olsen's process produces the feature set and hypotheses; Lean Analytics' techniques would be used afterward to measure whether the shipped result actually achieved fit. Olsen's book does touch measurement only glancingly (e.g., a brief mention of tracking engagement to validate the hi-fi MVP's follow-on build), and that glancing content is not reproduced here to avoid duplicating Lean Analytics' more rigorous treatment of the same territory.
- **The UX layer (pyramid layer 5) and the hi-fi MVP concept were not checked against `skills/ux-flow-design/SKILL.md`, which this file's brief did not include in required reading.** It is plausible that skill already covers prototyping fidelity levels under standard UX vocabulary independent of Olsen (low-fi/mid-fi/hi-fi wireframes are common industry terms, not unique to this book). A future reader adopting any UX-layer or hi-fi-MVP content from this file should read that skill first and treat this file's UX-layer claims as unverified against existing repo content, not as a confirmed gap.
- **The book's examples are consumer and B2B SaaS products circa the mid-2010s (its two extended case studies are a consumer photo-sharing app and a B2B marketing SaaS product), and its survey-based techniques (Opportunity Score, Kano) assume access to a customer base large enough to survey meaningfully.** For this repo's actual first product — pre-launch, design-partner stage, 3–5 named customers per `gtm-strategy`'s closed-beta motion — a formal quantitative survey of 50+ respondents per Olsen's own recommended sample size is not yet feasible. The techniques still apply at smaller scale (structured interview scoring in place of a survey, per Croll & Yoskovitz's own "qualitative before quantitative" ordering, which Olsen's process implicitly assumes too), but a future adopter should scale the *rigor* of the scoring method to the actual number of design partners available, not treat Olsen's survey-sample-size guidance as a hard prerequisite that blocks using the ranking logic at all.
- **This book predates, and is a likely uncredited ancestor of, some already-independently-arrived-at content in this repo.** `jtbd-analysis`'s "solution-shaped jobs" anti-pattern and `business-model-canvas`'s feature-vs-value-proposition warning both restate Olsen's problem-space/solution-space discipline without citing it — this file does not claim those skills are wrong or incomplete on that point, only that the underlying discipline has a named source and a slightly more general frame (the full five-layer pyramid) than either skill individually exposes.

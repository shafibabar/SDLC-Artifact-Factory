# Good Strategy / Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters
**Authors:** Richard P. Rumelt (with his later book *The Crux: How Leaders Become Strategists*, 2022, where noted)
**Domain:** Strategy formulation: what a strategy is, how to tell it from goals and slogans, and how it connects purpose to objectives and action (within Product Strategy and Go-to-Market)

## Core Concepts

- **A strategy is a response to a challenge, not a statement of ambition.** Rumelt's central claim (the book is from 2011) is that most documents called "strategy" are not strategies at all. They are goals, visions, slogans or budgets. A real strategy starts by naming the obstacle that stands between the organisation and what it wants, and then says how to get past it. In the plugin's terms, this is the link the Strategy phase is missing. `vision-statement/SKILL.md:34` already says that "strategy is a separate thing: the focused, sequenced bets that move toward the vision", and `:71` gives the chain "one product vision → product strategy (which problems first) → team objectives". But no Strategy artifact actually holds the "product strategy" link. The vision, the mission, the OKR set and the roadmap each touch part of it, and none of them owns it. The Doerr file says the same thing from the OKR side (`measure-what-matters-doerr.md:92`).
- **The kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.** Rumelt's core structure has three parts, and he insists all three must be present:
  - **Diagnosis.** It explains what is going on and what makes the situation hard. A good diagnosis cuts a complicated situation down to the few things that matter most, and so it already tells you where to look for a solution. It is a judgement, not a list of data. A diagnosis can be wrong, and it should be written so that it can be shown to be wrong.
  - **Guiding policy.** This is the overall approach chosen to deal with the obstacles the diagnosis names. Rumelt compares it to guardrails: it rules out whole families of actions and points effort in one direction, but it does not list the actions. It usually gains its power by using a source of advantage, or by concentrating effort where it will count.
  - **Coherent actions.** These are concrete steps, commitments of resources and policies that carry out the guiding policy. "Coherent" is the key word. The actions must coordinate and reinforce each other, and they must not work against each other. A pile of separately sensible initiatives is not coherent action.
- **The hallmarks of bad strategy.** Rumelt names four:
  - **Fluff.** Buzzwords and abstraction made to sound like strategic thinking. It restates the obvious in grand language and gives no guidance. `mission-statement/SKILL.md:157-159` already rejects "generic aspirational fluff". Rumelt is the source for that term.
  - **Failure to face the challenge.** If a strategy does not name the problem, nobody can judge it or improve it. This is the most common failure, and it is what happens when a document has goals but no diagnosis.
  - **Mistaking goals for strategy.** Performance targets ("grow 20%", "be the market leader") are set, and the plan stops there. A goal says what you want. A strategy says how you will overcome what stops you getting it. Setting ambitious targets and urging people on is not strategy. This is the hallmark most relevant to `okr-authoring`.
  - **Bad strategic objectives.** These come in two forms. The first is a **"dog's dinner"** of objectives: a long, mixed list of things to do, with no priority and no link to a diagnosis, often the output of a planning meeting where every function adds its item. The second is the **"blue-sky" objective**: a restatement of the hoped-for end state, with no account of how to get there and no feasible next step.
- **Why bad strategy is so common.** Rumelt gives three reasons. First, **strategy requires choice**: saying no to some people, projects and markets. Organisations avoid that discomfort and so produce lists that offend nobody. Second, **template-style strategy**: fill-in-the-blank planning that produces a vision statement, a mission statement, a set of values and a list of "strategic goals", and calls the bundle a strategy. Rumelt criticises this approach directly, because every slot can be filled without ever diagnosing a problem. Third, **"New Thought"** thinking: the belief that a vivid enough vision and positive thinking will make success happen. This criticism of templates applies directly to this plugin's Strategy order. See "What the book implies about a mission statement" below.
- **Sources of power.** Most of the book's second part covers where a good strategy gets its force. Each source comes with case studies.
  - **Leverage.** Concentrate effort on a pivot point where a small, well-chosen action produces a large effect. Leverage comes from anticipating others' behaviour, from spotting the pivot, and from concentrating instead of spreading.
  - **Proximate objectives.** A **proximate objective** is a target close enough to be feasible: one the organisation can be expected to reach, and one that removes enough ambiguity that people know what to do next. Rumelt treats creating proximate objectives as a core leadership job. His best-known example is from the lunar programme, as I recall it. Nobody knew what the Moon's surface was like, so engineers were given a written specification of an assumed surface to design the lander against. That turned an open question into a solvable design problem. The more uncertain and dynamic the situation, the closer the objective needs to be.
  - **Chain-link systems.** In some systems performance is set by the **weakest link**, so improving any other link adds nothing. Such systems tend to get stuck in mediocrity, because no single improvement pays off by itself. Getting out requires either fixing the binding link first or improving several links together under strong central direction. Once excellence is achieved, the same chain-link quality makes it hard for others to copy.
  - **Design.** Some advantage comes from **fitting parts together**: a set of resources and actions, coordinated in time and space, that only works as a whole. His military example of design is Hannibal at Cannae. The point for product work: coherence is designed in, not added up from separately chosen items.
  - **Focus.** Concentrate on one segment, one job, or one advantage, rather than serving everyone a little. Focus is a form of design applied to who you serve.
  - **Growth.** Rumelt is sceptical of growth as a goal in itself. Healthy growth is the *result* of an advantage that keeps being renewed. Growth bought by acquisition or by chasing size is often value-destroying.
  - **Using advantage.** An advantage is an asymmetry that lets you produce more value, or the same value more cheaply, than rivals in a specific situation. Rumelt stresses that advantages are often **narrow and situational**. The strategic work is deepening an advantage, widening the area it applies to, building demand for what it delivers, or making it harder to copy. Simply having an advantage is not enough.
  - **Using dynamics.** Ride **waves of change**: shifts in technology, regulation, cost or customer behaviour that disrupt existing positions. The skill is to see a wave early and to understand what it changes and what it leaves alone, so as to position for the new structure.
  - **Inertia and entropy.** **Inertia** is an organisation's unwillingness or inability to adapt: its routines, its culture, and the hold of the status quo. A rival's inertia can be an opportunity. Your own inertia is a challenge the diagnosis must name. **Entropy** is the natural drift of any organisation towards disorder, confusion and loss of focus unless management keeps working against it. For a product, the backlog and the roadmap drift towards a "dog's dinner" unless they are regularly pruned back to the guiding policy.
- **Strategy as a hypothesis.** Part III ("Thinking like a strategist") treats a strategy as a **hypothesis about what will work**, to be tested against what actually happens, not a plan to defend. Rumelt warns against **premature closure** (grabbing the first plausible answer) and suggests checking your own judgement by imagining how informed critics would attack it. This fits well with the repo's existing hypothesis framing of Key Results (`okr-authoring/SKILL.md:32`). That framing is currently credited to LeMay. Rumelt's version applies to the strategy as a whole, not only to one KR.
- ***The Crux* (2022): find the crux, and treat strategy as problem-solving.** Rumelt's later book keeps the kernel and sharpens where it starts. As I recall its main ideas:
  - **The crux.** Among all the challenges facing an organisation, the strategist looks for the **crux**: the one that is both *important* (solving it unlocks a lot of progress) and *addressable* (something can actually be done about it). Picking a challenge that is critical but impossible is as bad as picking one that is easy but trivial.
  - **Challenge-led, not goal-led.** Rumelt argues hard against starting strategy with goals or a vision. He is critical of corporate "strategic plans" that are really resource budgets, and of goal-setting systems used as a substitute for strategy. I recall him naming the OKR fashion as an example of goals standing in for strategy, though I am not sure of the exact framing. Work starts from a written list of challenges, narrows them to the crux, and only then sets objectives.
  - **Gnarly problems.** Real strategic challenges are badly structured: the goals conflict, the causes are unclear, and the possible actions are not known in advance. Templates fail on them because the problem's shape is part of what has to be worked out.
- **What the book implies about a "mission statement" as an artifact.** Plainly: Rumelt gives **no support** for a mission statement as a distinct strategy artifact, and he names the vision–mission–values template as a *cause* of bad strategy. A mission sentence is not a diagnosis, not a guiding policy and not an action. The repo's mission does useful work in only two places. Both belong to other parts of the kernel:
  - **The scope constraint** ("a mission you cannot use to say 'no' is not doing its job", `mission-statement/SKILL.md:35-37`; the out-of-scope list, `:102-103`, `references/mission-examples-and-template.md:33-36`). In Rumelt's terms, this is the job of the **guiding policy**. A guiding policy rules things out *because of* a diagnosis. A mission rules things out because of a sentence's wording. The first can be argued with. The second cannot.
  - **The present-day beneficiary and outcome** ("We [do X] for [user] so that [outcome]", `:79-83`). This restates the vision's protagonist and need (`vision-statement/SKILL.md:57-58`) in the present tense. It adds no new decision.

  Rumelt would not call the repo's mission bad. Its quality criteria (single verb, named beneficiary, out-of-scope list) are designed to keep fluff out. But the artifact's slot in the Strategy order is where the **missing diagnosis and guiding policy** should be. Today, at the lean profile, the mission and GTM are skipped (`artifact-manifest/resources/phase-requirements.json`, entries `mission-statement` and `gtm-strategy`). That means a lean product goes from a vision straight to OKRs and a roadmap with no written statement of the challenge at all.
- **How diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent actions maps onto vision → OKRs → roadmap.** The mapping is close but **not one-to-one**, and the gap is the important part.

  | Kernel element | Where it lives today | Fit |
  |---|---|---|
  | (Purpose / destination: not part of the kernel) | **Vision**: future state, protagonist, why it matters | Rumelt would call the vision a statement of aspiration. It is necessary context, but it is **not** a strategy. |
  | **Diagnosis** | Partly the vision's "today's need" (`vision-statement/SKILL.md:58`). Partly scattered across competitive analysis and the business model canvas | **Missing as a statement.** Nothing says "the critical obstacle is X, and this is why it is hard". |
  | **Guiding policy** | Partly the vision's "durable bet" and "rules out" (`:61`, `:64`). Partly the mission's scope constraint. Partly GTM's beachhead choice | **Spread across three artifacts**, and two of them are skipped at lean. |
  | **Proximate objectives** (a source of power, not part of the kernel) | **OKRs** | Good fit. An Objective should be the next feasible target the guiding policy implies, and its KRs show it was reached. |
  | **Coherent actions** | **Roadmap** | Good fit, *provided* the roadmap is tested for coherence (items reinforce each other under one guiding policy), not just for traceability (each item links to some KR). |

  So the owner's order **vision → OKRs → roadmap** is consistent with Rumelt *if* a diagnosis and guiding policy sit between the vision and the OKRs. Without them, the OKR set is exactly "goals mistaken for strategy": targets with no stated theory of how the obstacle is overcome. The roadmap then has nothing to be coherent *with* except a list of KRs. This also settles the order question in W1-2 on principle: objectives come from the guiding policy, and actions come from the objectives. The one feedback loop is that if no coherent set of actions could reach an objective, the objective is not proximate and goes back for revision. This matches the feedback check in `measure-what-matters-doerr.md:41`.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Write the kernel down, in three short labelled paragraphs.** *Diagnosis:* the critical challenge, why it is hard, and what makes it the crux (important *and* addressable). *Guiding policy:* the approach, what it rules out, and the advantage or leverage it relies on. *Coherent actions:* the 3–6 main moves and how they reinforce each other. For lean, this fits in about 150 words. Where this text lives is the owner's decision (see Applies To). Whatever the location, it should exist at every profile.
- **Run the four bad-strategy tests on every Strategy artifact before hand-off.** (1) *Fluff:* delete every adjective and abstraction. Is a decision still left? (2) *Challenge named:* can a reader say what obstacle this addresses? (3) *Goal or strategy:* does the text say *how*, or only *how much*? (4) *Objectives:* are they a short list that follows from one guiding policy, or a dog's dinner?
- **Make every Objective proximate.** For each Objective, ask two things. Can the team reasonably reach it this cycle with the means it has? And once it is set, does the team know what to do on Monday? A "be the leader in X" Objective fails both tests: it is blue-sky. Note the tension with Doerr's aspirational OKRs (`measure-what-matters-doerr.md:13`). Stretch is fine for a KR target, but the Objective itself should still be a feasible next position, not an end state.
- **Test the roadmap for coherence, not just traceability.** For each Now item, ask: does it make another item easier or more valuable, or does it pull resources away from one? Mark the items that reinforce each other. An item that links to a KR but reinforces nothing else is a candidate to cut. That is entropy at work.
- **Look for a weakest link before sequencing.** Ask: is there one capability whose weakness caps the value of everything else? Examples are trust in classification accuracy, or data never leaving the tenant. If so, it goes first in Now, ahead of "highest value". Improving the other links first wastes effort while that one is still weak.
- **Derive "what we will not do" from the guiding policy, not from a sentence.** Every out-of-scope item, whether in the vision's rules-out list, a mission scope list or the roadmap's "Explicitly Not On This Roadmap" section, should cite the diagnosis or guiding-policy reason it is excluded.
- **Treat the kernel as a hypothesis with a review trigger.** Record what evidence would show that the diagnosis was wrong (a beta result, a lost-deal pattern, a churn reason). When that evidence arrives, revise the kernel *before* the next OKR cycle.

## Key Terminology

These are not yet in `glossary-management`. Adding any of them to an artifact template needs a glossary entry first (CLAUDE.md § Ubiquitous Language).

- **Strategy (Rumelt)**: a coherent response to an important challenge: a diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions. Not a goal, a vision or a budget.
- **Kernel**: the three-part core every good strategy contains: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.
- **Diagnosis**: a judgement that explains the situation and names the critical obstacle, simplifying complexity down to what matters.
- **Guiding policy**: the overall approach to overcoming the diagnosed obstacle. It rules out whole families of action without listing actions.
- **Coherent actions**: coordinated, mutually reinforcing commitments of resources that carry out the guiding policy.
- **Proximate objective**: a target close enough to be feasible, which removes enough ambiguity that the organisation knows what to do next.
- **Crux** (*The Crux*): the challenge that is both the most important and addressable. It is where strategy work focuses.
- **Fluff**: abstract, buzzword-heavy language posing as strategic thought.
- **Dog's dinner (of objectives)**: a long, unprioritised list of objectives with no common guiding policy.
- **Blue-sky objective**: an objective that restates the desired end state with no feasible path.
- **Chain-link system**: a system whose performance is capped by its weakest link.
- **Leverage**: concentrating effort on a pivot point where a small action produces a large effect.
- **Inertia / entropy**: an organisation's resistance to change / its natural drift into disorder and loss of focus.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** the kernel and its three parts, and Rumelt's insistence that all three be present; the four hallmarks of bad strategy (fluff, failure to face the challenge, goals mistaken for strategy, bad strategic objectives, including "dog's dinner" and "blue-sky"); the three reasons bad strategy is common, including his direct criticism of vision–mission–values template planning; the list of sources of power as section themes (leverage, proximate objectives, chain-link systems, design, focus, growth, advantage, dynamics, inertia and entropy); strategy as a hypothesis; *The Crux*'s central idea that the crux is both important and addressable, and that strategy starts from challenges, not goals.
- **Less certain:** the exact chapter order and titles; which case study sits under which chapter. Apple's 1997 turnaround and the first Gulf War are kernel examples I am fairly sure of. Hannibal at Cannae for design, and the lunar-surface specification for proximate objectives, I recall with moderate confidence. Other company cases (Nvidia, Crown Cork & Seal, the Resnicks' businesses, AT&T inertia) I remember only loosely, so they are left out of the guidance above. For *The Crux*, I am confident of the crux idea and the challenge-led stance, less sure of its exact wording on OKRs and strategic plans, and unsure of its chapter structure. No page numbers, direct quotations or statistics are given, deliberately. The Porter attribution for "the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do" is recalled, not checked against the text.
- **Rumelt does not write about product management.** His cases are companies, armies and governments. The mapping onto vision → OKRs → roadmap is this file's interpretation, not his. He never mentions roadmaps. He mentions OKRs, if at all, only as an example of goal-setting standing in for strategy.
- **Tension with Doerr, to be stated in the skills rather than hidden.** Doerr's aspirational OKRs (`measure-what-matters-doerr.md:13`) and Rumelt's warning against "goals as strategy" can be reconciled: OKRs are proximate objectives *under* a guiding policy, and stretch belongs in KR targets, not in a blue-sky Objective. But a reader of Rumelt alone would be more sceptical of OKRs than this repo is.
- **This is argued practitioner judgement, not empirical research.** Rumelt is a strategy academic (UCLA Anderson) and consultant. His evidence is case analysis, chosen to illustrate rather than to test. Treat the kernel as a strong, widely used structure, not as a proven method.
- **Scale.** The kernel scales down well: a solo operator can write it in a paragraph per part. The sources-of-power material assumes rivals and organisations, and matters more at the standard and enterprise profiles.
- **Overlap with sibling files.** `inspired-cagan.md` holds the vision-versus-strategy split and outcome roadmaps. `measure-what-matters-doerr.md` holds OKR mechanics and the order argument from the OKR side. `obviously-awesome-dunford.md` holds positioning, which supports the "advantage, not mission" fix for W1-4. `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` holds the beachhead, which is a *focus* guiding policy in Rumelt's terms. This file adds only the link between them: the diagnosis and the guiding policy.

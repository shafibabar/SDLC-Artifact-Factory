# Crossing the Chasm
**Authors:** Geoffrey A. Moore
**Domain:** Product Strategy and Go-To-Market

## Core Concepts

- **The Technology Adoption Lifecycle is a five-segment bell curve, not a single "market."** Moore adapts Everett Rogers' diffusion-of-innovations curve into five buyer psychographics: **innovators** (technology enthusiasts, want the newest thing for its own sake, tolerate anything that's broken), **early adopters** (visionaries, buy to gain a revolutionary strategic advantage before competitors, will tolerate bugs and missing features in exchange for being first), **early majority** (pragmatists, buy evolutionary improvements to infrastructure they already trust, want proven references from people like them before committing), **late majority** (conservatives, buy only once a solution is a de facto standard and cheap, resist change), and **laggards** (skeptics, buy only when a technology is embedded invisibly in something else). Each segment does not just adopt later than the last — it buys for a *different reason* and requires a different value proposition, channel, and pricing model. Treating "the market" as one undifferentiated curve is the root error the whole book corrects.
- **The chasm sits between early adopters and the early majority, and it is the single hardest gap in the curve.** Visionaries (early adopters) and pragmatists (early majority) are not adjacent-and-similar groups — they are nearly opposite buyer psychologies. A visionary wants a revolutionary, unproven capability and will act as their own reference; a pragmatist wants an evolutionary, safe, well-referenced choice and explicitly distrusts the enthusiasm of visionaries as a signal ("if the visionaries love it, it's probably immature"). A go-to-market motion built to win visionaries — leading with the most novel, unproven capability, selling on vision rather than track record — actively repels pragmatists. Most B2B technology products that "die on the vine" are not failing to get noticed; they win early adopters, generate excitement, and then stall permanently because the early-majority sales motion never gets built. This chasm-death pattern looks like: strong early references that never convert into a second wave, deals that stay stuck in extended proof-of-concept indefinitely, and revenue that plateaus just above the early-adopter ceiling.
- **The beachhead (D-Day) strategy: concentrate all resources on winning one narrow market segment completely, rather than spreading thin across many.** Moore's central metaphor is the Normandy invasion — Allied forces did not attempt to liberate all of France simultaneously; they picked one narrow beach, committed overwhelming force to it, and only then expanded outward from a secured position. Applied to GTM: pick a single, narrowly defined target market segment (not "the market" generically) and win 100% of it — not 5% of a huge market. Total domination of a small, well-chosen segment produces word-of-mouth, referenceability, and a repeatable sales/marketing motion that a diluted multi-segment effort never produces. A company that "goes wide" too early ends up as a distant also-ran in five markets instead of the clear leader in one.
- **Target market segment selection is a disciplined choice against explicit criteria, not an intuitive pick.** Moore's criteria for a viable beachhead segment: the segment has a *compelling reason to buy* now (not a nice-to-have); the segment is reachable by a *single, coherent sales and marketing channel*; the segment's economic buyer is identifiable and accessible; no entrenched competitor already owns the segment; the segment is winnable with the resources actually available; and — critically — the segment can serve as a credible *reference base* for expansion into adjacent segments. A segment that scores well on market size alone but fails on referenceability or channel coherence is a trap.
- **Whole product thinking (adapted from Theodore Levitt): pragmatists buy the whole product, not the generic product.** The *generic product* is what ships in the box — the core software. The *whole product* is everything the target customer actually needs to realize the compelling reason to buy in full: integrations with the systems they already use, implementation and support services, documentation, training, a credible ecosystem of partners, and proof (case studies, certifications, references) that the solution works in their specific context. Visionaries will assemble a whole product themselves out of enthusiasm; pragmatists will not — if the whole product isn't already assembled and packaged, they will not buy, no matter how good the generic product is. Moore's four-ring whole-product model (generic product → expected product → augmented product → potential product) is a planning tool for identifying exactly what is missing between what you ship and what the target segment needs to say yes.
- **The bowling alley: after the beachhead is won, expand into adjacent segments in a deliberate sequence, using each win as a reference for the next.** The revised edition's "bowling alley" elaboration extends the D-Day metaphor: like bowling pins, each adjacent market segment is knocked over using the momentum and reference credibility from the segment just won, rather than attacking all adjacent segments simultaneously. Segment adjacency is defined by shared whole-product requirements or a shared buying influence — a segment is a good "next pin" if much of the whole product already assembled for the beachhead transfers directly.
- **Positioning for the pragmatist buyer must emphasize safety, not novelty.** Once a company is marketing to the early majority, the message must shift from "revolutionary" to "proven, safe, and referenceable" — pragmatists are risk-averse buyers who look for signals that others like them have already succeeded with this choice (case studies, named references in their own vertical, market-leadership claims). Moore's positioning statement template — "For (target customer) who (statement of need or opportunity), the (product name) is a (product category) that (statement of key benefit). Unlike (primary competitive alternative), our product (statement of primary differentiation)" — is designed specifically to force this segment-specific, competitively-grounded framing rather than a generic feature pitch.
- **Economic buyer versus user/technical buyer.** Especially once crossing into the early majority, the person who feels the pain and the person who signs the check are frequently different roles with different risk tolerances. The economic buyer for a pragmatist-segment purchase evaluates total cost of ownership and organizational risk, not technical elegance — whole-product completeness and referenceability are what this buyer actually evaluates, even when a technical champion inside the account is the one who found the product.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Plot the current customer base on the Technology Adoption Lifecycle curve before writing any GTM strategy.** If existing traction is concentrated in visionaries (early, high-risk-tolerance buyers who bought for competitive advantage rather than proven value), explicitly flag that the company has not yet crossed the chasm — the next GTM motion needs a different message and channel, not just "more of the same, faster."
- **Run the beachhead-segment selection worksheet before defining a single ICP.** Score every candidate market segment against Moore's six criteria (compelling reason to buy now, coherent single channel, accessible economic buyer, no entrenched competitor, winnable with available resources, credible reference base for expansion) rather than picking the segment with the largest addressable market. The segment that wins is often the smaller, narrower one.
- **Build a whole-product map for the chosen beachhead segment, explicitly separating "what we ship" from "what the customer needs to say yes."** List every integration, service, document, certification, and partner relationship the target economic buyer will require before the compelling reason to buy is fully realized — then treat gaps in that list as GTM/roadmap work, not just product backlog items.
- **Draft the positioning statement using Moore's exact template** (for [target customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [benefit]; unlike [primary alternative], we [differentiator]) and check that "target customer" names the beachhead segment specifically, not the whole addressable market.
- **After the beachhead is won, sequence expansion using bowling-pin logic**: for each candidate adjacent segment, name what fraction of the already-assembled whole product transfers unchanged, and prioritize segments where that fraction is highest — expansion cost is lowest where whole-product reuse is highest.
- **Run a "stuck in the chasm" diagnostic periodically once early traction exists**: are deals closing on a repeatable cycle time, or extending indefinitely in proof-of-concept? Are new customers referenceable to each other (same vertical, same buying trigger), or is each win a one-off? Are late-stage deals being won on vision, or on proof? A "yes" to indefinite POCs and a "no" to mutual referenceability are the classic symptoms of a company stuck between early adopters and the early majority.

## Key Terminology

Worth considering for `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language, if this cluster's concepts are adopted into a skill:

- **Technology Adoption Lifecycle** — the five-segment adoption curve (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards). Distinct from a generic "customer segment" — each lifecycle position implies a different buying psychology, not just a different demographic.
- **The Chasm** — the specific gap between early adopters and the early majority, where GTM motions built for visionaries fail against pragmatists. Not a synonym for "market risk" generally — it names one specific, well-documented transition.
- **Beachhead Market** — the single, narrowly defined target market segment selected for total domination before any expansion is attempted. Distinct from `gtm-strategy`'s existing "ICP" term: an ICP describes the profile of a good customer; a beachhead is the strategic choice of *which narrow segment to attack first and completely*, chosen against explicit criteria (channel coherence, economic buyer accessibility, absence of entrenched competition, reference value for later expansion).
- **Whole Product** — the generic product plus everything else (integrations, services, documentation, partners, proof) the target segment's buyer needs to fully realize the compelling reason to buy. Distinct from "feature completeness" — the whole product can require zero additional generic-product features and still be incomplete if support, integration, or proof points are missing.
- **Bowling Alley / Bowling Pin Strategy** — the deliberate, sequenced expansion from a won beachhead into adjacent segments, prioritized by how much of the assembled whole product transfers unchanged. Distinct from broad-based simultaneous multi-segment expansion.
- **Economic Buyer** — the individual who owns the budget and bears organizational risk for the purchase decision, as distinct from the technical or user champion who may have initiated interest. Relevant wherever `gtm-strategy`'s Sales and Distribution Model or `business-model-canvas`'s Customer Segments block distinguish "users" from "buyers" — Moore's term sharpens that existing buyer/user split specifically for the economic-risk dimension.

## Caveats

- **Check the sibling files in this cluster once all exist, rather than assuming no overlap.** This file was written before `obviously-awesome-dunford.md`, `loved-lauchengco.md`, and `the-lean-product-playbook-olsen.md` existed in `research/product-strategy-and-gtm/`. Likely overlaps to resolve when all four are present: Moore's beachhead-segment-selection criteria versus Olsen's product-market-fit pyramid's "target customer" layer (both ask "who first?" from different angles — Moore emphasizes strategic sequencing and whole-product economics, Olsen emphasizes iterative validation of underserved needs); and Moore's positioning-statement template versus Dunford's full positioning methodology (competitive alternatives → unique attributes → value → market category → target segment), where Dunford's is very likely the more operationally detailed treatment of positioning specifically, with Moore's version being the coarser, earlier-generation ancestor. A future skill author should not implement both templates as if they were independent — reconcile them, most likely by keeping Dunford's as the detailed positioning technique and Moore's as the segment-sequencing/beachhead technique that decides *which* segment gets positioned against first.
- **The book's canonical examples are enterprise, sales-force-driven, hardware-and-software B2B from the early-1990s high-tech industry (with a 1999 and 2014 revision updating examples toward enterprise software and, briefly, consumer tech).** Its assumed GTM motion is direct/channel enterprise sales with long cycles and a human sales force — not the self-serve, product-led-growth motion that `gtm-strategy`'s Component 5 (Sales and Distribution Model) already recommends as the default for this repo's SMB private-deployment product ("self-serve with a guided POC"). The beachhead and whole-product concepts translate cleanly (they are about market sequencing and completeness, not about sales-force mechanics), but Moore's specific advice about deploying and compensating a sales force to attack a beachhead does not apply here and should not be imported.
- **This repo's actual first product may already have an implicit, unnamed beachhead.** `sdlc-context.json`'s `first_product` section defines the target market as "SMBs (50–500 employees), private deployment" with an MVP scoped narrowly to three file formats (PDF, DOCX, XLSX), two storage connectors (Google Drive, AWS S3), and one compliance framework (SOC 2 — CC6, CC7, A1), with remediation workflows explicitly out of scope. `gtm-strategy`'s Launch Sequencing Stage 1 additionally specifies "3–5 pre-selected customers who helped shape requirements" as a closed-beta design-partner motion. Taken together, this scoping *behaves like* a beachhead (a narrow, winnable slice of a larger market) but was arrived at through MVP feasibility and design-partner practicality, not through Moore's explicit six-criteria segment-selection process, and it is not named as a beachhead anywhere in the repo's strategy skills. This is the central finding of this research file: the discipline is implicit in the scoping decisions but absent as a *named, criteria-driven technique* — nothing currently forces a check of whether "SMBs 50–500 employees, SOC 2" is actually the segment with a coherent single channel, an accessible economic buyer, no entrenched competitor, and — most importantly for what happens after Stage 1 — a clear next adjacent segment for bowling-alley expansion. Without that, the design-partner motion validates a *product* but does not by itself validate that a *beachhead was strategically chosen* rather than merely convenient.
- **"Crossing the chasm" and "launch stage" are different axes and must not be conflated if this material is adopted into `gtm-strategy`.** Moore's chasm is about buyer psychology across the technology adoption curve (visionary versus pragmatist); the existing Launch Sequencing component (closed beta → soft launch → GA) is about audience size and marketing exposure over time within a presumed-fixed target segment. A product can complete all three launch stages while still selling only to visionaries and never crossing the chasm — the two concepts are complementary, not substitutes, and a future skill revision should keep them as separate checks rather than merging Stage 1–3 into "the chasm-crossing process."
- **The book is a strategic-sequencing text, not a positioning-copywriting or pricing text.** It says relatively little about message wording, channel-execution tactics, or pricing mechanics beyond "whole product must be priced/packaged for the pragmatist buyer" — those are already covered in more operational depth by `gtm-strategy`'s existing Messaging Framework, Channel Strategy, and Pricing Model components. Treat this book as the source for *which segment, in what order, with what completeness bar* — not as a replacement for the tactical GTM content already in the skill.

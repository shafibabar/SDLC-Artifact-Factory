# The Four Steps to the Epiphany
**Authors:** Steve Blank (primary text); secondarily *The Startup Owner's Manual*, Steve Blank & Bob Dorf (2012), cited only where it articulates the same framework more clearly
**Domain:** Product Strategy and Go-To-Market — Customer Development Methodology

## Core Concepts

- **Customer Development is a four-stage, parallel-but-sequential process, run alongside (not instead of) product development, and it is the direct ancestor of this repo's phase name.** Blank's four stages: **Customer Discovery** (find out if anyone has the problem you think they have, and whether your proposed solution addresses it — testing the founders' hypotheses about problem, product, and customer against real people outside the building), **Customer Validation** (build a repeatable, scalable sales roadmap that other salespeople — not just the founder — can follow, by actually selling to a small number of early customers using that roadmap; the test is not "does the product work" but "can we sell/onboard it the same way twice, profitably, using a process we can hand off"), **Customer Creation** (once the roadmap from Customer Validation is proven, turn on demand-generation spending to scale it), and **Company Building** (transition the organization itself from an informal, hypothesis-testing structure optimized for learning into formal functional departments optimized for execution at scale). Each stage has its own exit criteria and its own distinct failure mode; treating all four as one undifferentiated "get customers" activity is the error the whole methodology corrects — directly parallel to Moore's warning (`crossing-the-chasm-moore.md`) against treating the Technology Adoption Lifecycle as one undifferentiated market.
- **"Get out of the building" — the discipline that no fact about the customer exists until it has been tested against a real customer's real behavior outside the founder's own head.** Blank's most quoted line, and the organizing discipline beneath all four stages: a founder's own conviction, an internal team's consensus, or even a slide deck's logical coherence proves nothing about whether a real customer has the problem, will pay for the solution, or will buy it the way the sales roadmap assumes. Every Customer Development stage is structured as a cycle of stating a hypothesis, then testing it face-to-face with prospective customers, then revising the hypothesis based on what was actually observed — not what the team hoped to hear. This is the same underlying discipline Fitzpatrick's Mom Test (`the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md`) operationalizes at the level of a single conversation (talk about their life, ask about the past, listen more) — Blank names the organizational-process-level version of the same problem (a team can collectively fool itself just as easily as one founder can be led by a compliment), while Fitzpatrick supplies the conversational mechanics for not fooling yourself once you're actually outside the building. They are the same discipline at two different altitudes, not two competing ideas.
- **"Earlyvangelists" — Blank's specific, more demanding replacement for "early adopter," defined by four checkable conditions, not a psychographic type.** An earlyvangelist (a portmanteau of "early" and "evangelist") is a prospective customer who: (1) has a problem they already recognize as painful — not one that needs to be explained to them; (2) has already spent their own time, money, or organizational capital trying to solve that problem themselves — a spreadsheet, a manual workaround, a consultant, an internally-built stopgap — which is direct evidence the pain is real rather than merely acknowledged; (3) has, or can obtain, budget to pay for a real solution; and (4) is visible or credible enough within their own organization or market to act as a reference once they've adopted the product. All four conditions are procedural and checkable against a specific candidate — this is a screening test a founder can actually run on a named person, not a personality profile to infer.
- **The Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder) is Customer Development's companion artifact — the canvas states the hypotheses; Customer Development is the process that tests them against real customers.** Blank (in *The Startup Owner's Manual* specifically, which post-dates and explicitly incorporates the by-then-published Business Model Canvas more than the original 2005 book does) frames each block of the canvas — Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Revenue Streams, and the rest — as an untested guess at the outset, and Customer Discovery/Validation as the iterative process of getting out of the building to confirm or invalidate each block one at a time, updating the canvas as evidence comes in rather than treating it as a document written once and left static. This is background, not this file's primary finding — `business-model-canvas` in this repo (Strategy phase, owner `product-strategist`) does not currently frame its nine blocks as testable hypotheses requiring Customer-Development-style validation; it presents completing the canvas as a single planning exercise. That framing gap is real but secondary to this research pass's assignment and is not developed further here.
- **A pivot is a named, legitimate outcome of a failed Customer Development test — distinct from both "success" and "just try harder."** When a Customer Discovery or Customer Validation hypothesis fails real-world testing, Blank's methodology treats that failure as information about which specific assumption was wrong (the target segment, the stated problem, the proposed solution, the pricing model, or the channel) — not as a verdict on the company or a reason to simply repeat the same test with more effort or more time. The response is to revise the specific wrong hypothesis and run the test again — Blank calls this returning to an earlier step; Eric Ries, building directly on Blank's Customer Development work for *The Lean Startup*, later coined the now-more-common term "pivot" for this same move (a structured change in a specific hypothesis, made in response to validated evidence that the current one is wrong, while keeping what testing has confirmed). The two vocabularies name the same discipline; "pivot" is the term that stuck in wider product usage, and is the term used here for clarity.
- **Customer Validation's actual test is repeatability and salability, not just "does the product work for these customers."** This is the single most load-bearing distinction for grounding this repo's usage of the same phase name. Blank is explicit that Customer Discovery already establishes product/problem fit with a handful of earlyvangelists; Customer Validation's job is different and harder — prove that a *documented, repeatable sales and onboarding process* (a "roadmap") converts a *new* prospect, using people other than the founder, before spending real money to scale demand generation in Customer Creation. A Customer Validation stage that only re-confirms "yes, earlyvangelists like it" without testing whether the sales/onboarding motion itself is repeatable has not actually completed Blank's Customer Validation — it has re-run Customer Discovery.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Screen every candidate closed-alpha/closed-beta participant against Blank's four earlyvangelist conditions before recruiting them**, not just against psychographic fit: does this named contact already recognize the problem unprompted, have they already spent real effort (time, money, a workaround) trying to solve it themselves, do they have or can they get budget, and are they credible enough in their organization or market to serve as a reference afterward. A candidate who scores well on "resembles the ICP" but has never actually tried to solve the problem is a weaker signal than one who has a battle-worn spreadsheet already.
- **Before declaring a beta stage's qualitative graduation bar met, run an explicit "outside the building" check**: was the "usable for its intended purpose" affirmation obtained by asking the participant to describe specific, observed behavior and friction (Mom-Test-style, past-tense, specific-instance) — or was it an internal team consensus, a demo the team ran and liked, or a leading question the participant politely agreed with? A qualitative bar met by internal conviction rather than externally observed behavior has not actually been tested outside the building, regardless of how confidently it's recorded.
- **When a stage fails its graduation bar, explicitly name which hypothesis is suspect before deciding whether to extend, remediate, or pivot** — segment (wrong participant profile), problem (the participants don't actually have the pain assumed), solution (the pain is real but this doesn't solve it), or process (the product is right but the sales/onboarding roadmap doesn't repeat). "Extend the timeline" is the correct response only when the hypothesis itself is believed sound and simply needs more evidence; it is the wrong response when the evidence gathered so far actually points at one specific hypothesis being wrong.
- **Track a design partner's self-funded workaround explicitly as a selection artifact** — record what they were already doing before the product existed (spreadsheet, manual process, a competing tool, a consultant) as part of the participant record, not just their ICP-fit rationale. This is a specific, checkable fact (Blank's condition 2) rather than an impression, and it directly predicts whether the resulting feedback will be motivated by real pain or by goodwill toward the relationship.
- **When compiling closed-beta evidence for `acceptance-sign-off`, add a repeatability check distinct from the per-participant usability affirmation**: did onboarding this participant use the same documented steps as the previous one, with the same effort/support level — or did each participant require bespoke, founder-led hand-holding? A "yes, it works" from three participants who each needed a different ad hoc onboarding path is Customer-Discovery-grade evidence, not Customer-Validation-grade evidence, under Blank's own distinction between the two stages.

- **Customer Development** — Blank's four-stage methodology (Customer Discovery, Customer Validation, Customer Creation, Company Building) for testing a startup's hypotheses about customers, problems, and go-to-market process against real-world behavior before scaling. - **Customer Validation** — Blank's second Customer Development stage: proving a repeatable, scalable sales/onboarding roadmap works by actually selling to early customers with it, not merely confirming the product works for them. Confirmed **not** currently a glossary headword — it appears in `CLAUDE.md` and `agents/requirements-analyst.md`/`beta-program-design`'s frontmatter purely as a phase name, with no definition tracing it to Blank's methodology or distinguishing it from the generic "Product Validation" headword already in the glossary's Product and Discovery section. - **Earlyvangelist** — Blank's four-condition definition of a qualified early customer (already-recognized pain, prior self-funded attempt to solve it, budget or access to it, reference-credible). Not present in the glossary. Distinct from Moore's "early adopter"/"visionary" (already covered in `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md`'s Key Terminology, itself also not yet in the glossary per that file's own note) — see the Caveats section below for the precise distinction.
- **Pivot** — a structured, evidence-driven change to one specific hypothesis (segment, problem, solution, or process) after a Customer Development test fails, while retaining what testing has already confirmed; distinct from both "the program failed" and "just extend the timeline." - **Get Out of the Building** — Blank's discipline that no customer-behavior fact is established until tested against real customers outside the team, as opposed to internal-team consensus. ## Caveats

- **Blank's earlyvangelist and Moore's early adopter/visionary overlap heavily but are not the same concept, and a future skill revision should not merge them.** Both describe a customer segment that buys before the mainstream market is ready to. But Moore's early adopter is defined *psychographically* — a visionary who wants a revolutionary strategic advantage and will tolerate being first, defined by what they want and how much risk they'll accept. Blank's earlyvangelist is defined *procedurally* — four checkable facts about a specific named candidate (already recognizes the pain, already tried to fix it themselves, has or can get budget, is reference-credible), independent of their psychological motivation. In practice the two populations overlap substantially (a Moore-style visionary very often satisfies Blank's four conditions), but they are useful for different jobs: Moore's framework is the right tool for deciding *which market segment* to attack first (the beachhead); Blank's framework is the right tool for screening *which specific named person* within an already-chosen segment is actually a good beta/design-partner candidate. `beta-program-design`'s Participant Selection table is doing the second job, which is why Blank's contribution lands there specifically and Moore's beachhead-selection material (already noted in `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` as landing in `gtm-strategy`) does not.
- **This file deliberately treats "The Four Steps to the Epiphany" (2005) and "The Startup Owner's Manual" (Blank & Dorf, 2012) as one methodology across two editions, per this research task's own framing, rather than presenting them as competing accounts.** Where the two differ in practice: the 2005 original is denser, more academic in tone, organized strictly around the four-step Customer Development process, and predates the Business Model Canvas and Lean Startup vocabulary that later became standard; the 2012 successor is written for a broader founder audience, explicitly incorporates Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas as the artifact whose hypotheses Customer Development tests, and uses "earlyvangelist" and "get out of the building" with the benefit of Ries's by-then-published Lean Startup terminology (including "pivot," which Ries coined building on Blank's own underlying idea) folded back in. This file draws the core stage definitions and the earlyvangelist criteria from both editions as a single account, and cites the canvas connection and the "pivot" cross-reference specifically to the 2012 edition's more explicit articulation, consistent with the task's own instruction to treat them as one methodology, not two.
- **Confidence level on this summary is high for the core stage structure, the earlyvangelist criteria, and "get out of the building"** — these are the book's most consistently and prominently cited contributions across startup/product-management practice, and are drawn from strong, well-documented general knowledge of both editions' actual, well-known content. **Confidence is more moderate on precise chapter-level details or exact original wording** (e.g., the book's specific worked case studies, precise phrasing of exit criteria per step) — this file does not claim to reproduce those and none of its findings above depend on them; every finding is grounded in the stage-purpose distinctions, the earlyvangelist checklist, and the pivot concept, all of which are treated at the level of well-established methodology rather than page-specific claims.
- **The "pivot" term itself is, strictly, Eric Ries's coinage (The Lean Startup, 2011) for a move whose underlying discipline originates in Blank's Customer Development work** (Ries was Blank's student and collaborator, and *The Startup Owner's Manual*'s 2012 text explicitly uses "pivot" in describing Customer Development outcomes). This file uses "pivot" throughout because it is the term that reached this repo's likely audience and is the clearer, more current label — but a strict bibliographic reading would credit the vocabulary to Ries and the underlying process discipline (revise a specific tested hypothesis rather than repeat the test unchanged) to Blank. Neither this repo nor this cluster has yet produced a dedicated Lean Startup research file; if one is added later, the pivot-vocabulary attribution should be reconciled there rather than re-litigated in this file.
- **The book's canonical context is Silicon Valley enterprise/hardware startups of the 1990s–2000s, often with venture funding and a multi-person founding team explicitly assigned to Customer Development conversations** — not a solo-operator, private-deployment SMB compliance product. The core discipline (test hypotheses against real customers before scaling) translates without modification; the book's team-coordination mechanics (a VP of Customer Development, a dedicated Customer Development team distinct from the engineering team) do not apply to this repo's single-agent-per-role architecture and should not be imported, consistent with how `the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md`'s Caveats handles the same team-versus-solo-operator mismatch for its own source material.

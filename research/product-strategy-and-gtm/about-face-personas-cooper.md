# About Face / The Inmates Are Running the Asylum: Goal-Directed Personas
**Authors:** Alan Cooper, *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum* (1999; second edition 2004). Alan Cooper, Robert Reimann, David Cronin and (from the fourth edition, 2014) Christopher Noessel, *About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design* (the personas and goals chapters, and the Goal-Directed Design process around them). Read alongside John Pruitt and Tamara Adlin, *The Persona Lifecycle: Keeping People in Mind Throughout Product Design* (2006), and its shorter follow-up, Adlin and Pruitt, *The Essential Persona Lifecycle* (2010).
**Domain:** User research synthesis, interaction design, persona practice

## Core Concepts

- **A persona is a model of a behaviour pattern, not a portrait of a person.** Cooper introduced personas in *Inmates* as a fix for software designed by and for engineers (the "inmates" of the title). His argument: a team that designs for "the user" designs for nobody, because "the user" stretches to fit whatever the speaker wants. A persona is a precise, named description of one *kind* of user, with goals and behaviours, that the whole team can design for and argue about. In *About Face* the definition is tightened. A persona is a composite archetype synthesised from patterns observed across real users in research. It is not one real customer, and not an average of all of them. `user-persona/SKILL.md:27-36` already says "composite archetype of a real *type* of user", which is Cooper's definition. What the skill lacks is the *method* that turns research into that archetype (behavioural variables, below). Without it an agent has nothing to synthesise and fills the fields with plausible guesses.

- **The elastic user.** Cooper's name for the failure personas exist to prevent. When a team says "the user", each person pictures someone different: a novice when arguing for a wizard, an expert when arguing for keyboard shortcuts. The user bends to every argument, so no argument can be settled. A specific persona with stated goals and skills cannot be stretched that way. "Would *this* person do that?" has an answer. This is the strongest reason a persona needs a name and specific attributes, and the reason a user story's role should be a persona, not "a user" (`SKILL.md:112-113` makes the second point; it does not name the elastic user).

- **Goals, not tasks, are what a persona is built around.** Cooper separates goals (what a person wants to achieve or how they want to feel, which changes slowly) from tasks (the steps they take today with today's tools, which change whenever the technology does). Design for tasks and you automate the current workaround. Design for goals and you can remove steps entirely. *Inmates* gave a first taxonomy: personal goals (for example, not feeling stupid, not making mistakes), corporate goals, practical goals, and "false goals" that are really tasks or technology choices (for example, "save memory" or "use a database"). *About Face* replaced this with the three-level model that is now standard:
  - **End goals:** what the person wants to get done by using the product. These drive the product's functions and its flows. They are the goals closest to a job to be done.
  - **Experience goals:** how the person wants to feel while using it: in control, not stupid, confident, not overwhelmed. These drive the interaction and visual design and often become usability NFRs.
  - **Life goals:** who the person wants to be, beyond the product: for example, to be recognised as the person who keeps the company safe. These explain long-term loyalty and messaging, and rarely drive a feature directly.

  The skill has one "Goals" field with primary, secondary and success metric (`SKILL.md:73-74`, `references/persona-fields-and-grounding.md:105-118`). The success metric ("what gets them recognized or promoted") is really a life goal, and nothing captures experience goals at all, even though the skill's own Adoption barrier field (`references/persona-fields-and-grounding.md:162-165`) is usually a violated experience goal.

- **Behavioural variables: how research becomes a persona.** *About Face* gives a concrete synthesis procedure. As I recall it, in its third and fourth editions it runs in seven steps:
  1. **Identify behavioural variables** from the interviews, grouped by role. The book's categories are *activities* (what they do, how often, how much), *attitudes* (how they think about the domain and the technology), *aptitudes* (education, training, ability to learn), *motivations* (why they do it), and *skills* (domain and technical capability). Each variable is a range with two ends, for example "trusts automated classification ↔ verifies every result by hand".
  2. **Map each interviewee onto each variable**, placing them along the range. Precision of relative position matters more than exact measurement.
  3. **Identify significant behaviour patterns**: clusters of the same interviewees appearing together on several variables (the book suggests looking for groups that cluster on roughly six to eight variables, a heuristic I recall but would not cite as a figure). Each cluster is a candidate persona. The pattern must make logical sense: the clustered variables should explain each other.
  4. **Synthesise characteristics and relevant goals** for each pattern, drawn from the data.
  5. **Check for completeness and redundancy.** Any gap in the mapping means a missing persona, so research more. Two personas that differ only in demographics, or in one variable, are merged.
  6. **Develop a narrative**, a short description of the persona's day and context, written to explain behaviour, not to entertain.
  7. **Designate persona types** (below).

  This is the load-bearing missing piece in the current skill. `SKILL.md:136-150` goes straight from "pull the ICP" to "draft each field". The behavioural-variable step is what stops personas from being role titles with adjectives. It is also the step that makes a persona *checkable*: a reviewer can see which observed people sit behind which pattern.

- **Persona types, and designing for one primary.** *About Face* designates each persona as one of six types:
  - **Primary:** the main design target. Their needs cannot be fully met by an interface built for anyone else. Cooper's rule is that each *interface* has exactly one primary persona; if a product genuinely has two primaries, it probably needs two interfaces (or two distinct surfaces). If you find more than about three primaries, the problem is scoped too broadly.
  - **Secondary:** mostly satisfied by the primary's interface, plus a few specific additions. You design for the primary first and accommodate secondaries without compromising the primary.
  - **Supplemental:** users whose needs are fully met by the primary's design. They are listed so the team knows they were considered.
  - **Customer:** the buyer, who is not necessarily a user. Their needs go into the purchase, administration and trust story, not the daily interface.
  - **Served:** people who never touch the product but are affected by its use. The book's example is a patient under a radiology machine. For a data-governance product, the data subjects whose records are scanned would be served personas.
  - **Negative (anti-persona):** who the product is explicitly *not* for, named so scope creep can be resisted. Typical examples are the power user or the technology enthusiast who would pull the design toward complexity.

  "Design for one" is the core claim in *Inmates*. A design that tries to please everyone pleases no one. A design that delights one well-chosen primary persona also tends to satisfy many others. The skill has "PRIMARY | BUYER | CHAMPION" (`references/persona-template-and-examples.md:65`) and an anti-persona (`:70-71`). "Buyer" is Cooper's customer persona and "champion" is a secondary or supplemental. Both repo examples are marked PRIMARY (`:194-197`). By Cooper's rule that is only right if the Data Steward and the Compliance Officer each own a distinct surface (a scanning console and a reporting view). The file argues for two primaries (`:177-185`) without naming that condition.

- **Personas are distinct from roles, segments and profiles.** Cooper and Pruitt-Adlin both draw this line. A *user role* (Cohn's term in user-story practice) is a job title and a relationship to the system. A *market segment* is who buys, defined by demographics or firmographics. A *user profile* is a list of demographic traits. A persona is a behaviour pattern with goals. Two people with the same role can be different personas (one trusts automation, one does not), and one persona can cover several roles. The skill's "Segment / role" field (`SKILL.md:71-72`) blurs this: it puts the persona inside the ICP, which is correct, but treats the role as the persona's identity. `competing-against-luck-christensen.md` adds the JTBD side of the same boundary: the job defines the segment, the persona describes the person executing it. That file covers the job/persona boundary; it is not repeated here.

- **Provisional personas: personas without research, labelled as such.** Cooper's own position is that real personas come from ethnographic research. *About Face* allows **provisional personas** when research is impossible: built from the best available second-hand knowledge (support logs, domain experts, sales staff, market data), used to get started, and explicitly marked as hypotheses to be replaced. The same idea has several names, which an agent should recognise as one concept:
  - **Provisional persona** (Cooper, *About Face*).
  - **Ad hoc persona** (Don Norman's 2004 essay on ad hoc personas and empathetic focus, which argues that even assumption-based personas help a team keep a person in mind, provided nobody mistakes them for data).
  - **Assumption persona** (Adlin and Pruitt). They use these deliberately as a *first step*: the team writes down the personas it already believes in, so its assumptions are visible and can be tested, before real data is gathered.
  - **Proto-persona** (Jeff Gothelf, *Lean UX*): a quick, workshop-made sketch, validated afterwards.

  The skill's PROTO / RESEARCH honesty rule (`SKILL.md:40-61`) is sound and consistent with all four. What the sources add is that a provisional persona is *a hypothesis with a test attached*, not just a label. Adlin and Pruitt's assumption personas exist to be compared against data, and they should say what evidence would confirm or overturn them.

- **The Persona Lifecycle: personas as an organisational process, not a document.** Pruitt and Adlin's book (from their persona work at Microsoft; see also Pruitt and Grudin's 2003 paper "Personas: Practice and Theory") organises persona work in five phases, using a deliberately extended parenting metaphor:
  1. **Family planning:** decide whether the organisation is ready, who is on the persona team, what the personas are for, and which existing data sources exist. Assumption personas are often written here.
  2. **Conception and gestation:** turn data into personas. Their technique is to extract **factoids** (single facts, each tied to its source: an interview, a survey, a support ticket, a report), cluster them by affinity, build **skeletons** (short bullet sketches of candidate personas), prioritise the skeletons, then grow the chosen ones into full personas with a **foundation document** in which each statement carries a reference to the data behind it.
  3. **Birth and maturation:** introduce the personas to the organisation and make them understood: posters, summaries, persona-specific walkthroughs.
  4. **Adulthood:** use them in the work. Their key tool is the **persona-weighted feature matrix**: each candidate feature is scored for its value to each persona, and each persona carries a weight by importance, so feature priority comes out as a persona-weighted sum. Personas also drive scenarios, design reviews, and recruiting for usability tests.
  5. **Lifetime achievement, reuse and retirement:** assess whether the personas did their job, reuse them on the next product where the population is the same, and **retire** them when the product, the market or the evidence has moved on. A persona that nobody revisits is a liability: it keeps steering decisions with stale or never-validated facts.

  The skill's lifecycle is two states and one arrow (`references/persona-fields-and-grounding.md:62-75`, "start proto, validate to research"). It already tracks grounding per attribute (`:72-75`), which is Pruitt and Adlin's sourced-factoid idea in miniature. It has no adulthood (how personas are used to prioritise) and no retirement (when a persona is withdrawn).

- **The common failure modes.** Across Cooper and Pruitt-Adlin the recurring persona failures are:
  - **Demographic fluff.** Age, hobbies and a pet's name standing in for behaviour. The skill already bans this (`SKILL.md:84-90`, `references/persona-fields-and-grounding.md:175-195`).
  - **Stereotypes.** A persona assembled from the team's picture of "a typical X", which re-imports bias. Stock photos and invented names make this worse, because they look like evidence.
  - **Too many personas.** A cast of a dozen means the team designs for none of them. The fix is type designation: few primaries, the rest secondary or supplemental.
  - **The elastic user** (above), and its cousin the **self-referential user**: the team designs for itself and calls it a persona.
  - **Edge-case personas.** Designing the primary interface around a rare, extreme user. Edge cases must be handled, but they are not design targets.
  - **Personas that are not used.** Pruitt and Adlin's main finding: personas fail more often because they are made and then ignored than because they are badly made. They are not referenced in reviews, not tied to features, not maintained.
  - **Personas presented as research when they are not.** The failure the skill's honesty rule targets, and the one most likely when an AI agent writes the personas.

- **Name and image: where the sources and the skill differ.** Cooper and Pruitt-Adlin both recommend giving a persona a first name and a photograph, because a memorable person gets used in conversation and a set of fields does not. The skill bans the photo (`references/persona-fields-and-grounding.md:183`). For an artifact an AI agent writes, the ban is the better rule. A stock or generated face encodes a demographic stereotype and makes an assumption-based persona look like a real person. Keep the name, as Cooper intends, for the anti-elastic-user effect. Skip the image. Say in the skill that this is a deliberate departure from the sources, not an oversight.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Write the behavioural variables before writing any persona.** For each role, list five to ten variables as two-ended ranges under the five headings (activities, attitudes, aptitudes, motivations, skills). Place each interviewee on each range. Clusters across several variables are the personas. With no interviewees (the usual case for an agent), do the same exercise with *hypothesised* positions and say so. The table then becomes the persona hypothesis and the interview plan: each variable is a question to put to the first design partners.
- **Write a persona hypothesis before research, Cooper style.** Before interviews, write down which kinds of user you expect, which behavioural variables you expect to separate them, and therefore whom to recruit. This is the right first artifact for an agent with no user data. It tells the user (the only person who can hold the interviews, per W1-23) whom to talk to and what to listen for.
- **Split goals into end, experience and life goals.** End goals become functional requirements and the persona's job. Experience goals become usability NFRs and onboarding constraints ("must never make her feel the tool knows her data better than she does"). Life goals go to messaging (`gtm-strategy`) and are usually one line or omitted for a B2B tool.
- **Designate a type for every persona, and exactly one primary per interface.** If you want two primaries, name the two surfaces. If you cannot, pick one and demote the other to secondary. Add a customer persona when buyer and user differ, a served persona when the product acts on people who never use it (data subjects, patients, end customers of a B2B tool), and one negative persona.
- **Merge personas that differ only in demographics or role title.** Completeness-and-redundancy check from *About Face*: if two personas sit in the same place on most behavioural variables and share their goals, they are one persona.
- **Tag every statement with its source, Pruitt-Adlin style.** In the persona, each attribute carries a source tag: an interview reference ("DP-2, 2026-11-03"), a second-hand source ("support tickets", "the user's problem statement"), or "assumption". This generalises the skill's `(proto)` / `(research)` tags (`references/persona-template-and-examples.md:74-75`) into something a reviewer can check.
- **Label agent-written personas by provenance, not just by kind.** When an AI agent writes a persona with no research, the honest label is not just "proto". State three things at the top: *who wrote it* (the agent), *what it was inferred from* (the problem statement, the ICP, the stakeholder map, the user's own descriptions), and *that no user was interviewed*. A concrete form:
  `PROTO — assumption-based · written by requirements-analyst (AI) from the problem statement and ICP · no user research · validate before Design`.
  Pair it with **validation questions**: for each load-bearing attribute, the past-specific question that would confirm or overturn it, and what answer would mean the persona is wrong.
- **Score features per persona, weighted (scaled down for small products).** For standard and enterprise products, use a persona-weighted feature matrix as the input to MoSCoW: features × personas, each cell a value score, each persona a weight (the primary weighted highest). This is the "adulthood" use and keeps needs scored per persona rather than pooled, which matches the Olsen point already noted in the wave-1 sources list. For lean products, skip the matrix.
- **Give every persona set a review date and a retirement rule.** Record when the personas were last checked against evidence, what would trigger a re-check (new segment, a journey map that finds a job no persona covers, a design partner contradicting an attribute), and when a persona is retired (marked `retired` in the manifest, not silently deleted).

## Key Terminology

Worth considering for `skills/glossary-management/`, if adopted:

- **Persona**: a named, composite archetype of one behaviour pattern among real or hypothesised users, with goals. It is distinct from a user role, a market segment and a user profile.
- **Goal-Directed Design**: Cooper's design process (research, modelling, requirements, framework, refinement, support), which designs from user goals rather than tasks or features.
- **End goal / experience goal / life goal**: what the person wants to get done, how they want to feel while doing it, and who they want to be.
- **Behavioural variable**: a two-ended range of behaviour or attitude (activity, attitude, aptitude, motivation, skill) on which research participants are placed to find personas.
- **Elastic user**: an unspecified "user" who stretches to justify any design argument.
- **Primary / secondary / supplemental / customer / served / negative persona**: Cooper's six persona types, by role in the design.
- **Provisional persona** (also ad hoc, assumption or proto-persona): a persona built without direct user research, labelled as a hypothesis to be validated.
- **Persona hypothesis**: the pre-research statement of which user kinds and behavioural variables are expected, used to plan recruiting.
- **Factoid**: Pruitt and Adlin's single, sourced fact from a data source, the building block of a data-backed persona.
- **Persona-weighted feature matrix**: features scored for value to each persona, combined using persona weights.
- **Persona retirement**: withdrawing a persona that no longer matches the evidence, the product or the market.

## Caveats

- **Confident:** Cooper's origin of personas in *Inmates*, the elastic user, design for one, goals over tasks, the existence of personal / corporate / practical / false goals in *Inmates*, and the end / experience / life goal model in *About Face*. Also confident: behavioural variables and the mapping-and-pattern procedure, the six persona types and the one-primary-per-interface rule, provisional personas, and the general Goal-Directed Design phase sequence. For *The Persona Lifecycle*: the five lifecycle phases and their parenting names, factoids, skeletons, foundation documents, the persona-weighted feature matrix, assumption personas, and the argument that personas fail most often by not being used.
- **Less certain:** the exact number and order of *About Face*'s persona-creation steps (I give seven as I recall from the third and fourth editions; earlier editions differ), the "six to eight variables" clustering heuristic, any recommended number of interviews per role, and the "more than about three primaries" threshold, which I recall as a rule of thumb rather than a firm rule. I am not sure which edition first introduced the served persona, or whether *Inmates* or *About Face* first uses "persona hypothesis". The assumption-persona technique is clearly Adlin and Pruitt's, but I am not certain whether it is developed most fully in the 2006 book or the 2010 *Essential* version. The specific example personas in *Inmates* are deliberately not named here.
- **Other names are credited from memory.** Don Norman's "ad hoc personas" essay (2004) and Jeff Gothelf's proto-personas (*Lean UX*) are cited for the terms, not as research files in this repo. Neither is a `sources:` entry until it has its own file.
- **No page numbers, quotes or statistics are given, deliberately.** None should be added to the skill from this file without the books in hand.
- **Persona practice has a critical literature** (for example, Chapman and Milham's 2006 argument that personas cannot be verified, and later studies of how practitioners actually use them). I know these only in outline. They support this file's main practical point rather than undercutting it: a persona is only as good as its evidence, so the label and source tags matter more than the prose.
- **Cooper assumes research exists.** His method is built on ethnographic interviews by trained designers. In this plugin an AI agent writes the personas and usually has no users to talk to. The provisional-persona route, the provenance label and the validation questions are this file's adaptation of the sources to that situation, not something the books prescribe for AI authors.

# The Challenger Customer (with The Challenger Sale) and the Stakeholder-Analysis Toolkit
**Authors:** Brent Adamson, Matthew Dixon, Pat Spenner and Nick Toman (*The Challenger Customer*, 2015); Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson (*The Challenger Sale*, 2011). The supporting tools are from other sources: Aubrey Mendelow (power/interest grid), Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann (*Making Strategy*, 1998), Ronald Mitchell, Bradley Agle and Donna Wood (stakeholder salience, 1997), and RACI, which comes from practice with no single author.
**Domain:** B2B buying-group dynamics, commercial insight, and stakeholder analysis

This file has two jobs. First, it is the source for the **customer side** of stakeholder mapping: how a B2B buying committee behaves and who in it can actually move a decision. Second, it gives a source to the **delivery side** tools that `stakeholder-mapping` already teaches without one: the power/interest grid, salience and RACI. Every section below says which source each idea comes from. The Challenger books say nothing about Mendelow, salience or RACI, and those sources say nothing about buying committees.

## Core Concepts

### From *The Challenger Customer* (and *The Challenger Sale* where noted)

- **The consensus problem.** *(Challenger Customer.)* B2B purchases are no longer decided by one buyer. They are decided by a buying group that spans functions (finance, IT, security, legal, operations, the user team). Each member brings different priorities, measures and fears. The authors' central claim is that the more people involved, the less likely the group is to buy at all. Deals rarely die because a competitor wins. They stall, because the group cannot agree on what the problem is and drifts back to the **status quo**. The real competitor in a complex B2B deal is often "no decision".
- **Individual priorities override shared ones.** *(Challenger Customer.)* Inside the group, each person weighs what the purchase means for them personally (risk to their reputation, more work for their team) as heavily as what it means for the company. The consensus a supplier hopes for is therefore hard to reach. The book warns against settling for a weak lowest-common-denominator agreement: the group agrees to the thing nobody objects to, which is usually the cheapest, least different option, or nothing.
- **Seven customer stakeholder profiles.** *(Challenger Customer.)* The book sorts the people a supplier meets inside a customer into seven profiles in three groups. The profiles describe **attitude toward change and behaviour inside the organisation**, not job title. A CISO can be any of them.
  - **Mobilizers** (they can move the organisation to act):
    - **Go-Getter**: driven by results and getting things done; drawn to any good idea that improves the organisation; asks "how do we make this happen?"
    - **Teacher**: driven by ideas and insight; likes to share them with colleagues and persuade them; a natural carrier of a new way of thinking.
    - **Skeptic**: cautious and hard to persuade; tests every claim and wants the details. Hard to win, but once convinced, very influential, because colleagues trust that the Skeptic checked.
  - **Talkers** (easy to reach and willing to talk, but they do not build consensus):
    - **Guide**: shares inside information freely (who decides, what the politics are), but cannot move the organisation.
    - **Friend**: accessible, likes the supplier, will take meetings; often the person sellers mistake for a champion.
    - **Climber**: focused on personal gain; supports what makes them look good and is not trusted to put the company first.
  - **Blocker**: defends the status quo; has little interest in change and may actively resist it. The book's advice is broadly not to invest in converting Blockers, and to let a Mobilizer deal with them inside the organisation.
- **Target Mobilizers, not Talkers.** *(Challenger Customer.)* Suppliers naturally spend their time with Talkers, because Talkers are friendly and available. A good relationship with a Friend or Guide feels like progress and does not produce a decision. The book's prescription: find the Mobilizers and equip them. The test that separates the two groups is how a person reacts to a challenging insight. Talkers ask what it means for them. Mobilizers ask what it means for the company and want to act on it, or (Skeptics) want to pressure-test it.
- **Commercial teaching.** *(The Challenger Sale, carried into The Challenger Customer.)* The earlier book argues that the best-performing B2B sellers **teach** customers something new about their own business, **tailor** the message to each stakeholder's priorities, and **take control** of the conversation, including money and next steps. Teaching is not product education. It is a reframe: an insight about a cost, risk or opportunity the customer has underestimated, which leads naturally, but not obviously, to something the supplier does uniquely well. *The Challenger Sale* gives the reframe a sequence: warm up with the customer's known challenges, reframe them, show the cost of the current way with evidence, make it personal, then describe a new way of working, and only then the solution.
- **Unteaching.** *(Challenger Customer.)* Customers do much of their learning before they talk to a supplier. They arrive with a picture of the problem and of what a solution looks like, often formed from generic content or from a competitor. So a supplier first has to **unteach**: disrupt the existing mental model before teaching a new one. Content that agrees with what the customer already believes ("thought leadership" that confirms) does not change a decision. Content that shows them something they got wrong does.
- **Collective learning.** *(Challenger Customer.)* Since the obstacle is group disagreement, the book argues for getting the buying group to agree on the **problem** before anyone argues about the **solution**. The supplier helps the Mobilizer bring the group together around a shared understanding of the cost of the status quo. If the members first meet over competing vendor proposals, each defends their own priorities and the group stalls.
- **Make buying easier.** *(Challenger Customer; also the authors' related work on the "consensus sale".)* The group often does not know how to buy something new. A supplier that gives the Mobilizer the material to win colleagues over (what each function will worry about, how to answer it, how others made the decision) reduces the work and risk of buying. My recollection is that the "prescriptive" framing (telling the customer how to buy, not offering endless options) is developed most fully in the authors' later articles. Treat that part as adjacent to the book, not as its core.
- **Marketing's role changes.** *(Challenger Customer.)* Since much learning happens before a seller is present, the book gives marketing the job of producing content that unteaches and equips Mobilizers early. For a product with no sales team (this repo's PLG default), that is where all the teaching has to happen: in the trial, the docs and the content.

### From the stakeholder-analysis sources (not from the Challenger books)

- **Mendelow's power/interest grid.** *(Mendelow, early 1980s and 1991; popularised in Johnson and Scholes' *Exploring Corporate Strategy*.)* Mendelow's original matrix related power to the **predictability** of stakeholders. The power/interest version is the one now taught everywhere. Two axes, four quadrants, one strategy each: **Key players** (high power, high interest): manage closely, involve in decisions. **Keep satisfied** (high power, low interest): meet their needs, do not over-communicate, and watch for their interest rising. **Keep informed** (low power, high interest): communicate regularly; they are often allies and early warnings. **Minimal effort / monitor** (low on both). The grid is qualitative: a stakeholder is high or low on each axis. A three-point score has no source.
- **Eden and Ackermann, *Making Strategy*.** *(1998.)* They renamed the quadrants by the stakeholder's role in strategy: **Players** (high power, high interest), **Subjects** (high interest, low power), **Context setters** (high power, low interest) and the **Crowd** (low on both). Two points matter most. First, the grid is a starting point for **acting on** stakeholders: who could be moved from Subject to Player by forming a coalition, and which Context setters must be kept from becoming hostile Players. Second, they extend the grid into influence networks (who influences whom), because a stakeholder's power often runs through someone else. John Bryson's later stakeholder-analysis guidance for public and non-profit strategy builds on this work.
- **Stakeholder salience.** *(Mitchell, Agle and Wood, Academy of Management Review, 1997.)* Who managers actually pay attention to depends on three attributes: **power** (can they impose their will), **legitimacy** (is their claim proper or recognised) and **urgency** (is their claim time-sensitive and critical to them). The combinations give seven classes. One attribute: **dormant** (power only), **discretionary** (legitimacy only), **demanding** (urgency only). Two attributes: **dominant** (power + legitimacy), **dangerous** (power + urgency), **dependent** (legitimacy + urgency). All three: **definitive**. Attributes are not fixed: a dormant stakeholder who gains urgency becomes dangerous. Salience adds the time dimension that a power/interest grid lacks. A CISO with power and legitimacy but no urgency is "dominant"; the day a deployment request lands, urgency appears and the CISO becomes "definitive".
- **RACI and its variants.** *(Practice; the origin is unclear and no single author is credited.)* A decision or task matrix: **Responsible** (does the work), **Accountable** (owns the outcome; exactly one per row), **Consulted** (two-way input before), **Informed** (one-way after). The skill's "exactly one A" rule is the standard rule. Common variants: **RASCI** adds **Support**; **RACI-VS** adds **Verify** and **Sign-off**; **CAIRO** adds **Out of the loop**; **DACI** (Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed) is used for decisions rather than tasks; Bain's **RAPID** (Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide) separates **Decide** from **Agree**, where Agree is a formal veto. Typical failures: too many Cs (everyone consulted, decisions slow), an R with no authority, and A at a level too senior to engage.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Split the map in two, by who you can actually engage.** *(This repo's synthesis of W1-27 and the sources above.)* The skill should produce:
  - **(a) Delivery stakeholders**: real, named roles whose cooperation the product needs to be built and launched: the product owner, design partners, at larger scale a security reviewer, finance approver, platform team, legal, support. Power/interest grid (Mendelow / Eden and Ackermann), an engagement plan with cadence, and RACI belong **here**, because these are people you can meet monthly.
  - **(b) Customer buying committee**: **archetypal roles** in the ICP company that take part in the purchase. These are hypotheses about a segment, not people. Do not give them a cadence or a newsletter. For each role record: what it must believe to say yes, its likely objection, the evidence that answers it (a proof point or artifact), and whether it is likely to act as a **Mobilizer**, a **Talker** or a **Blocker** (Challenger Customer). Salience (power, legitimacy, urgency) fits this side better than the grid, because buying triggers are urgency.
  - Beneficiaries and outside parties (auditors, regulators, channel partners) go in whichever list matches how they are engaged: a design-partner auditor who reviews evidence formats is delivery; "the auditor the customer will face" is part of the buying context.
- **Name a Mobilizer hypothesis and a Blocker per segment.** For each beachhead segment, state which committee role is most likely to carry the change (often a Go-Getter in the user team or a Teacher in compliance), and which role is most likely to defend the status quo. Remember the profiles are attitudes: name them as "likely", and say how Stage 1 design partners will confirm them.
- **Do not confuse the Friend with the champion.** In design-partner and beta work, the person who answers every email and loves the product may be a Friend. Test with an insight: does this person ask what it means for the company and take it to colleagues? If not, find who does.
- **Write the "no decision" alternative into the map and the GTM.** Treat the status quo as the main competitor in every B2B deal, consistent with Dunford's "do nothing" alternative (`obviously-awesome-dunford.md`). The buying-committee section states what each role loses by staying put.
- **Build a reframe before the message house.** *(Challenger Sale.)* Before the headline, write the one insight that unteaches the ICP's current view (for the first product: "your data inventory is out of date the day after the audit"). It must lead to a strength only this product has; if any competitor could deliver it, it is education, not commercial teaching.
- **Give the Mobilizer a consensus kit.** One page per committee role: the concern, the answer, the proof. For the first product: a security brief for the CISO, an ROI line for the CTO, a data-flow diagram for legal. The existing CISO mitigation in the skill already does this for one role; extend it to every role.
- **Fix the scale on the grid.** Use the grid as Mendelow drew it: high or low on each axis, with a short reason. If a score is kept, use 1–5 with 3 and above as "high", and say so (closes W1-28). Mark stakeholders whose position is moving with an arrow (Eden and Ackermann), and re-score at each phase gate.
- **Use salience to decide re-scoring.** On the delivery side, any stakeholder who gains **urgency** (a deadline, an incident, an audit) is re-scored immediately, not at the next gate.
- **Keep RACI for real decisions with real people.** Agents can be R. Only a person can be A. Use RAPID's "Agree" (a veto) when a compliance or security sign-off can block a release, so the veto is visible rather than hidden as a C.
- **Scale it.**
  - **Lean** (`phase-requirements.json`: stakeholder map and GTM both `skip`): no artifact. If a lean product has a buyer distinct from the user, one line in the vision or mission naming who decides is enough. Internal stakeholders are the product owner alone.
  - **Standard** (typically SMB B2B or a self-serve product): delivery list of two to five roles (product owner, design partners, perhaps one reviewer); a buying committee of two to four roles with one Mobilizer hypothesis and one Blocker; a RACI of three to five decisions. Self-serve does not remove the committee: it appears at procurement, when security and finance review a trial that a user started.
  - **Enterprise** (sales-led, regulated, multi-tenant): a buying committee per segment or tenant type, with Mobilizer and Blocker hypotheses, a collective-learning plan (how the group gets to agree on the problem), and a consensus kit per role; a delivery map with Eden and Ackermann's influence links between teams; salience for regulators and auditors; RACI or RAPID with exactly one A per decision.

## Key Terminology

Candidates for `glossary-management`; add an entry before any template uses them.

- **Buying committee** (or buying group): the customer-side people who take part in a B2B purchase decision. Distinct from delivery stakeholders.
- **Delivery stakeholders**: the people whose cooperation the product needs to be built and launched.
- **Mobilizer**: a customer stakeholder who can build consensus and move the organisation to act (Go-Getter, Teacher, Skeptic). Distinct from "champion", which only means someone who likes the product.
- **Talker**: a customer stakeholder who is accessible and informative but cannot build consensus (Guide, Friend, Climber).
- **Blocker** (Challenger sense): a stakeholder who defends the status quo. The skill already uses "blocker" for anyone whose objection can stop adoption; keep one definition and say which.
- **Commercial teaching / reframe**: an insight that changes how the customer sees their own problem and leads to the supplier's unique strength.
- **Unteaching**: disrupting the mental model a customer formed before talking to a supplier.
- **Collective learning**: getting the buying group to agree on the problem before evaluating solutions.
- **No decision**: the buying outcome where the group returns to the status quo; the main competitor in complex B2B deals.
- **Power/interest grid**, **Key players / Players**, **Context setters**, **Subjects**, **Crowd**: Mendelow's grid and Eden and Ackermann's names for its quadrants.
- **Stakeholder salience**: the degree of attention a stakeholder gets, from power, legitimacy and urgency.
- **RACI / RAPID**: decision-rights matrices; RAPID's "Agree" is a formal veto.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** the consensus problem and the "no decision" outcome; the seven profiles and their grouping into Mobilizers (Go-Getter, Teacher, Skeptic), Talkers (Guide, Friend, Climber) and Blocker; the advice to target Mobilizers rather than Talkers; the idea of unteaching and of building agreement on the problem before the solution; *The Challenger Sale*'s teach / tailor / take control and the general reframe sequence. Mendelow's quadrant strategies; Eden and Ackermann's Players / Subjects / Context setters / Crowd; the three salience attributes and seven classes; RACI and its common variants including RAPID.
- **Less certain:** the exact names of the steps in *The Challenger Sale*'s pitch sequence (I give them in paraphrase); exactly how much of "making buying easier" and the "prescriptive" approach is in *The Challenger Customer* rather than the authors' later Harvard Business Review articles and Gartner's later buyer-enablement work; the exact boundary between Mendelow's 1981 and 1991 work; who first wrote RACI.
- **No statistics.** Both Challenger books rest on CEB (now Gartner) survey research and quote figures about buying-group size, how far through the purchase customers are before they contact a supplier, and how often each seller profile wins. None are reproduced here. If the skill needs a figure, it must be checked against the text. No page numbers or direct quotations are given, deliberately.
- **The Challenger books are written for sales teams.** They assume sellers meeting buyers in long, complex B2B deals. This repo's default motion is self-serve with a guided POC, run by a solo product owner. What transfers is the **map** (who decides, who can mobilise, who blocks, what "no decision" costs) and the **content** (a reframe and a consensus kit delivered through the trial and docs). What does not transfer at lean or standard scale is the field-sales choreography.
- **Profiles are attitudes, not titles.** The skill must not assign "CISO = Blocker" as fact. In a Strategy-phase artifact the profiles are hypotheses about a segment, confirmed only by real conversations with design partners. The agent cannot hold those conversations; the user does (consistent with W1-23).
- **The salience and grid sources are academic and older.** Mendelow and Mitchell et al. are management-theory sources, not product guides. They are well established and widely taught, but the per-quadrant cadences in the skill (weekly, monthly, quarterly) are this repo's conventions, not theirs.
- **Overlap with sibling files.** `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` holds the economic buyer vs technical champion split and the pragmatist buyer's need for safety; this file adds the group dynamics around that buyer. `obviously-awesome-dunford.md` holds "do nothing" as a competitive alternative; this file explains why it wins so often in group decisions. `product-roadmaps-relaunched-lombardo.md` holds shuttle diplomacy and nemawashi; those are the **internal** version of collective learning (agree the direction one person at a time before the meeting) and apply to delivery stakeholders, while collective learning applies to the customer's committee. `loved-lauchengco.md` holds the message house and sales enablement, where the reframe and consensus kit would sit.

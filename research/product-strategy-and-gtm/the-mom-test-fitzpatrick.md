# The Mom Test: How to Talk to Customers and Learn if Your Business is a Good Idea When Everyone is Lying to You
**Authors:** Rob Fitzpatrick
**Domain:** Product Strategy and Go-to-Market — Customer Discovery Interview Technique

## Core Concepts

- **The Mom Test is a three-rule test for whether a question can produce honest data, named for the fact that even your own mother — who loves you and wants to protect your feelings — will lie to you if you ask her badly.** If you ask your mom "Do you think it's a good idea for me to quit my job and start a business selling artisanal dog food?", she will say yes, because the question is really "please tell me my idea is good," not a question about her life. The three rules: **(1) talk about their life instead of your idea** — the conversation should be about the other person's world, not a pitch or a validation request for yours; **(2) ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future** — "what did you do last time this happened" beats "what do you usually do" beats "what would you do if"; **(3) talk less, listen more** — the interviewer's job is to extract facts, not to explain, defend, or sell. A question that survives all three passes the Mom Test; a question that fails any one of them produces data that feels like validation but is not. This is the single organizing idea of the entire book — every other technique in it is a corollary or an application of these three rules.
- **The core failure mode the book exists to prevent: people are reflexively nice, and "good" data is easy to mistake for "nice" data.** Fitzpatrick's target failure is not "customers lie maliciously" — it's that ordinary social politeness generates false positives that feel exactly like product validation. "This is a great idea," "I would definitely use that," "You should totally build this" are not signals of anything except that the person did not want the conversation to feel awkward. The danger is specifically that these responses are *emotionally satisfying* to hear — they feel like the thing a founder is looking for — which is why they are dangerous rather than merely useless: they actively reinforce the founder's own confirmation bias while providing zero predictive information about whether the person will ever actually pay for, adopt, or change behavior around the product.
- **Three types of bad data: compliments, fluff, and ideas — each with a distinct signature and a distinct fix.** **Compliments** ("I love it," "that's really cool," "nice work") are pure noise — praise offered to be polite, unconnected to any behavior. The fix is to not fish for them and to actively deflect them back into a real question ("What's the story with the last time you tried to solve that?"). **Fluff** is generic, unfalsifiable language about behavior — "I usually...", "I always...", "I would..." — statements phrased as fact but actually describing an imagined average or an imagined future rather than one specific real event. The fix is to press for a specific instance: "When's the last time that happened?" **Ideas** are unsolicited feature requests and solution suggestions ("You know what you should build...") — not worthless, but dangerous if taken at face value, because a customer's proposed solution is evidence of a pain they're trying to solve, not a validated spec. The fix is to receive the idea, then dig behind it for the motivating problem ("What is it about your current process that made you think of that?") rather than logging it directly as a backlog item.
- **Pitching contaminates the conversation — "leading the witness."** The moment a founder describes their product or idea, the conversation's dynamics change: the other person now knows what answer would please the founder, and (per the compliments failure mode above) will often supply it, consciously or not. Fitzpatrick's guidance is to delay or avoid describing the product entirely for as long as possible during a discovery conversation, and to treat any early urge to explain "here's what we're building" as a signal that the conversation has stopped being about the customer's life and started being a pitch. This is not about withholding information out of secrecy — it's a controls-for-bias discipline, structurally identical to a blinded study: you cannot get an unbiased read on the customer's actual world once they know what answer you're hoping for.
- **Good questions surface facts about the past and present; bad questions ask for predictions, hypotheticals, or opinions about a future the person cannot actually see.** Canonical bad questions: "Would you buy a product that did X?", "How much would you pay for X?", "Do you think this is a good idea?" — all three ask the person to simulate a future decision under conditions (no real money changing hands, no real switching cost, no real competing priority) that make the answer unfalsifiable and reliably over-optimistic. Canonical good questions: "Talk me through the last time that happened," "What have you already tried?", "Why didn't that work?", "What else have you tried?", "Where does the money for that currently come from?" (a specific, past/present-tense version of the pricing question), "Who else should I be talking to about this?" The pattern across every good question: it can be answered by describing something that already happened, not by imagining something that hasn't.
- **Real validation is a commitment, not enthusiasm — advancement, not applause.** A conversation that ends in compliments has produced nothing measurable. A conversation that ends in a concrete next step — the person agrees to a follow-up meeting on their calendar, gives a warm introduction to their boss or a peer, pays a deposit, agrees to a pilot with a defined start date, or gives real access (their calendar, their data, their time) — has produced a costly, self-interested signal that is much harder to fake out of politeness. Fitzpatrick's advice is to always be asking for some form of commitment or advancement by the end of a substantive conversation, and to treat a conversation that produces neither useful facts nor an advancement as a conversation that has taught you very little, however pleasant it felt.
- **Ask the questions whose answers you're afraid to hear.** A recurring diagnostic in the book: if there's a question you're avoiding because you're worried the answer will deflate your idea (e.g., "Is this actually a priority for you right now?", "What have you budgeted for solving this?", "Who has to approve a purchase like this?"), that avoidance is itself a signal that you already suspect the honest answer is bad news — and it is precisely the question you most need to ask. Founders who only ask questions they're confident will get a comfortable answer are running a confirmation exercise, not a discovery process.
- **Keep it casual — the framing of the conversation shapes how honest the answers are.** A conversation explicitly billed as a formal "meeting" to "get feedback on our product" primes the other person to be diplomatic, evaluative, and polite — the exact posture the Mom Test is designed to avoid. A casual conversation framed around their world and their problem ("I'm trying to understand how teams like yours currently handle X") produces more candid, specific, past-tense answers. This is a framing discipline layered on top of the three core rules, not a fourth rule — it's about how the invitation to talk is worded, which sets expectations before the first real question is even asked.
- **Choosing who to talk to is itself part of the discipline.** Not every willing conversation is equally valuable. Fitzpatrick favors talking to people who visibly, urgently have the problem — evidenced by the fact that they have already spent time, money, or organizational capital trying to solve it themselves (a spreadsheet, a manual workaround, a consultant, a competing tool) — over people who politely agree the problem "sounds important" in the abstract. Prior, self-funded effort to solve the problem is itself a Mom-Test-compliant data point (a specific past action) that is far more predictive than a stated opinion about how important the problem is.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Run every planned discovery question through the three-part Mom Test filter before asking it out loud:** does it talk about their life (not your idea)? Does it ask about a specific past event (not a generic habit or a future hypothetical)? Will you be listening more than you talk while asking it? A question that fails any leg gets rewritten before the conversation, not diagnosed as bad after the fact.
- **Replace every "would you..." / "do you think..." / "how much would you pay..." question in an interview guide with its past-tense, specific-instance equivalent** — "would you use a tool that does X" becomes "walk me through the last time you needed to do X — what did you actually do?"; "how much would you pay for X" becomes "what are you spending today to deal with X, and where does that money come from?"
- **When a compliment lands mid-conversation ("this sounds great"), redirect immediately rather than accepting it as data** — a scripted redirect such as "Glad it's resonating — can you walk me through the last time this specific problem came up for you?" converts a compliment into another chance at a specific-instance answer instead of letting the conversation end on applause.
- **When an unsolicited feature idea lands ("you should build a dashboard for this"), do not log it verbatim as a requirement** — ask "what's the problem you're picturing that dashboard solving?" and record the underlying problem statement, not the proposed solution; this is the direct interview-time discipline that feeds `jtbd-analysis`'s existing "solution-shaped jobs" anti-pattern rather than letting the raw feature request reach that stage already contaminated.
- **End every substantive discovery conversation with an explicit ask for advancement**, scaled to the relationship and the stage: a specific follow-up on the calendar, an introduction to another named stakeholder (a buyer, a champion, a peer team), access to something real (a workflow walkthrough, a sample of their current spreadsheet, their calendar for a pilot), or — once the conversation warrants it — a deposit or signed pilot agreement. Treat a conversation that produces zero commitment as inconclusive, not as validation by omission.
- **Before scheduling a discovery conversation, write down which question you are most afraid to ask** — typically a question about priority, budget, or approval authority — and make sure it is on the list; if it never gets asked across a whole batch of conversations, treat that omission itself as a signal worth investigating.
- **Screen candidates for discovery conversations by evidence of prior self-funded effort to solve the problem** (a workaround, a spreadsheet, a consultant engaged, a competing tool trialled), not solely by role-title or company-profile fit — role and company fit describe who *should* care; prior effort is evidence they actually *do*.

- **Candidate new skill: `customer-discovery-interviewing`** (phase: ideate, owner: requirements-analyst, used before or alongside `user-persona` and `jtbd-analysis`, and referenced by `gtm-strategy`'s Stage 1 design-partner motion). This clears the bar the sibling files in this cluster have consistently applied for promoting a concept to its own skill: it has its own elicitation method (the three-rule filter; past-tense-instance rewrites of common bad questions), its own artifact (an interview question bank plus a live "compliment/fluff/idea" catch-and-redirect script), and its own quality criteria (a question passes the Mom Test or it doesn't; a conversation ends in facts-and-commitment or it doesn't) distinct from what `user-persona` or `jtbd-analysis` already own. Critically, this is not a restatement of either of those skills: both `user-persona` and `jtbd-analysis` specify *what* gets recorded once good data exists (attributes, job stories); this candidate skill specifies *how to run the conversation that produces trustworthy raw data in the first place* — a genuinely upstream, currently-unaddressed concern, and the one this repo's actual Stage 1 closed-beta motion needs most given its small, relationship-warm design-partner group.
- **Not proposed as a new agent.** Interview-question discipline is knowledge — a filter, a set of rewrite patterns, a catch-and-redirect script — not reasoning distinct from what `requirements-analyst` already performs when producing personas and job stories; it belongs in a skill the existing agent consumes, consistent with every other book in this cluster's conclusion on this point (Moore, Dunford, Olsen, and Lauchengco's research files all reach the same "skill, not agent" conclusion for their own new-content proposals).
- **Not proposed as an addition inside `gtm-strategy` itself.** Unlike Moore's beachhead-selection worksheet (a strategy-level scoring step that fits naturally as a new sub-step inside `gtm-strategy`'s existing component sequence), the Mom Test's content is a conversational micro-skill applied inside individual customer interactions across multiple phases and skills (design-partner conversations for `gtm-strategy` Stage 1, persona-building interviews for `user-persona`, job-story elicitation for `jtbd-analysis`) — it is reused by name across three different owning artifacts rather than belonging to any single one of them, which is exactly the shape of a standalone, cross-cutting skill rather than a component bolted onto one.

## Key Terminology

Worth considering for `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language, if this cluster's concepts are adopted into a skill:

- **The Mom Test** — the three-rule test for whether a customer-discovery question can produce honest data: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not generics or future opinions; talk less, listen more. Distinct from "customer interview" generically — it names a specific, falsifiable filter a question either passes or fails, not just the general practice of talking to customers.
- **Compliments, Fluff, and Ideas (the Three Types of Bad Data)** — Fitzpatrick's taxonomy of responses that feel like validation but carry no predictive signal: compliments (unearned praise), fluff (generic or future-tense claims about behavior), and ideas (unsolicited feature/solution suggestions taken at face value instead of being probed for the underlying problem). Distinct from `jtbd-analysis`'s "Backwards JTBD" anti-pattern, which catches contaminated *conclusions* after the fact rather than naming the *raw-data* failure modes that produce them.
- **Leading the Witness / Pitching** — describing your product or idea to the person you're interviewing before or during the conversation, which biases their subsequent answers toward what they believe will please you. The book's central "controls for bias" discipline.
- **Advancement / Commitment** — a concrete, costly next step (a scheduled follow-up, a warm introduction, a deposit, real access) offered by the person being interviewed, used as the actual unit of validation in place of stated enthusiasm. Distinct from a "compliment," which costs the speaker nothing and predicts nothing.
- **Specific-Instance Question** — a question that can only be answered by describing something that has actually already happened ("what did you do the last time..."), as opposed to a generic-habit question ("what do you usually do...") or a hypothetical-future question ("what would you do if..."). The core mechanical unit of Rule 2 of the Mom Test.

## Caveats

- **This book is narrower and more purely tactical than every other book in this cluster, and that narrowness is the point — do not expand its scope to compete with them.** Moore (segment sequencing), Dunford (positioning methodology), Olsen (needs discovery and prioritization), and Lauchengco (product marketing as an ongoing organizational function) are all strategy- or process-level frameworks that presuppose customer conversations are already happening and producing trustworthy data. Fitzpatrick's book operates one level below all four: it is exclusively about the mechanics of a single conversation — what to ask, what to ignore, how to redirect — and says nothing about market segmentation, positioning, pricing, or launch sequencing. It should be read as a technique that improves the *input quality* feeding every one of the other three books' processes, not as a competing or overlapping strategic framework.
- **Precise distinction from Olsen's Opportunity Score (`lean-product-playbook-olsen.md`) — these are not two flavors of the same "talk to customers" advice, and treating them as interchangeable would be a mistake.** Olsen's Opportunity Score is a *quantitative aggregation* technique: it assumes you already have (or can survey) a reasonably sized pool of respondents, asks each of them to rate a candidate need on two 1–10 scales (importance, satisfaction), and combines those numeric ratings across the pool into a ranked list via `Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)`. It answers "given honest signal from many people, which needs matter most?" — a ranking and prioritization problem that operates *after* individual data points exist and *assumes those data points are truthful*. Fitzpatrick's Mom Test is a *qualitative, single-conversation* discipline that has no numeric scale, no aggregation step, and no minimum sample size — it answers a prior, different question: "how do you ask any one question, to any one person, in a way that doesn't produce a false-positive answer in the first place?" A design partner could rate a need 9/10 importance on an Opportunity Score survey purely out of politeness toward a founder they already like — the survey's math would faithfully aggregate that number, but the number itself would be exactly the kind of compliment-shaped bad data the Mom Test exists to prevent. In this repo's actual context — a 3–5-person closed-beta design-partner group per `gtm-strategy`'s Stage 1, far below Olsen's own recommended survey sample size (already flagged as a scaling concern in `lean-product-playbook-olsen.md`'s Caveats) — the two techniques are complementary in a specific, sequential way: the Mom Test's interview discipline is what should govern *how* any structured importance/satisfaction scoring conversation with a design partner is conducted, because at n=3–5 there is no large-sample averaging to dilute one badly-elicited, politeness-driven rating. Fitzpatrick's technique is a precondition for Olsen's technique producing trustworthy numbers at this repo's actual scale — not a rival method solving the same problem.
- **The book's worked examples are Fitzpatrick's own consulting and accelerator practice — mostly early-stage, often consumer-facing or small-team B2B startups — not enterprise SaaS sales processes.** Its core three-rule discipline (talk about their life, ask about the past, listen more) is stage- and market-agnostic and applies without modification to this repo's B2B, private-deployment, compliance-focused first product. Some of the book's more tactical advice on *finding* conversations (cold outreach scripts, warm-intro chains through personal networks) is written for a founder with no existing customer base and translates less directly to this repo's context, where `gtm-strategy`'s Stage 1 design partners are explicitly "customers who helped shape requirements" — i.e., already-identified, already-warm relationships. The finding-conversations chapters are the least load-bearing part of the book for this repo; the question-discipline chapters are the most.
- **The book's later chapters on running discovery as a team-wide organizational habit (assigning "customer conversation" ownership across a founding team, weekly review rituals for what was learned) assume a multi-person team coordinating discovery work.** Per this repo's solo-operator, single-agent architecture (the same structural point `loved-lauchengco.md` makes about product marketing as a function), there is no separate team to coordinate — any adopted content should be scaled down to a checklist the `requirements-analyst` agent applies on Shafi's behalf during actual design-partner conversations, not imported as a team-process framework.
- **Confidence level on this summary is high.** The Mom Test is a short, famously focused, single-thesis book (its three-rule test is its entire spine, restated and applied rather than expanded into competing frameworks), and its content is unusually well-known and consistently summarized across startup/product-management practice — this file's Core Concepts are drawn from strong, confident general knowledge of the book's actual, well-documented content rather than from an uncertain reconstruction.

# Competing Against Luck: The Story of Innovation and Customer Choice
**Authors:** Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, David S. Duncan (2016). Read alongside the other main Jobs to Be Done schools: Anthony Ulwick, *Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice* (2016; also *What Customers Want*, 2005) and Outcome-Driven Innovation; Alan Klement, *When Coffee and Kale Compete* (2016), with the job-story format; and Bob Moesta's switch interview and forces of progress (Moesta and Chris Spiek of the Re-Wired Group; Moesta's later *Demand-Side Sales 101*, 2020).
**Domain:** Customer motivation, needs discovery, innovation theory

## Core Concepts

- **A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance.** This is the book's definition and its spine. People do not buy products. They "hire" them to make progress, and they "fire" whatever they were using before. Three parts of the definition carry the weight. *Progress* means movement toward a goal, not a task. *Circumstance* means the who, where, when and "while doing what" of the moment the need arises. The authors argue that the circumstance, not the customer's attributes, is the causal unit. A product attribute or a customer demographic can correlate with a purchase, but it does not cause one; the circumstance does. The famous illustration is the milkshake study. A fast-food chain made its milkshakes better along the dimensions customers named, and sales did not move. When the researchers asked what job the morning milkshake was hired for, the answer was "make a long, boring commute bearable and keep me full until lunch, one-handed." The competitors were bagels, bananas and boredom, not other milkshakes. `jtbd-analysis/SKILL.md:27-33` already teaches "progress in a situation" and "hire", and puts the job ahead of the solution. What it does not teach is *why* circumstance beats attributes. That is the argument a persona-first team most needs to hear.

- **Every job has functional, social and emotional dimensions, and the full "job spec" also covers the obstacles and the experience.** The book is firm that a job is never purely functional. In its fuller form, which it calls a job spec, the job is described by its functional, social and emotional dimensions, by the obstacles that get in the way of progress, by what the customer would have to give up to switch, and by the *experiences* of buying and using the product that would satisfy it. The last point matters most for design. The authors argue that the winning products are not those with the best features but those that *integrate around the job*: they deliver the right experience at each point where the customer buys and uses them, sometimes in ways that have nothing to do with the core product. The skill teaches the three dimensions well (`jtbd-analysis/SKILL.md:67-81`, `references/job-statements-and-dimensions.md:107-124`), and it is right that emotional and social pull are often decisive for a compliance buyer. Obstacles, trade-offs and purchase/use experiences are missing. They are the part of the spec that turns into onboarding, trust and support requirements.

- **Hiring and firing: every hire is also a firing, and the real competition is whatever gets fired.** Because a new hire displaces something, the competitive set is defined by the job, not by the product category. That set often includes doing nothing, a workaround, a spreadsheet, a consultant, or a product from a completely different category. The book separates the **Big Hire** (the purchase decision) from the **Little Hire** (each moment the product is actually used). A product can win the Big Hire and still lose because no Little Hires follow. Shelfware is the enterprise version of this. This links straight to Dunford's "competitive alternatives" (`obviously-awesome-dunford.md`). Dunford asks what customers would use if your product did not exist. JTBD explains why those alternatives are the right comparison set.

- **Non-consumption is often the biggest market.** Carried over from Christensen's disruption work (*The Innovator's Solution*): the largest opportunity is often people who are not consuming any solution, because what exists is too expensive, too complicated or too inaccessible for their circumstance. For a small-business compliance product, the incumbent is frequently "nobody does this until the auditor asks." That is non-consumption, and it means the job has to be framed around the moment the need turns urgent, not around a steady-state workflow.

- **Data fallacies: why companies lose sight of the job.** The book has a section (from memory, roughly a chapter) on how organizations drift away from jobs as they grow. Three of its fallacies are worth keeping. *Active versus passive data*: the job is vivid at founding and fades into dashboards later. *Surface growth*: chasing adjacent sales that serve other jobs. *Conforming data*: collecting the data that confirms the existing plan. These match the skill's "Backwards JTBD" anti-pattern (`jtbd-analysis/SKILL.md:167-171`) closely, and they give it a named source.

- **Ulwick's school: Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) treats the job as a process that can be measured.** Ulwick (Strategyn) defines the job as a stable functional process that a *job executor* is trying to get done. He breaks it down in four steps:
  - **The job statement**: *verb + object of the verb + contextual clarifier*, for example "manage a data estate's compliance posture across cloud storage". It must be solution-free and stable over time.
  - **The job map**: a universal eight-step template (define, locate, prepare, confirm, execute, monitor, modify, conclude) used to list every step of the job.
  - **Desired-outcome statements**: the customer's success metrics for each step, written in a fixed syntax, *direction + metric + object of control + contextual clarifier*. Example: "Minimize the time it takes to confirm that a newly connected source has been fully classified." A typical job yields many of these, often around a hundred.
  - **Opportunity scoring**: a quantitative survey asks a large sample how important each outcome is and how satisfied they are with how it is met today. The scores go into the opportunity algorithm `Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)` to find underserved and overserved outcomes. Ulwick then segments the market by *unmet outcomes*, not by demographics.

  Ulwick also separates jobs into the core functional job, related jobs, emotional jobs (personal and social), consumption-chain jobs (buy, install, learn, maintain, upgrade, dispose), and the purchase decision-maker's financial job. He separates the people too: job executor, job beneficiary and buyer. The skill's job types table (`references/job-statements-and-dimensions.md:91-103`) and its core-job shorthand `[verb] + [object] + [contextual clarifier]` (`SKILL.md:126-127`, `references/...:78-87`) are Ulwick's, uncredited. The Opportunity Score section (`references/...:150-187`) credits Olsen "building on" Ulwick, but it scores *jobs*. ODI scores *outcomes within a job*. A job-level score of "9 + (9−3) = 15" (`references/...:210`) is a category error by ODI's own rules.

- **Klement's school: the job is the customer's wish for a better self, and the job story is its format.** Klement frames a job as the progress a person wants in *their own life*, the "better me" they are trying to become. In his view the product and the activities around it are means, not the job. The job-story format, "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]", came out of Intercom around 2013 (Paul Adams's team). Klement became its best-known advocate and refined it, notably in replacing the user story's persona-led "As a…" with a situation-led "When…". Klement's main disagreement with Ulwick is about what a job *is*:
  - **Jobs as activities** (Ulwick): a functional process you map and measure.
  - **Jobs as progress** (Klement, closer to Christensen and Moesta): a desired change in the person's circumstance. Klement argues ODI is really task analysis, and that the same activity can serve very different jobs.

  Ulwick has replied in public that the progress framing is too vague to measure or act on. The two camps have argued in essays and blog posts for years. Neither side's view is settled, and the plugin does not need to take a side. It needs to stop merging the two silently, which is finding W1-21.

- **Moesta's forces of progress and the switch interview: how you find out what job was hired.** Moesta (who worked with Christensen on the milkshake study and is credited in *Competing Against Luck*) treats every switch as the result of four forces. Two drive change: the **push** of the current situation and the **pull** of the new solution. Two resist it: the **anxiety** of the new solution and the **habit** of the present. A switch happens only when push and pull outweigh anxiety and habit. The research method is the **switch interview**. You interview someone who *recently* switched (bought, or fired a product). You rebuild the timeline of that decision as a documentary-style reconstruction:
  - **First thought** (the struggling moment).
  - **Passive looking**, ended by a triggering event.
  - **Active looking**, ended by another event.
  - **Deciding**, then the **purchase**, then **first use** and ongoing use.

  The interviewer probes for the specific events, places, people and feelings at each step. The forces are already in the skill (`references/job-statements-and-dimensions.md:128-146`), with a correct table. The timeline and the switch interview itself are absent, and they are the missing *method* for gathering a forces analysis.

- **How the schools differ, and which to use when.**

  | School | Unit of analysis | Core artifact | Best when | Weak when |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Christensen / Moesta | The circumstance and the switch | Job spec; forces diagram; switch timeline | You need to know *why* people switch and what really competes. Early discovery, positioning, non-consumption | You need a ranked, quantified backlog |
  | Ulwick (ODI) | Measurable outcomes along a job's process | Job statement, job map, outcome statements, opportunity scores | A known job in a mature market with enough customers to survey; prioritising among many improvements | Few customers (the survey math needs a sample); new-category or emotional-progress jobs |
  | Klement / Intercom | The situation that triggers a motivation | Job stories | Turning a job into design and backlog input that stays situation-led; feeding user stories | Used alone, it drifts to feature-level stories with no anchoring job |

  These are complementary layers more than rival methods. Moesta's switch interview tells you which job and forces matter. Ulwick's job statement names the stable job, and his outcomes measure it. Klement's job stories carry particular situations into design. The skill already uses all three. It should say which is which and in what order.

- **JTBD and personas.** The JTBD camps all doubt persona-first thinking. Christensen argues customer attributes correlate with purchases but do not cause them. Intercom and Klement introduced job stories partly to get the persona out of the story's opening clause. Ulwick replaces personas with *job executors* and segments by unmet outcomes. None of them says "never write personas". The practical synthesis is to let the **job define the segment and the persona describe the person who executes it**. The persona answers *who* to recruit, what they use today and how they decide. The job answers *why* and *when*. The persona's Trigger, Current approach and Primary frustration fields are the forces of progress in persona form: trigger ≈ the struggling moment and push, current approach ≈ habit, adoption barrier ≈ anxiety.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Write the core job as a job statement first, then the job stories under it.** The job statement uses Ulwick's syntax, *verb + object + contextual clarifier*, is solution-free, and is a single line. Job stories use Klement's "When… I want to… so I can…" and are tied to particular situations. The job statement is the stable anchor. Each job story names one situation in which the job comes up. Label the two formats by name in the artifact so a reader never confuses them.
- **Test the altitude of a job statement with the "would it have existed fifty years ago?" question.** A true job (for example "be ready to show an auditor where regulated data lives") predates the technology. A statement that only makes sense with your product in the picture is a task or a feature.
- **Run a switch interview with someone who recently changed how they do the job.** Pick someone who bought a tool, hired a consultant, built a spreadsheet, or dropped a tool, recently enough to remember details. Reconstruct the timeline (first thought → passive looking → event → active looking → event → deciding → first use) and ask for the concrete details at each point: *where were you, who else was there, what had just happened, what did you try first*. This is the same past-specific discipline as *The Mom Test* (see `the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md`, not repeated here). The switch interview adds the *structure*: a fixed timeline and a target interviewee, the recent switcher.
- **Fill a four-forces table from the interview, one row per force, each cell quoting a specific event.** The design levers follow directly from the table. Anxiety is reduced with trust, spot-checks, reversibility and evidence of accuracy. Habit is displaced by importing the old spreadsheet or mirroring the familiar workflow. Push is amplified by naming the cost of the struggling moment. Pull is the only one of the four most teams work on.
- **List what gets fired, including non-consumption.** For each core job, record what the customer used before: nothing, a manual workaround, a spreadsheet, a consultant, an adjacent tool. Hand that list to `competitive-analysis` and `gtm-strategy` as the competitive set.
- **Separate the Big Hire from the Little Hire.** For each job, write one line on what wins the purchase and one on what brings the user back on an ordinary Tuesday. The second line usually becomes an onboarding requirement or a time-to-first-value requirement.
- **Use desired-outcome statements and opportunity scoring only when the sample supports them.** Write outcomes in the *minimize/increase + metric + object + clarifier* syntax, against the steps of the job map. Score importance and satisfaction for *outcomes*, not for whole jobs. With a design-partner cohort of 3–5, treat the scores as a directional ranking from structured conversations, not as ODI's survey result. Say so in the artifact.
- **Check the motivation clause of every job story for solution leakage.** "I want to see a prioritised list of compliance gaps" is a product output. "I want to know which gaps could fail the review before the reviewer finds them" is the motivation. Only the second survives a different solution.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **No new agent.** JTBD analysis is knowledge that `requirements-analyst` applies. Choosing a school is a decision criterion, which belongs in the skill (the CLAUDE.md boundary clarification).
- **No standalone `outcome-driven-innovation` skill yet.** Full ODI (job map, around a hundred outcome statements, a quantitative survey, outcome-based segmentation) is a heavy, survey-dependent method. The first product is a small-business design-partner product, and nothing in it would use the method at full strength. Fold a scaled-down version into `jtbd-analysis` as the enterprise tier (an `examples/` case plus a `references/` page on outcome statements). Promote it to its own skill only if a product reaches a customer base that can be surveyed.
- **The switch interview strengthens the case for the candidate `customer-discovery-interviewing` skill** proposed in `the-mom-test-fitzpatrick.md`. That skill would be the one home for Mom Test rules, the switch-interview timeline, earlyvangelist screening and Maurya's problem interview. `jtbd-analysis`, `user-persona` and `feedback-template` would point to it instead of each restating Mom Test material.

## Key Terminology

Worth considering for `skills/glossary-management/`, if adopted:

- **Job (to be done)**: the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. It is distinct from a *task* (a step inside a job) and from a *need* stated in product terms.
- **Job statement**: Ulwick's one-line, solution-free form of a job: *verb + object + contextual clarifier*. It is stable over time.
- **Job story**: Klement's and Intercom's situational form: *When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]*. It is distinct from a user story, which opens with a role.
- **Desired-outcome statement**: Ulwick's measurable success criterion for a step of a job: *direction + metric + object of control + contextual clarifier*.
- **Job map**: Ulwick's eight-step decomposition of a job (define, locate, prepare, confirm, execute, monitor, modify, conclude).
- **Opportunity score**: `Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)`, computed per *desired outcome*.
- **Forces of progress**: push, pull, anxiety and habit, the four forces that decide whether a switch happens.
- **Switch interview**: Moesta's timeline reconstruction of a recent purchase or firing decision.
- **Struggling moment**: the moment the current way stops being good enough, where demand starts.
- **Hire / fire**: choosing a solution for a job, which always displaces another.
- **Big Hire / Little Hire**: the purchase decision versus each act of actual use.
- **Non-consumption**: people with the job who use no solution at all.
- **Job executor / beneficiary / buyer**: Ulwick's split between the person doing the job, the person it is done for, and the person who pays.

## Caveats

- **Confident**: the progress-in-a-circumstance definition, hire/fire language, the milkshake story, the functional/social/emotional dimensions, the job spec's inclusion of purchase and use experiences, the Big Hire / Little Hire distinction, non-consumption, and the general argument that attributes correlate with purchases but do not cause them. All of these are central to *Competing Against Luck* and widely and consistently summarized. Also confident: Ulwick's job statement and outcome syntax, the eight-step job map, the opportunity algorithm and outcome-based segmentation. Equally confident: the job-story format and its Intercom origin, Klement's "progress versus activities" disagreement with Ulwick, the four forces, and the general shape of the switch timeline.
- **Less certain**: the exact chapter where the book discusses the forces, the precise names and number of the "data fallacies", and how deeply the book itself covers the switch interview as opposed to crediting Moesta's practice. I recall the forces diagram appearing in the book, but the full interview method lives in Moesta and Spiek's own materials. Also uncertain: the exact stage names of Moesta's timeline, which vary between his talks and *Demand-Side Sales 101*, and the recency window he recommends for interviewees. Klement's precise position on the functional/emotional/social split is uncertain too. I believe he treats emotional progress as the core of the job rather than as a separate dimension, but I would not quote him on it. The "around a hundred outcomes per job" figure is Ulwick's order of magnitude as I recall it, not a statistic to cite.
- **No page numbers, quotes or survey figures are given, deliberately.** None should be added to skills from this file without the book in hand.
- **The schools' public disagreement is partly commercial.** Strategyn, the Re-Wired Group and Klement each sell training. Their claims about who originated what (Ulwick says Christensen's early job thinking drew on his 1990s work) should not be presented as settled history in a skill. Credit each technique to the person most associated with it, and leave the priority dispute out.
- **ODI's quantitative core does not survive at this repo's scale.** With 3–5 design partners, opportunity scores are a structured ranking from conversations, not ODI. The existing scaling caveat (`references/job-statements-and-dimensions.md:180-187`) is right and should be kept.

# Inspired: How to Create Tech Products Customers Love
**Authors:** Marty Cagan (Silicon Valley Product Group)
**Domain:** Product Management Practice — Product Discovery, Team Topology, and the Discovery/Delivery Split (within Product Strategy and Go-to-Market)

## Core Concepts

- **Empowered product teams versus feature teams — the book's central, most-cited distinction.** A *feature team* is handed a prioritized list of features and projects (a roadmap with dates) and is measured on output — did it ship what was on the list, on time. An *empowered product team* is handed a problem to solve or an outcome to move (typically expressed as a business objective or a Key Result), is trusted to figure out the best solution, and is measured on the outcome actually achieved, not on what got shipped. Cagan calls a feature team run at scale a "feature factory": a team that ships continuously but rarely moves the metrics that matter, because no one on the team was ever asked to solve a problem — only to build what was already decided. This distinction is the lens the rest of the book's techniques exist to serve, and it is the lens this file uses to evaluate the repo's actual architecture (see Applies To and the required comparison below).
- **The product trio — product manager, product designer, and a lead engineer, collaborating on discovery together, continuously, not in sequential handoff.** Cagan is explicit that discovery is not a solo PM activity that produces a spec engineering then implements. The trio works the same problem from three angles at once: the PM worries about value and business viability, the designer worries about usability and the overall user experience, and the engineer worries about feasibility and often contributes the cleverest solutions precisely because engineers understand what's newly possible with current technology in ways a PM or designer typically doesn't. Excluding engineers from discovery — treating them as a delivery-only resource who receives a finished spec — is named directly as a common and costly mistake, not a neutral division of labor.
- **The four big risks every product idea must address before it earns significant engineering investment: value, usability, feasibility, viability.** *Value risk*: will customers choose to buy or use this. *Usability risk*: can users figure out how to use it. *Feasibility risk*: can engineers actually build it with the time, skills, and technology available. *Viability risk*: does this work for the business — does it hold up for marketing, sales, finance, legal, and executive stakeholders, not just for the customer. Cagan's insistence is that all four must be addressed *before* full-scale delivery investment, and that they are assessed together, by the same trio, because a solution that resolves value risk but fails feasibility (or resolves feasibility but fails viability) is not actually de-risked — it just has one risk left undiscovered until it's expensive to fix.
- **Product discovery and product delivery are distinct disciplines with opposite economics, run in parallel by the same team — not sequential phases run by different people.** *Discovery* is fast and cheap: its entire purpose is to answer "should we build this" using the lightest artifact that can produce a reliable answer — a prototype, not production code. *Delivery* is where the actual robust, scalable, tested production version gets built, and it is comparatively slow and expensive. Cagan's operating rhythm has the same team running discovery on next week's or next sprint's candidate ideas *while* delivering the already-validated idea from the previous cycle — discovery constantly runs a cycle or two ahead of delivery, on the same people, not as a separate department or a separate phase gate that the whole team passes through together. This is often referred to (not primarily by Cagan himself, but by the wider community around his work) as "dual-track" — discovery and delivery as two continuous, parallel tracks rather than two sequential stages.
- **Prototypes are the primary discovery instrument, and they come in several deliberately different fidelities depending on which risk they're built to test.** A *feasibility prototype* (often a quick engineering spike) tests whether something is technically possible at all. A *user prototype* (a clickable mockup — historically built in tools like InVision or, today, Figma — with no real backend) tests usability and, to a degree, value, by putting something realistic in front of real users and watching their reaction rather than asking whether they'd use it. A *live-data prototype* wires a user prototype to real backend data to test value risk more convincingly, since users respond differently to their own real data than to placeholder content. A *hybrid prototype* combines these. None of these are shipped — that is the entire point; they exist only to produce a fast, cheap answer before committing the delivery team's time.
- **Concierge tests and Wizard-of-Oz tests validate value risk by faking the automation, not the outcome.** In a concierge test, the team manually delivers the outcome the future product would automate (e.g., a person manually assembles the report a system would eventually generate), openly, so the customer knows a human is doing the work — testing whether the outcome itself is valued before any engineering exists to automate it. A Wizard-of-Oz test does the same thing covertly — the customer believes they are interacting with an automated system, when a person is operating it behind the scenes — used specifically to test whether the *experience* of the automated version would land, not just whether the underlying outcome is wanted. Both are named explicitly as legitimate, low-cost ways to test value and usability risk for an idea that would otherwise require weeks of engineering to test for real.
- **A/B testing is a discovery technique for teams with enough traffic to use it, not primarily a delivery-phase optimization tool.** Cagan positions split-testing as a way to resolve value risk quantitatively at scale — shipping a cheap, rough version of two or more variants to real traffic and measuring which one actually produces the desired customer or business behavior — distinct from qualitative discovery techniques (interviews, prototypes) that work at any traffic volume but produce qualitative rather than statistically powered evidence. He is candid that this technique requires meaningful production traffic to be useful, which is why it sits at the end of his discovery-technique list rather than the start.
- **The Opportunity Assessment is a lightweight, fast, pre-commitment gate — roughly ten questions, answerable in an hour, not a heavyweight PRD.** Before a team invests real discovery effort (let alone delivery effort) in a candidate opportunity, Cagan has them answer a short, fixed set of questions: what business objective does this serve; how will we measure success; what problem does this solve for the customer; who is the target customer; how big is the market; what alternatives exist today; why are we well-suited to win this; why now; what is the go-to-market strategy; and what are the biggest risks (value/usability/feasibility/viability) this idea carries. The document is deliberately shallow — its job is to filter out weak candidate ideas before real discovery time is spent on them, not to fully specify a solution.
- **Product vision and product strategy are distinct, and both precede any individual team's discovery work.** *Product vision* is the multi-year (Cagan typically frames it in 2–5-year or longer terms) picture of what the product is trying to become and why it matters — durable, rarely revised. *Product strategy* is the sequenced, prioritized set of bets — usually expressed as a small number of focused objectives at a time — the organization pursues to get there; Cagan is emphatic about focus ("the essence of strategy is saying no" in substance, if not his exact phrasing) — spreading a team's attention across many objectives simultaneously is treated as a strategy failure, not a scheduling inconvenience. Empowered teams receive their slice of this strategy as a problem/objective (frequently operationalized as an OKR Key Result), not as a pre-decided feature.
- **Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) are the mechanism that hands empowered teams a problem instead of a solution.** A team is given a Key Result to move (an outcome, with a target and a deadline) and is trusted to discover, prototype-test, and deliver whatever combination of solutions actually moves it — the KR is the contract between leadership and the team; the specific features are the team's own discovery output, not something handed down alongside the KR. This is the direct organizational mechanism behind the empowered-team/feature-team distinction above — a KR-driven team is structurally incapable of being a feature team, because it was never given a feature list to begin with.
- **Roadmaps built as a prioritized list of features and dates are named directly as an anti-pattern, and their harm is structural, not cosmetic.** Cagan's objection is not that roadmaps exist, but that a feature-and-date roadmap commits a team to a *solution* before discovery has happened — it converts what should be an empowered team's discovery output into an already-decided input, guaranteeing the team becomes a feature team regardless of how it's staffed or described. His alternative is an outcome-based / objectives roadmap: a sequence of problems or business objectives to address, in priority order, with the actual solutions left for each team's discovery process to determine when that objective's turn comes.

## Actionable Techniques

- Before committing meaningful discovery time (let alone delivery time) to a candidate opportunity, run a lightweight Opportunity Assessment — the ten-ish-question, hour-long version, not a full requirements pass — and use it as a go/no-go filter, not a design document.
- For any candidate solution with real engineering uncertainty, build a feasibility prototype (a quick spike, not production code) before committing to a delivery estimate based on assumption.
- For any candidate solution with real usability or value uncertainty, build a user prototype (clickable, no real backend) and put it in front of 5–8 real or representative target users, watching their reaction and comprehension rather than asking "would you use this" — the same anti-pattern Olsen's sibling file names independently for the hi-fi MVP.
- When a candidate idea's value is uncertain but building the automation would take weeks, run a concierge or Wizard-of-Oz test first — manually deliver (openly or covertly) the outcome the future feature would automate, and observe whether the customer actually values receiving it, before writing a line of the automation.
- Explicitly classify every candidate idea's outstanding risk before it enters delivery: which of value/usability/feasibility/viability has been tested and resolved, and which is still assumed. An idea entering delivery with an untested risk in any of the four categories is entering delivery unvalidated in Cagan's specific sense, regardless of how well-specified its requirements document is.
- When defining what a team owns, check whether it is being handed a problem/outcome (a Key Result, a business objective) or a solution (a named feature, already decided). If it is the latter, the team is structurally a feature team for that unit of work, no matter what it is called.
- Treat discovery as continuous, not a phase that closes: even after a solution direction is validated and delivery begins, the next cycle's discovery should already be running against the next candidate idea in the strategy sequence — not waiting for the current delivery cycle to finish before starting.

## Applies To

**Positioning relative to `lean-product-playbook-olsen.md` (read directly, per this file's brief) — the two books cover adjacent but distinct territory, and should not be merged.** Olsen's Lean Product Process (target market → underserved needs → value proposition → MVP feature set → hi-fi prototype → test with customers, iterate) is itself a discovery process, and its hi-fi MVP step is functionally close to Cagan's user prototype technique — both insist on validating a candidate solution with real users, cheaply, before committing engineering. But Olsen's book is scoped to *one product's* path to product-market fit and says nothing about *team topology* — nowhere does Olsen distinguish an empowered team from a feature team, or discuss who on a team does discovery versus delivery, or name feasibility and viability as risks distinct from the needs-fit his Opportunity Score ranks. Cagan's distinctive, non-duplicative contributions to this cluster are: (1) the empowered-team/feature-team organizational distinction, entirely absent from Olsen; (2) the product trio's inclusion of engineering *in* discovery, not just as delivery's downstream recipient, also absent from Olsen; and (3) the four-risk framework as a named, exhaustive checklist (value, usability, feasibility, *and* viability) — Olsen's book concentrates almost entirely on value (needs-fit) and touches usability via the hi-fi MVP, but has no equivalent treatment of feasibility or viability as separate, explicitly-checked risk categories. **A naming collision to flag explicitly**: Cagan's *Opportunity Assessment* (a ten-question, initiative-level go/no-go gate — business objective, market size, competition, GTM, risk) and Olsen's *Opportunity Score* (a quantitative `Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)` formula for ranking already-scoped candidate needs from survey data) share a name fragment and roughly the same place in a discovery sequence, but are mechanically different tools solving different problems — the former is a qualitative initiative-level filter, the latter is a quantitative needs-ranking calculation. A future skill author adopting content from both files must not collapse these into one artifact.

### The required comparison: does this repo's actual agent architecture match Cagan's empowered-team model?

**No — and the mismatch is structural, not a matter of degree.** Cagan's empowered team is a durable, cross-functional trio (PM, design, engineering) that does continuous discovery and delivery in parallel, on the same people, holding a candidate solution against all four risks together before committing real engineering time. This repo's actual architecture, as designed in `CLAUDE.md` and enacted by `agents/product-strategist.md` and `agents/requirements-analyst.md`, is a **sequential, phase-gated pipeline**: Strategy (product-strategist) fully completes and gets Shafi's approval before Ideate begins; Ideate (requirements-analyst) fully completes — personas, JTBD, requirements, epics, a complete story backlog with Gherkin acceptance criteria — before Design begins; Design (domain-modeler, enterprise-architect, ux-architect) fully completes, including the one place feasibility is assessed at all, before Implement begins; and only in Implement do `backend-engineer`/`frontend-engineer` — the closest thing this repo has to Cagan's "engineering" leg of the trio — ever see the work, by which point requirements, acceptance criteria, and architecture are already fixed. There is no point in this sequence where a PM-equivalent, a design-equivalent, and an engineering-equivalent jointly hold a candidate idea up against value, usability, feasibility, *and* viability risk together before committing to build it — each risk is checked, if at all, by a different agent, in a different phase, with the earlier phases already signed off and effectively irreversible by the time the later one runs. Concretely: viability-adjacent checks (business model canvas, GTM strategy) are locked in Strategy; value/usability-adjacent checks (personas, JTBD) happen in Ideate with no prototype ever built or tested against a real user; feasibility is assessed for the first time in Design, by `enterprise-architect`, an agent that had no voice in Ideate's persona or JTBD work at all — the exact "engineers excluded from discovery" pattern Cagan names as a direct cause of feature-factory outcomes. The `requirements-analyst.md` agent's own "60% capacity rule" and MoSCoW prioritisation of a fully-decomposed story backlog is, in Cagan's vocabulary, a feature-and-date roadmap being handed to delivery — precisely the artifact he treats as evidence of a feature team, not an empowered one. Even Customer Validation — the phase whose name sounds closest to Cagan's discovery-stage user testing — runs *after* Deploy, validating what was already fully built, rather than a cheap prototype run *before* the build commitment; this is the inversion of Cagan's discovery/delivery ordering, not an instance of it. None of this means the repo's process is poorly designed for what it is — a solo, non-technical PM (Shafi) approving each chunk of an AI-executed pipeline is a genuinely different operating context than a company staffing durable cross-functional teams, and a literal product trio is not achievable when there is no persistent human design/engineering staff to form one. But by Cagan's own explicit definition, this repo's actual architecture is structurally closer to the feature-team pattern he warns against — sequential handoff of an already-decided solution from discovery to delivery — than to the empowered-team model, and the one bright spot (product-strategist's outcome-oriented OKRs, and impact-mapping's goal-traced deliverables) is present at the strategic layer but does not survive the handoff into a fully-specified, MoSCoW-prioritised backlog by the time engineering ever sees the work.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **Candidate new skill: `discovery-prototyping`, owned by `requirements-analyst`, run within Ideate between epic definition and full story-backlog commitment.** This clears the bar this cluster has applied elsewhere (`lean-product-playbook-olsen.md`'s Kano precedent): it has its own elicitation method (build a feasibility, user, live-data, or hybrid prototype, or run a concierge/Wizard-of-Oz test), its own output artifact (a risk-assessment record per candidate epic — which of value/usability/feasibility/viability has been tested, with what result), and its own quality criteria (a prototype tests one risk cleanly; a concierge test is disclosed or covert deliberately, not accidentally; a candidate epic does not proceed to `example-mapping` with an untested value or usability risk). This is a genuine structural gap, not a restatement: nothing in `jtbd-analysis`, `user-persona`, or `impact-mapping` tests a candidate *solution* against real usage — all three validate the *problem* space and, in impact-mapping's case, the *goal-traceability* of a deliverable, but none puts anything in front of a real user before it's fully specified. Given this repo's actual constraints (no production traffic yet, a closed-beta motion per `sdlc-context.json`'s `first_product`), the skill should scope to the qualitative techniques (prototypes, concierge/Wizard-of-Oz) and explicitly flag A/B testing as inapplicable pre-launch, consistent with `lean-product-playbook-olsen.md`'s own caveat about its survey techniques needing real traffic/respondent volume this repo doesn't yet have.
- **Not proposed as a standalone skill: a full re-implementation of Cagan's Opportunity Assessment.** Its ten questions substantially overlap content already produced elsewhere in this repo's pipeline — business objective and success metric (product-strategist's OKR set), target customer and market sizing (`user-persona`, `gtm-strategy`'s ICP), competition (`competitive-analysis`), why-us and GTM (`gtm-strategy`, `business-model-canvas`) — the genuinely new element is only the *gate* itself (a fast, explicit go/no-go checkpoint before deep discovery effort, naming all four risks at once) rather than any of its individual questions. The right-sized fix is a lightweight amendment to `impact-mapping` or `epic-definition` (not read directly here, but named as the consumer of impact-mapping's WHAT deliverables in that skill's own Connection to Other Skills table) — add a short, explicit four-risk checklist per epic before it's selected into MVP scope, rather than authoring a new SKILL.md that would substantially restate content four other skills already own.
- **No case for a new agent.** The trio model and the four-risk framework are knowledge — a checklist and a set of lightweight testing techniques — not reasoning distinct from what `requirements-analyst` already performs during Ideate; both belong in a skill the existing agent consumes, consistent with every other book in this cluster's identical conclusion. The deeper mismatch this file surfaces (feasibility and viability being assessed in separate phases by separate agents, after earlier phases are already locked) is an architectural property of the phase-gated pipeline itself, not something a new skill or agent could fix without changing how phases are sequenced — that is a decision for Shafi, not a skill-authoring recommendation this file is positioned to make.

## Key Terminology

Candidates for `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language — none of these currently appear there, confirmed against the terms actually cited in the four sibling files' own Key Terminology sections:

- **Empowered Product Team** — a durable, cross-functional team given a problem or business outcome to solve and trusted to determine the solution, measured on outcome achieved. Distinct from a **Feature Team** (or "feature factory" at scale) — a team handed a pre-decided list of features and dates, measured on output shipped. This pair is the load-bearing distinction of the whole book and is not currently named anywhere in this repo's skills or agent files.
- **Product Trio** — the product manager, product designer, and a lead engineer collaborating on discovery together and continuously, as opposed to a sequential PM-writes-spec-then-engineering-builds handoff.
- **Product Discovery / Product Delivery** — two distinct disciplines with opposite economics: discovery is fast and cheap and answers "should we build this" using prototypes, never production code; delivery is the actual robust, tested, production build. Distinct from this repo's phase names Ideate and Implement, which map loosely onto discovery and delivery respectively but, per the comparison above, are run sequentially by different agents rather than continuously in parallel by the same team, which is the entire point of Cagan's distinction.
- **Value Risk / Usability Risk / Feasibility Risk / Viability Risk** — Cagan's four-part risk taxonomy every candidate solution must clear before significant delivery investment. Distinct from this repo's NFR-specification categories (which describe *how well* a built system must perform) — these four are pre-build questions about *whether a candidate idea should be built at all*, asked before any NFR would apply.
- **Opportunity Assessment** — Cagan's lightweight, roughly ten-question, hour-long go/no-go document for a candidate initiative, checked before deep discovery effort begins. Not to be confused with Olsen's **Opportunity Score** (see `lean-product-playbook-olsen.md`) — a quantitative needs-ranking formula, a different tool despite the shared name fragment; see the explicit note under Applies To.
- **Concierge Test / Wizard-of-Oz Test** — value/usability-risk discovery techniques in which a human manually delivers (openly, in a concierge test; covertly, in a Wizard-of-Oz test) the outcome a future automated feature would provide, to test demand and experience before building the automation.
- **Continuous Discovery** — the practice of running discovery on next-cycle candidate ideas in parallel with delivering the current, already-validated idea, rather than treating discovery as a one-time phase that closes before delivery opens.

## Caveats

- **This file's required cross-reference work covers all four sibling files, and the overlap findings are load-bearing, not decorative.** `lean-product-playbook-olsen.md` is the deepest point of overlap (both books cover pre-build validation of a candidate solution) and is addressed at length under Applies To — the verdict is that the two are complementary (Olsen: one product's PMF path, heaviest on the value/needs risk; Cagan: team topology plus a broader four-risk net) rather than duplicative, and the Opportunity Assessment/Opportunity Score naming collision is flagged explicitly so a future skill author doesn't merge them. `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md` and `obviously-awesome-dunford.md` sit at a different altitude entirely — both are about *which market segment to attack and how to frame the product against alternatives once a solution exists* — Cagan's book has essentially nothing to say about market segmentation or positioning, and this file found no material overlap with either beyond the shared "practitioner book, not peer-reviewed research" caveat both of those files also carry. `loved-lauchengco.md` is about product *marketing* as an ongoing organizational function (positioning-as-living-hypothesis, launch as a three-audience process, win/loss analysis) — also no material overlap; Cagan's book is almost entirely about product management discovery practice and team structure, and does not address marketing, messaging, or launch mechanics at all.
- **Confidence on this summary is high but is drawn from strong, well-established familiarity with one of the most widely taught and cited books in the product management field, not a fresh page-by-page re-read of the source text for this file.** The core structures summarized here — the empowered/feature team distinction, the product trio, the four risks, the discovery/delivery split, the named discovery techniques (prototypes, concierge/Wizard-of-Oz, A/B testing), and the Opportunity Assessment — are the book's most consistently and publicly reiterated content (including in Cagan's own public writing, talks, and the SVPG blog, which closely track the book), and the risk of misattribution on these core points is low. Exact chapter numbers, the precise wording of the Opportunity Assessment's ten questions, and some secondary content (the book's several role-specific chapters for PM archetypes, platform teams, and experience teams) are not reproduced here at the same confidence level and should be verified against the physical text before being quoted verbatim in any future skill's frontmatter or body.
- **The book assumes an organizational context this repo structurally does not have: multiple durable, persistent human team members who can specialize into PM/design/engineering roles and stay together across cycles.** This repo's actual "team" for any given phase is a single Claude Code agent invocation, reset between phases, reviewed chunk-by-chunk by one non-technical PM (Shafi). A literal product trio — three role-specialized people or agents deliberating together in the same room across the life of a feature — is not an available configuration here regardless of which skills exist; the honest reading of this book's core contribution to this repo is therefore diagnostic (it names precisely how and where the current pipeline resembles a feature-team pattern) rather than directly prescriptive of an org-chart fix, since there is no org chart to redraw. The `discovery-prototyping` skill proposed above is scoped accordingly: something a single agent can execute as an added step within Ideate, not a proposal to restructure the phase-gated pipeline into concurrent cross-functional teams.
- **The book's canonical examples are consumer and B2B SaaS products at technology companies with meaningful existing user bases and production traffic (Cagan's own background is largely eBay, plus decades of SVPG client engagements across well-known Silicon Valley companies).** Its heavier-traffic techniques (A/B testing in particular, and to some extent live-data prototypes that need real backend data to test against) assume a scale this repo's actual first product does not yet have — pre-launch, 3–5 named design partners per `gtm-strategy`'s closed-beta motion (per `sdlc-context.json`'s `first_product` section, consistent with `crossing-the-chasm-moore.md`'s and `lean-product-playbook-olsen.md`'s identical caveat about their own techniques). The qualitative techniques (feasibility prototypes, user prototypes, concierge/Wizard-of-Oz tests) do not have this dependency and are the ones this file's `discovery-prototyping` proposal is scoped around for exactly that reason.
- **This file focuses on the book's discovery/team-topology content because that is what maps directly onto the four skills and two agent files this file's brief required reading — it does not attempt comprehensive coverage of every chapter** (e.g., the book's later chapters on specific PM archetypes for platform, growth, and B2B enterprise teams, and its leadership/coaching chapters aimed at VPs of Product, are not summarized here because no read skill or agent in this repo's current inventory corresponds to that organizational layer).

# This Is Service Design Doing
**Authors:** Marc Stickdorn, Markus Edgar Hormess, Adam Lawrence, Jakob Schneider
**Domain:** Service Design

## Core Concepts

- **Service design is holistic — it designs the whole service, not just the interface (Ch. 1–2).** The book's central claim is that most "UX" work stops at the digital or physical touchpoint, while the service itself — the end-to-end system of people, processes, and infrastructure that produces the customer's experience — extends far beyond what the customer sees. A support ticket resolved fast might still be a bad *service* if the backstage handoff between two teams silently dropped context. This repo's UX skill set (`user-journey-mapping`, `ux-flow-design`, `information-architecture`) is customer-perspective work by design — none of the three ever asks "what has to succeed behind the screen for this touchpoint to work?" That question is this book's reason for existing.
- **Six principles of service design (Ch. 1): human-centred, collaborative, iterative, sequential, real, holistic.** Human-centred means considering the experience of *everyone* affected by the service — not just the target customer, but frontline staff and backstage operators too. Collaborative means stakeholders of diverse backgrounds are actively involved in design, not just consulted. Iterative means small, cheap explorative iterations rather than one big-bang launch. Sequential means the service is visualised as a sequence of interrelated actions orchestrated into a coherent whole over time. Real means needs are investigated in reality (not assumed) and ideas are prototyped in reality (not just described) — intangible value is made tangible before it's built. Holistic means the service must sustainably address the needs of all stakeholders in its entire environment, not optimise one touchpoint at the expense of the system around it. These principles are the "why" behind the book's toolkit — the front-stage/back-stage split exists specifically to make the "holistic" and "human-centred" principles checkable rather than aspirational.
- **The service blueprint (Ch. 3, drawing on Lynn Shostack's 1984 original and Bitner/Ostrom/Morgan's refinements) makes the invisible half of the service visible.** A blueprint is a diagram with explicit horizontal lanes, read left to right against the same timeline as a customer journey: **Physical Evidence** (what's tangible at each step — a confirmation email, a badge, a scan progress bar), **Customer Actions** (front-stage, what the customer does — identical in spirit to a journey map's Actions row), **Frontstage/Onstage Interactions** (visible employee or system actions the customer directly perceives), **Backstage/Behind-the-Scenes Actions** (employee or system actions that support the frontstage but are invisible to the customer), and **Support Processes** (internal processes, systems, and infrastructure that enable the backstage — the layer furthest from the customer). Three horizontal boundary lines separate them: the **Line of Interaction** (between customer and frontstage — crossed whenever the customer and the organisation interact directly), the **Line of Visibility** (between frontstage and backstage — everything below this line is invisible to the customer, by definition), and the **Line of Internal Interaction** (between backstage and support processes — separates people-facing backstage work from systems/infrastructure that enable it).
- **Front-stage/back-stage is borrowed from Erving Goffman's dramaturgical sociology, not invented for service design.** The book is explicit about this lineage (Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959) — the theatre metaphor is deliberate: a service is a performance, the customer only ever sees the "stage," and the "line of visibility" is a hard boundary the design must actively decide what crosses. This is a stronger, more literal metaphor than this repo's `event-storming-facilitation` ever uses — Event Storming's `<External System>` and `{Aggregate}` cards imply an "inside vs. outside" boundary but never name a customer-visibility boundary specifically; a Bounded Context can be entirely backstage (e.g., a classification engine) with no front-stage projection at all, and Event Storming has no notation that flags that fact.
- **The double-diamond-derived four-phase process: Research (Explore), Ideation (Create), Prototyping (Reflect), Implementation (Ch. 4–8).** The book organises its ~40-tool toolkit around four iterative macro-phases rather than the Design Council UK's original four-word "Discover/Define/Develop/Deliver" labels (it acknowledges the lineage but uses its own names and draws the process as a repeating, non-linear loop rather than a strictly bounded double diamond). **Research** establishes what's real — interviews, contextual inquiry, service safaris, desk research, stakeholder/ecosystem mapping. **Ideation** diverges then converges on concepts — brainstorming variants, "How Might We" reframing, Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas to check viability alongside desirability. **Prototyping** makes ideas real before building them — role-play, desktop walkthroughs, Wizard-of-Oz backstage simulation, service walkthroughs. **Implementation** rolls the service out incrementally — pilots, minimum viable service (MVS), staff training, and only then the full build.
- **Moments of Truth (Jan Carlzon, cited in Ch. 3) — not every touchpoint carries equal weight.** A Moment of Truth is a touchpoint where the customer's overall perception of the service is disproportionately shaped — often an emotionally charged, high-stakes, or trust-critical interaction. The book's technique is to identify these on the blueprint/journey first and concentrate design and prototyping effort there rather than spreading investment evenly across every step. This is functionally the same idea as `user-journey-mapping`'s "emotional valleys" (its Anti-Patterns section: "a journey map with no valley is not evidence of a great product... the valleys are the deliverable") — the book supplies the named term and ties it explicitly to *both* sides of the line of visibility, since a Moment of Truth can be caused by a backstage failure the customer never directly sees the cause of (a late report is a front-stage Moment of Truth whose root cause — a stalled backstage scan — the journey map alone cannot surface).
- **Service ecosystem maps and stakeholder maps (Ch. 5) — the wider network beyond one customer's journey.** Before mapping any single journey or blueprint, the book has designers map the full ecosystem: every actor (customers, staff, third-party partners, regulators, systems) and the value or information each exchanges with the others. This is deliberately broader and earlier than a single-persona journey map — it exists to catch actors a journey-map-first process would never surface (e.g., an auditor who never touches the product but consumes its output, or a third-party system like Google Drive whose behaviour constrains what the service can promise).
- **Co-creation over consultation (Ch. 1, Ch. 6).** Prototyping and blueprinting in the book are explicitly workshop-run, cross-functional, in-the-room exercises — frontline staff, backstage engineers, and customers sketch the blueprint together, because the people who run the backstage process are the only ones who actually know what happens there. A designer reconstructing backstage steps alone, without the people who run them, reliably gets the backstage lanes wrong. This has a direct, blunt implication for a solo-operator context: an AI agent inferring backstage lanes from the domain model *is* the equivalent failure mode the book warns against, unless the backstage content is grounded in something a real domain expert actually validated (in this repo's case: the Event Storming session output, which — per `event-storming-facilitation`'s own quality criteria — requires "at least one person with first-hand domain knowledge").
- **Minimum Viable Service (MVS) — the implementation-phase counterpart to an MVP (Ch. 8).** Rather than a stripped-down *product*, an MVS is a stripped-down but fully *real*, live service delivered to a small cohort — including its human/operational parts — before scaling. The book's point: a service's riskiest unknowns are as often operational (can the backstage team actually handle this process repeatedly, under real load, without the designer in the room?) as they are about the interface. This is a distinct risk category from anything in this repo's `ux-flow-design` (which validates screen-level interaction paths) or `beta-program-design` (not read in full here, but understood from its skill name to be closer to a go-to-market cohort mechanism than an operational-readiness rehearsal).

## Actionable Techniques

- **Run a service blueprint workshop directly off an existing journey map and Event Storming output**, rather than starting from scratch: take a journey map's Stage/Actions/Touchpoints rows as the front-stage lanes verbatim, then populate the backstage and support-process lanes using the Actors, Commands, Aggregates, Policies, and External System cards already discovered in that domain's Event Storming Process Level session — translating DDD vocabulary into plain operational language above the line of visibility, while keeping the precise domain terms as a parenthetical for traceability.
- **Draw the line of visibility explicitly and ask, for every front-stage step, "what below this line has to succeed first?"** For a step like "Reviews compliance gap report" in the existing Maya Chen journey map example, this forces an explicit answer: the estate scan for every connected source must have completed, the classification engine must have finished labelling every discovered asset, and the compliance-evaluation service must have finished computing gaps against the selected framework — none of which the journey map's Touchpoints row ("Compliance Dashboard; Data Asset Detail pages") currently names.
- **Identify Moments of Truth on the blueprint, not just the journey map**, and check each one for a backstage cause the customer-facing emotional arc alone cannot explain — a "Frustrated" or "Anxious" emotion row entry with no visible front-stage cause is a signal to inspect the backstage/support-process lanes underneath that exact stage.
- **Run a desktop walkthrough before specifying a UX flow for any process with a nontrivial backstage component** — physically (or in an artifact) walk small props or index cards representing the customer, the frontstage system, the backstage service, and the support process through one full cycle of the scenario, out loud, before `ux-flow-design` locks in the happy path and error paths; this reliably surfaces backstage failure states (a stalled scan, a classification timeout) that a screen-first flow design misses.
- **Build a service ecosystem map once per product**, before the first journey map, listing every actor who exchanges value or information with the service — including actors who never open the product (an auditor consuming a report, a third-party API like Google Drive or S3 whose rate limits and permission model constrain what the service can promise) — and check every subsequently produced journey map's persona list against it for coverage gaps.
- **Treat a Minimum Viable Service, not just an MVP, as the Implementation-phase rollout unit** for any feature with a real operational/backstage component — pilot the backstage process (scan scheduling, classification review, compliance-report generation) with a small number of real tenants before scaling, and treat "can the backstage process run repeatedly without hand-holding" as its own release-readiness question distinct from "does the UI work."
- **Prioritise blueprinting for processes where the front-stage/back-stage gap is largest**, not for every journey uniformly — a simple CRUD-like flow (editing a display name) has a thin, uninteresting backstage; an estate scan, a classification pass, or a compliance evaluation has a thick, multi-system backstage where the blueprint earns its cost. Use the existing journey map's emotional valleys as the prioritisation signal for which journeys get a blueprint pass.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **`service-blueprinting` (new skill, owner `ux-architect`, phase `design`).** Produces the front-stage/back-stage/support-process blueprint described above, explicitly sourced from an existing journey map's front-stage rows plus an existing Event Storming session's backstage cards — not a replacement for either, a bridge artifact between them. Positioned as Step 1.5 in `ux-architect.md`'s Execution Sequence: run after journey maps (which supply front-stage content and the emotional-valley prioritisation signal) and before user flows (which need to know about backstage error states — a stalled scan, a classification timeout — as flow branches). Should reuse this repo's existing ASCII-diagram convention (as `ux-flow-design` and `event-storming-facilitation` already do) for the lane notation rather than requiring a diagramming tool, consistent with `CLAUDE.md`'s "reviewable by Shafi without IDE tooling" rule. Only warranted for journeys with a genuinely thick backstage (per the book's own "prioritise where the gap is largest" logic) — not every journey needs a blueprint, and the skill's Quality Criteria should say so explicitly to prevent uniform over-application.
- **`service-ecosystem-mapping` (optional new skill, owner `ux-architect` or `requirements-analyst`, phase `design` or `ideate`).** A lighter, earlier artifact than a journey map — an actor/value-exchange map covering every actor touched by the service, including ones a persona-first process would miss (auditors who consume but never use the product; third-party systems whose constraints shape the service). Lower priority than `service-blueprinting`; the existing `stakeholder-mapping` skill's Power/Interest Grid already covers a materially different purpose (buy-in and communication strategy for GTM, owned by `product-strategist` in the Strategy phase) and the two must stay clearly distinguished by name and purpose if both exist, per `CLAUDE.md`'s synonym-drift anti-pattern.
- **No new agent is warranted.** Both candidate skills fit cleanly inside `ux-architect`'s existing "UX design authority" role and its existing collaboration boundary with `domain-modeler` (the same boundary `ddd-agent-handoff`, already in `ux-architect`'s skill list, presumably governs) — this is an addition to that agent's toolkit, not a new domain.

## Key Terminology

Candidates for `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language, if `service-blueprinting` is built:

- **Service Blueprint** — the lane-based diagram itself; must not be confused with `ux-flow-design`'s "flow diagram" or `information-architecture`'s "IA hierarchy" in any glossary entry — distinct artifact, distinct purpose.
- **Front-stage / Back-stage** — Goffman's terms, adopted by the book as the primary vocabulary for "what the customer sees" vs. "what makes it work." Should be defined precisely enough that a future skill doesn't drift into calling the same distinction "client-side/server-side" (an implementation-jargon synonym `information-architecture`'s own Anti-Patterns table already warns against for UI labels — the same discipline should apply to this new term internally).
- **Line of Visibility** — the boundary line between front-stage and backstage; the single most load-bearing term from the book for this repo's purposes, since it is the concept the existing skill set currently has no notation for at all.
- **Line of Interaction / Line of Internal Interaction** — the two companion boundaries (customer↔frontstage, backstage↔support-process); lower priority than Line of Visibility but complete the notation if the skill is built.
- **Moment of Truth** — a touchpoint with disproportionate influence on overall perception; effectively synonymous in intent with `user-journey-mapping`'s existing "emotional valley," and a glossary entry should say so explicitly rather than let two terms for the same idea coexist unreconciled.
- **Minimum Viable Service (MVS)** — distinct from MVP; worth a glossary entry only if `beta-program-design` or a future implementation-readiness skill adopts the operational-rehearsal concept.

## Caveats

- **The book's toolkit assumes live, in-person, multi-stakeholder workshops (sticky notes, physical props, a room with frontline staff and backstage engineers together) — this repo has no such mechanism.** `CLAUDE.md`'s working model is one PM (Shafi) and one engineering agent; there is no frontline-staff-in-the-room co-creation step available. The actionable takeaway from this book for this repo is the *artifact shape* (the blueprint's lanes and lines) and the *cross-referencing discipline* (ground backstage content in something a domain expert actually validated — here, the Event Storming session, not the agent's own inference), not the live-facilitation techniques (desktop walkthroughs, Wizard-of-Oz role-play, service safaris) that assume a workshop room this repo doesn't have. A future `service-blueprinting` skill should say this plainly rather than instructing an agent to "facilitate a co-creation workshop" it cannot actually run.
- **The book predates and is architecture-agnostic to event-driven microservices — its "backstage" is a looser concept than a Bounded Context or Aggregate.** The mapping suggested here (Event Storming's Actors/Commands/Aggregates/Policies/External Systems → blueprint backstage/support-process lane content) is this document's own extrapolation, not something the book itself specifies — the book's backstage examples are organisational (a hotel's housekeeping staff, a bank's back-office processing team), not domain-modelled software systems. Treat the translation as a genuinely useful bridge this repo can build, not literal book content.
- **Do not duplicate `stakeholder-mapping`.** The book's stakeholder/ecosystem maps and this repo's existing `stakeholder-mapping` skill (Power/Interest Grid, owned by `product-strategist`, Strategy phase) solve different problems — GTM buy-in and communication strategy vs. service-design actor/value-exchange discovery — but use dangerously similar vocabulary ("stakeholder map" appears in both). If `service-ecosystem-mapping` is ever built, its frontmatter `description` and body must make the distinction explicit on first read, not leave it to a footnote, to avoid the exact synonym-drift anti-pattern `information-architecture`'s own Anti-Patterns table names.
- **This is a single-book cluster (`service-design`) with no companion titles yet.** Unlike `domain-driven-design` (three books cross-referencing each other) or `micro-frontends` (four), there is no second service-design book in this repo to cross-check claims against — treat this document's characterisation of the toolkit's exact tool count and phase names as this book's presentation specifically, not a consensus view of the wider service-design literature.

# Accelerate: The Science of Lean Software and DevOps
**Authors:** Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble, Gene Kim
**Domain:** DevOps & Delivery — Empirical Performance Science

## Core Concepts

- **The Four Key Metrics Are Validated Predictors, Not Opinions (Ch. 2–3).** Using four years of survey data and psychometric factor analysis (confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation modeling), the book demonstrates that **Deployment Frequency**, **Lead Time for Changes**, **Time to Restore Service (MTTR)**, and **Change Failure Rate** form a coherent, statistically validated construct called *software delivery performance* — and that this construct predicts organizational performance (profitability, productivity, market share, customer satisfaction) at high confidence. The key finding that contradicts received wisdom: high performers deploy more often *and* fail less often, recover faster *and* change more — tempo and stability reinforce each other rather than trading off.
- **High/Medium/Low Performer Clustering Is Real and Discontinuous (Ch. 3, Appendix A).** The survey data does not show a bell curve of delivery performance — it shows distinct clusters. High performers deploy on demand (multiple times per day), have lead times under one hour, restore service in under one hour, and change failure rates of 0–15%. Low performers deploy monthly or quarterly, have lead times of one to six months, restore in one to six months, and change failure rates of 46–60%. The gap between clusters is multiplicative, not additive; incremental improvement at the low end does not close it.
- **Trunk-Based Development Is a Technical Capability, Not a Culture Choice (Ch. 4).** The book's cluster analysis surfaces 24 technical, process, and cultural capabilities that statistically predict delivery performance. Among the technical ones: trunk-based development (branches live less than a day, or developers push directly to trunk), comprehensive test automation, deployment automation, shift-left security, and loose coupling of architecture. These are testable practices, not personality traits; teams that adopt them improve measurably, regardless of their prior performance cluster.
- **Continuous Delivery as a Capability Bundle (Ch. 4).** The book decomposes CD into testable sub-capabilities and finds each independently predicts performance: version control for all artifacts (including configuration), automated deployment pipelines, running all tests on every commit, deploying to production on demand, maintaining a deployable trunk at all times. The finding unique to this research (not in Humble/Farley's earlier book): teams with higher CD capability have *lower* burnout and *higher* job satisfaction — CD is not just a productivity lever, it is a quality-of-work lever.
- **The Westrum Organizational Culture Model (Ch. 3, 7).** Ron Westrum's sociology typology (Pathological → Bureaucratic → Generative) is adopted as the survey's culture measurement instrument. Generative organizations — characterized by high cooperation, bridging (messengers are not shot), shared risks, novelty implemented and celebrated, and failure treated as inquiry rather than prosecution — score dramatically higher on delivery performance. The book's claim: culture is both a predictor *and* an outcome; high CD capability enables the fast failure cycles that make a Generative culture possible.
- **Lean Product Management: Small Batches, Flow, and Experimentation (Ch. 8).** Working in small batches (features decomposed to ship in less than a week, not in large quarterly releases) independently predicts performance. The mechanism: small batches reduce WIP, shorten feedback loops, and make problems surface before they compound. The book connects this to Lean manufacturing's batch-size discipline; the specific practices scored: slicing features to deliver value incrementally, limiting work in progress, making flow visible, gathering and implementing customer feedback within a week of delivery.
- **Transformational Leadership Enables But Does Not Substitute for Technical Practice (Ch. 9).** Leadership that inspires shared vision, enables others to act, challenges the process, and models the way correlates with high performance — but only when the underlying technical capabilities (CD, trunk-based dev, test automation) are also present. Culture without automation is not enough; automation without culture does not sustain. The causal model is: transformational leadership → space to learn and improve → technical capability adoption → delivery performance improvement.
- **Toil and Burnout Are Measurable and Avoidable (Ch. 10–11).** The book defines *toil* (work that is manual, repetitive, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and scales linearly with service growth) as a driver of burnout. Deployment automation and CI/CD specifically reduce toil; teams with high CD capability report lower burnout rates in the survey data. Burnout in turn predicts attrition; the economic argument for CD includes the hiring cost of replacing burned-out engineers.

## Actionable Techniques

- Track all four DORA metrics per service — Deployment Frequency, Lead Time for Changes, Time to Restore Service, Change Failure Rate — as a delivery performance dashboard alongside the service's SLO dashboard; treat a regression in any of the four the same way you'd treat an SLO burn rate crossing a threshold.
- When diagnosing slow delivery, check whether branches are merged to main within a day — if not, the organization is in the long-lived-branch pattern that the cluster analysis associates with low performance regardless of other tooling.
- Decompose any feature backlog item whose implementation time exceeds one week into smaller deliverable slices; mark the decomposition as mandatory, not optional.
- Limit WIP explicitly: no engineer should be actively working on more than two features simultaneously; a visual WIP limit on the backlog board surfaces the constraint.
- Measure change failure rate not just as "how many deploys broke production" but as "percentage of changes requiring a hotfix or immediate rollback" — the denominator must be all deploys, not just the ones that caused incidents.
- Run a quarterly delivery performance retrospective using the four DORA metrics trended over time; the question is not "what went wrong" but "which metric moved, in which direction, and why."

## Key Terminology

Worth adding to `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language — none of these appear in `CLAUDE.md`'s platform-terms list:

- **Deployment Frequency** — how often an organization deploys to production; high performers deploy on demand (multiple times per day or week).
- **Lead Time for Changes** — the time from a commit being made to it running in production; high performers achieve under one hour.
- **Time to Restore Service (MTTR)** — how long it takes to restore service after a production incident or outage; high performers restore in under one hour.
- **Change Failure Rate** — the percentage of deployments to production that cause a service degradation and require remediation (hotfix, rollback, or patch); high performers stay below 15%.
- **DORA Metrics / Four Key Metrics** — the collective name for the above four, from the DevOps Research and Assessment research program; the validated, cross-industry benchmark set for delivery performance.
- **Toil** — operational work that is manual, repetitive, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and scales linearly with service growth; the named opposite of engineering work; reduction of toil is a measurable outcome of deployment automation.

## Caveats

- **The data is survey-based, not telemetry-based.** The DORA metrics as *concepts* are empirically validated; the specific metric *values* in any real organization's system are only as accurate as the instrumentation capturing them. The book's cluster boundaries (deploy multiple times/day = High, deploy monthly = Low) are directional — don't treat them as hard thresholds that put any single week's anomaly into a performance bucket.
- **The book measures software delivery performance, not product outcomes.** High delivery performance predicts organizational performance (profitability, market share) but does not guarantee it — a team that deploys bad ideas very quickly is a high-performing delivery organization and a failing product organization. The DORA metrics belong alongside `slo-definition` and `metrics-instrumentation-plan`'s product metrics, not as a replacement for either.
- **The four metrics are correlated, not independent levers.** Improving Deployment Frequency without also improving test automation typically increases Change Failure Rate — the four move together. Any attempt to game one metric (force-deploying more often to hit a frequency target while bypassing test gates) will show up as a simultaneous degradation in another.
- **The Westrum culture typology is a sociological classification, not a maturity model.** Organizations don't "advance" linearly from Pathological to Generative; the same team can exhibit different Westrum behaviors in different contexts. Use it as a diagnostic framing (is information flow cooperative or blame-oriented?) not a score to optimize.

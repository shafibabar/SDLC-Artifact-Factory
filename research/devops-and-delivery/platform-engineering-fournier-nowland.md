# Platform Engineering: A Guide for Technical, Product, and People Leaders
**Authors:** Camille Fournier, Ian Nowland
**Domain:** DevOps & Delivery — Platform Engineering Discipline

## Core Concepts

- **Platform Engineering Is a Product Discipline Applied to Internal Infrastructure (Ch. 1–2).** The book's foundational claim: a platform is a product whose customers are the organization's own engineering teams. Platform engineering applies product management practices — user research, roadmapping, adoption metrics, feedback loops — to the infrastructure and tooling that other teams use to build and operate their services. The key departure from traditional infrastructure/ops thinking: the platform team's measure of success is *developer adoption and productivity*, not just system uptime. A platform that is technically excellent but unused by its customers has failed.
- **The Thinnest Viable Platform (TVP) Principle (Ch. 3).** Platforms should start from the minimum set of capabilities that meaningfully reduces cognitive load for their internal customers — not the maximum set the platform team can imagine building. The TVP question: "what is the smallest set of platform capabilities that removes the largest friction from developers building on this platform?" Growing beyond TVP without validated customer pull is a form of over-engineering that creates maintenance burden without adoption benefit. The book explicitly warns against the "build it and they will come" trap: platforms are abandoned when they are not continuously shaped by what developers actually need.
- **Golden Paths, Not Forced Rails (Ch. 4–5).** A **golden path** is a well-lit, opinionated, supported route through a problem space — the platform's recommended way to deploy a service, provision a database, configure observability. It is *not* a mandatory rail that prevents developers from doing anything else; it is an investment in making the recommended way significantly easier than any alternative. When developers choose to go off the golden path, the platform team wants to understand why — it is signal, not a policy violation. Forced rails (only one way to do anything, permission gates for exceptions) destroy developer trust and drive workarounds that create invisible technical debt.
- **Platform as a Product: User Research, Roadmapping, Adoption Metrics (Ch. 6–7).** Platform teams that skip user research build the wrong things. The book's research practices for internal platforms: embed a platform engineer with a customer team for one sprint (experience their friction firsthand); run structured interviews ("walk me through the last time you deployed a service — what went wrong?"); track adoption metrics (percentage of services using the golden path, time-to-first-deploy for a new service, number of out-of-band exceptions requested). These replace the "we know what developers need" assumption that causes platform teams to build sophistication rather than simplicity.
- **Developer Experience (DX) and Cognitive Load (Ch. 8).** Cognitive load — the amount of complexity a developer must hold in their head to get their work done — is the primary metric the platform is designed to reduce. The book draws on Team Topologies' cognitive load framing (Matthew Skelton/Manuel Pais): a platform team's job is to maximize the cognitive load capacity a stream-aligned team (product team) can dedicate to their product domain by minimizing the cognitive load imposed by the infrastructure they run on. High DX platforms have: fast onboarding (a new engineer can deploy to production in one day), self-service provisioning (no ticket to create a new service, database, or environment), integrated documentation (reference docs live beside the tools), and fast, clear failure signals (when something goes wrong, the error message tells the developer what to do, not just what failed).
- **Internal Developer Portal and Service Catalog (Ch. 9).** A service catalog (Backstage is the dominant open-source implementation) is the platform's primary discoverability surface: every service, its owner, its dependencies, its SLO status, its documentation, and its deployment pipeline are queryable in one place. The book recommends starting a service catalog early — when there are fewer than 20 services, it is still a spreadsheet; at 50 services, the lack of a catalog creates real coordination failures. The catalog is also the gateway to the golden path: "click here to create a new service using the platform's standard template."
- **Platform Team Topology and Interaction Modes (Ch. 10–11, drawing on Team Topologies).** Platform teams should interact with stream-aligned (product) teams primarily in **X-as-a-Service** mode: the platform offers a well-defined, self-service capability; the product team consumes it without needing deep platform knowledge. When the platform team is working on a new capability for which the product team's domain knowledge is essential, they collaborate temporarily in **collaboration** mode; once the capability is stable and self-service, they return to X-as-a-Service. The anti-pattern: platform teams that are permanently in collaboration mode (every use of the platform requires a support ticket or a platform engineer to pair on it) — this indicates the platform's self-service layer has not been built or is broken.
- **Measuring Platform Success: Adoption, DORA Metrics, Toil Reduction (Ch. 12).** The book recommends three categories of platform success metrics: (1) **Adoption** — percentage of teams/services using the golden path, growth rate of adoption over time; (2) **Delivery performance** — the DORA four key metrics (Deployment Frequency, Lead Time, MTTR, Change Failure Rate) for the services using the platform, compared against a baseline before platform adoption; (3) **Toil** — hours per week engineers spend on undifferentiated infrastructure work, trended over time. A platform that improves DORA metrics and reduces toil is succeeding; a platform that improves adoption but not DORA metrics is providing the wrong golden path.

## Actionable Techniques

- Define the platform's TVP by asking each customer team: "what is the most time-consuming, most frustrating infrastructure task you did last month?" The top three answers across teams define the initial golden paths — not the platform team's own estimates of what is hard.
- Publish a published SLA for the golden path itself (not just the services running on it): time-to-first-deploy for a new service, time-to-provision a new database, time-to-create a new environment — track these as the platform's own SLIs, reviewed monthly.
- Run a quarterly DX survey with three questions: "how easy was it to use the platform to accomplish your goal?" (1–5 scale), "what was the most confusing part?", "what was missing?" — aggregate and publish the results to the whole organization.
- Track the number of out-of-band exceptions (requests to deviate from the golden path) as an inverse-adoption metric: rising exceptions mean the golden path does not cover real developer needs; declining exceptions mean the golden path is trusted.
- Time a new engineer's first production deploy from their first day: if it takes more than two days, the platform's onboarding golden path has failed, and that failure is the platform team's problem, not the new engineer's.
- Do not add a new platform capability until at least two different product teams have independently requested it — one request is a special case; two independent requests are a pattern.

## Key Terminology

Worth adding to `skills/glossary-management/`'s Ubiquitous Language:

- **Internal Developer Platform (IDP)** — the set of tools, services, and processes that a platform team builds and maintains for the benefit of product engineering teams; the platform team's product.
- **Golden Path** — the platform's well-lit, opinionated, supported route through a problem space; easier than any alternative but not the only allowed route.
- **Thinnest Viable Platform (TVP)** — the minimum set of platform capabilities that meaningfully reduces cognitive load for internal customers; the principle against over-engineering the platform.
- **Developer Experience (DX)** — the sum of all touchpoints a developer has with the platform: onboarding, provisioning, deploying, debugging, and getting help; the primary outcome the platform team is responsible for.
- **Cognitive Load** — the amount of complexity a developer must understand and manage to accomplish a task; the platform team's goal is to reduce it by handling infrastructure complexity on the developer's behalf.
- **Service Catalog** — an internal registry of every service in the organization — its owner, dependencies, SLO status, documentation, and deployment pipeline — queryable from a central interface; the platform's discoverability layer.
- **X-as-a-Service Interaction** — a platform-to-product team relationship mode in which the platform offers a well-defined, self-service capability and the product team consumes it without requiring platform team involvement; the target steady-state for mature platform capabilities.

## Caveats

- **The book targets organizations with multiple engineering teams building multiple products.** This repo's stated future includes multiple teams and products (per D023's rationale), but its current operational context is a single operator. The Platform Engineering discipline's full weight — user research, quarterly DX surveys, service catalog — is premature for a solo operator; the principles (golden paths, TVP, DX measurement) are the transferable substance, even when the "organization" is one person.
- **Backstage (the service catalog) is a significant operational investment.** The book mentions it as the dominant open-source implementation; actually running Backstage requires a Node.js server, a PostgreSQL database, and active curation. For an early-stage platform with fewer than 20 services, a well-organized `README.md` or a simple internal wiki page is a viable TVP for the service catalog; introduce Backstage when the service count and team count make its overhead worthwhile.
- **The book's measurement recommendations (DORA metrics as the platform success measure) align with Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble, Kim).** The two books should be read together: Accelerate establishes the statistical validity of the four key metrics; this book applies them as the platform team's accountability measure. Neither book alone gives the complete picture.
- **"Golden path" is a posture, not a specific tooling choice.** The book explicitly avoids endorsing any specific platform tooling stack; it teaches how to evaluate whether a platform capability is self-service and developer-friendly, not which tools to use. This repo's `helm-chart` + `opentofu-module` + GitOps + `cd-pipeline` stack is a golden path if and only if a new engineer can deploy a new service using it in under a day without asking for help.

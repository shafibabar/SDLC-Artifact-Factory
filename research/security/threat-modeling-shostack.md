# Threat Modeling: Designing for Security
**Authors:** Adam Shostack
**Domain:** Security — Threat Modeling

> Source note: this file is a synthesis of the book's framework (the Four-Question method, STRIDE-per-element, DFD-plus-trust-boundary modeling, attack trees) as taught by Shostack and as codified in the wider Microsoft SDL lineage — it is a reconstruction of stable, widely-taught technique, not a page-cited extract from the physical text.

## Core Concepts

- **The Four-Question Framework (the book's spine).** Shostack's central organizing idea is that all threat modeling reduces to four questions asked in order: (1) *What are we building?* — model the system; (2) *What can go wrong?* — find threats against that model; (3) *What are we going to do about it?* — decide on mitigations; (4) *Did we do a good job?* — validate the model, the threats found, and the mitigations chosen. The book's key pedagogical move is that these questions are approachable by ordinary engineers, not just security specialists — you do not need to be an attacker to enumerate what can go wrong if you have a structured model and a taxonomy. For this repo, question 1 is a diagram of the DataAsset ingestion → classification → compliance flow; question 4 is the validation loop that almost every lightweight threat-modeling effort skips.
- **STRIDE as a per-element threat taxonomy (Ch. 3).** STRIDE — Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege — is the book's primary answer to "what can go wrong?" Its power is that each threat category is the violation of a specific desirable security property, so the taxonomy doubles as a mitigation-property map: Spoofing violates **authentication**, Tampering violates **integrity**, Repudiation violates **non-repudiation**, Information disclosure violates **confidentiality**, Denial of service violates **availability**, Elevation of privilege violates **authorization**. Shostack strongly recommends applying STRIDE *per element* of the model rather than to the system as a whole — walk each process, data store, data flow, and external entity and ask which of the six apply to that element specifically. This is more thorough and far less hand-wavy than brainstorming threats against the system in the abstract.
- **Data Flow Diagrams and trust boundaries as the primary modeling artifact (Ch. 2).** Shostack favors the DFD as the model that anchors question 1. A DFD has exactly four element types: **external entities** (users, third-party services — Google Drive, S3), **processes** (the ingestion worker, the classification service), **data stores** (PostgreSQL, the Redpanda topic acting as a store-in-transit), and **data flows** (the arrows between them). Overlaid on the DFD are **trust boundaries** — the lines where the level of trust changes (the edge of a tenant's physically-isolated namespace, the mTLS boundary Linkerd draws between meshed pods, the boundary between the platform and an external SaaS). The book's recurring guidance: threats cluster on the data flows that *cross* trust boundaries, so the trust-boundary lines are where STRIDE analysis pays off most.
- **STRIDE-per-element as a coverage discipline, not a checklist ritual (Ch. 3–4).** The book pairs STRIDE with element-type heuristics: external entities are principally subject to Spoofing and Repudiation; data stores to Tampering, Information disclosure, and (for logs) Repudiation; data flows to Tampering, Information disclosure, and Denial of service; processes to all six. This gives a finite, reviewable grid — every element × its applicable STRIDE letters — that makes "did we look everywhere?" answerable instead of a matter of the modeler's imagination. The grid is the artifact a PM like Shafi can review without reading code.
- **Attack trees (Ch. 4).** For deeper analysis of a specific high-value goal, Shostack presents attack trees: the attacker's goal is the root, and each node is decomposed into the sub-goals (AND/OR) an attacker would chain to achieve it. Attack trees complement STRIDE — STRIDE is breadth-first threat *discovery* across the whole model; an attack tree is depth-first analysis of one goal (e.g., "exfiltrate one tenant's classified DataAssets across the physical isolation boundary"). The book is candid that authoring good attack trees from scratch is hard and that most teams get more value from reusing published trees and from STRIDE.
- **Software-centric over asset-centric and attacker-centric (Ch. 2).** Shostack contrasts three entry points: **asset-centric** (start from what you're protecting — the data), **attacker-centric** (start from who's attacking — threat personas), and **software-centric** (start from the software you're building — the DFD). He argues software-centric is the most reliable for engineering teams because the model (the DFD) is something the team already understands and can draw accurately, whereas asset lists drift and attacker personas invite speculation. Asset- and attacker-centric thinking are useful *supplements* for prioritization, not the primary structure.
- **Threat modeling belongs in the development lifecycle, iterated (Ch. 7, 12).** The book resists treating threat modeling as a one-time gate document. Model early (at design, when changing the model is cheap), revisit when the DFD changes (a new external ingestion source, a new data store), and treat the threat model as a living artifact tracked alongside the code. This is a direct fit for this repo's phase-gated SDLC: the threat model is a Design-phase artifact that the `pre-phase-advance` governance can require, and it must be re-validated whenever the architecture changes.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Draw the DFD first, boundaries second.** Before enumerating any threat, produce a DFD of the flow under review with all four element types labeled, then draw trust boundaries as dashed lines wherever trust changes: the per-tenant namespace edge, each Linkerd mTLS hop, the Google-Drive/S3 external-entity edge, and the PostgreSQL/Redpanda store edges. Number every element — threats are recorded against element numbers so the model and the findings stay linked.
- **Run STRIDE-per-element as a grid.** Build a table with one row per numbered element and one column per STRIDE letter; fill each cell that applies with the concrete threat and leave inapplicable cells struck out (external entities have no meaningful Tampering row, for instance). A completed grid *is* the "what can go wrong?" deliverable and its empty-vs-filled cells make coverage auditable.
- **Map every found threat to the violated security property and its mitigation.** Because each STRIDE letter names a property, each finding gets a mitigation lane automatically: Spoofing → authentication control (mTLS peer identity via Linkerd, JWT `sub`); Tampering → integrity control (signed events, `pgx` parameterized writes, Transactional Outbox); Repudiation → logging/non-repudiation (immutable audit trail, OpenTelemetry spans); Information disclosure → confidentiality (encryption at rest/in transit, ABAC filtering, PII never persisted as raw text); Denial of service → availability (rate limits, Redpanda backpressure, Circuit Breaker); Elevation of privilege → authorization (the ABAC `AccessPolicy.Evaluate` check, per-tenant scoping).
- **Prioritize with a documented scheme and validate what you defer.** Shostack warns against un-scored threat lists. Rank findings (the book discusses DREAD's weaknesses and favors simpler, explicit risk judgments); for every threat you decide *not* to mitigate now, record the accepted-risk rationale so "we deferred it" is a visible decision, not an omission.
- **Close the loop with question 4 explicitly.** Reserve a validation step that checks three things: (a) does the DFD still match the built system? (b) did we apply STRIDE to *every* element, not just the interesting ones? (c) is there a mitigation or an accepted-risk note for every filled cell? This "did we do a good job?" pass is the single most-skipped technique and the one that separates a real threat model from a diagram.
- **Reuse attack trees for the crown-jewel goal only.** Author or adapt one attack tree for the highest-value attacker goal in this domain — crossing the physical tenant-isolation boundary to read another tenant's classified data — rather than trying to tree everything. Use STRIDE for breadth and one attack tree for the depth case.
- **Keep the model in the repo and re-run on model change.** Store the DFD and STRIDE grid as a Markdown/diagram artifact versioned with the code, and treat any change to the DFD (new ingestion connector, new data store, a boundary moved) as the trigger to re-open the threat model.

## Key Terminology

- **STRIDE** — the six-category threat taxonomy (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege), each the violation of a specific security property (authentication, integrity, non-repudiation, confidentiality, availability, authorization). Worth adding to `glossary-management` as the canonical threat-classification vocabulary.
- **STRIDE-per-element** — the discipline of applying STRIDE to each individual DFD element rather than to the system as a whole, producing an auditable coverage grid. Distinct from "STRIDE-per-interaction," a heavier variant the book also discusses.
- **Data Flow Diagram (DFD)** — the software-centric model built from four element types (external entity, process, data store, data flow) that anchors question 1. Candidate glossary term alongside `Bounded Context` since it is the primary threat-modeling artifact.
- **Trust boundary** — a line on the DFD where the level of trust changes (the per-tenant isolation edge, an mTLS hop, an external-service edge); threats concentrate on flows that cross one. Precise, citable term worth glossary inclusion.
- **Attack tree** — a goal-rooted AND/OR decomposition of how an attacker reaches a specific objective; the depth-first complement to STRIDE's breadth-first discovery.
- **The Four-Question Framework** — What are we building? / What can go wrong? / What are we going to do about it? / Did we do a good job? — the method's top-level structure.

## Caveats

- **The book is from 2014 and predates this repo's stack.** None of its examples reference Go, chi, pgx, Redpanda, Linkerd, or Kubernetes; the mesh-mTLS-as-a-trust-boundary and Redpanda-topic-as-a-transient-data-store mappings above are this document's extrapolation of the book's language-agnostic modeling technique onto this repo's architecture, not content literally in the text.
- **STRIDE is a discovery aid, not a completeness proof.** Even a fully-filled STRIDE grid does not guarantee every real threat was found — the taxonomy structures the search but cannot enumerate novel or business-logic threats (e.g., an abuse of the classification workflow that violates no single STRIDE property cleanly). Treat the grid as a strong floor, not a ceiling.
- **DREAD and quantitative scoring are treated skeptically in the book and should be here too.** The book documents DREAD's inconsistency; the "prioritize with a documented scheme" technique above deliberately favors explicit, reviewable risk judgments over a false-precision numeric score. Do not import DREAD as if the book endorses it.
- **Attack trees are high-effort and easy to do badly.** The book is candid that authoring good attack trees from scratch is genuinely hard; the guidance above (one tree for the crown-jewel goal, STRIDE for breadth) reflects that — do not scope a threat model around building comprehensive attack trees.
- **Confidence: high on the framework, approximate on chapter numbers.** The Four Questions, STRIDE and its property mappings, the DFD element types, and the software- vs. asset- vs. attacker-centric distinction are the book's well-known, widely-cited contributions and I am confident in their substance. The specific chapter numbers cited are best-approximation signposts to the book's structure, not verified citations — treat them as navigational, not authoritative.

# The Privacy Engineer's Manifesto
**Authors:** Michelle Finneran Dennedy, Jonathan Fox, Thomas R. Finneran
**Domain:** Security — Privacy Engineering & Data Protection by Design

> Source note: This is a synthesis of the book's framework — privacy engineering as a discipline, the Fair Information Practice Principles, Cavoukian's Privacy by Design, and the PII data lifecycle — not a page-cited extract. The FIPPs, the PbD 7 principles, and GDPR/CCPA article structure referenced here are stable public standards; the framing and Go/repo-specific applications are this document's extrapolation of the book's language-agnostic argument onto this repo's stack.

## Core Concepts

- **Privacy engineering as a distinct discipline (Ch. 1–2).** The book's founding argument is that privacy is not a legal afterthought or a policy PDF but an engineering discipline with its own requirements, patterns, metrics, and test artifacts — practiced by builders, not only by counsel. Privacy must be *engineered in*, expressed as system behaviours a test can exercise, the same way reliability or security is. This reframes the compliance-design skill's own "Compliance as Code" thesis one level up: privacy obligations (minimisation, purpose limitation, erasure) are engineering requirements with acceptance criteria, not controls asserted "on faith" in a Word document.

- **PII is the atomic unit, and it has a lifecycle (Ch. 3–4).** The book insists privacy work begins by inventorying *personally identifiable information* precisely — distinguishing directly-identifying PII, linkable/quasi-identifiers, and sensitive categories — because the obligations differ per class. Every item of PII moves through a lifecycle: **collect → use → retain → disclose → destroy**. Privacy engineering places an explicit control at *each* stage (minimise at collect, bind purpose at use, TTL at retain, contract/consent gate at disclose, provable deletion at destroy). This repo's privacy-design skill has a Personal Data Inventory table but no lifecycle-stage control map — it lists categories, not the per-stage gate each category passes through.

- **The security/privacy distinction (Ch. 2, 6).** The book's sharpest conceptual contribution: **security protects data that exists; privacy governs whether the data should exist, why it may be used, and for how long.** A perfectly encrypted, access-controlled store of data you had no basis to collect is a security success and a privacy failure. Encryption at Rest, mTLS via Linkerd, and ABAC are *security* controls; data minimisation, purpose limitation, and retention are *privacy* controls, and one cannot substitute for the other. Several security-cluster skills conflate the two — treating "we encrypted it" as if it discharged a privacy obligation.

- **Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs) as the substrate (Ch. 4).** The book grounds all modern privacy regimes in the FIPPs: notice/awareness, choice/consent, access/participation, integrity/security, and enforcement/accountability (with collection limitation, purpose specification, use limitation, and data quality from the OECD lineage). These are the common ancestor of GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, and SOC 2's Privacy criteria — mapping a control back to its FIPP shows *why* it exists, not just *that* a regulation names it. compliance-design maps to SOC 2/GDPR/ISO article numbers directly, with no FIPP layer explaining the shared principle beneath, so a control that satisfies GDPR Art 5 and CCPA §1798.100 looks like two unrelated rows instead of one principle.

- **Privacy by Design — the seven foundational principles (Ch. 2, 8).** Cavoukian's PbD: (1) proactive not reactive / preventative not remedial; (2) privacy as the default setting; (3) privacy embedded into design; (4) full functionality — positive-sum, not zero-sum (privacy *and* utility, never traded); (5) end-to-end security across the full data lifecycle; (6) visibility and transparency — keep it open; (7) respect for user privacy — keep it user-centric. The privacy-design skill already lists these seven, so the book *confirms* that skill's spine — its added value is turning each principle into a lifecycle control and a metadata artifact rather than a table row.

- **Data minimisation as structural, not procedural (Ch. 4, 7).** The book's most operationally useful claim: the strongest privacy control is *not collecting or not retaining the data in the first place* — minimisation designed into the schema, so a privacy violation becomes structurally impossible rather than a discipline someone must remember. This maps exactly onto this repo's hard constraint that **raw extracted file contents are never stored — only entity types and counts.** The book gives that constraint its name and its generalisation: make the safe representation the *only* representable one, so "log the raw PII" has no data path, not merely a policy against it.

- **Privacy requirements engineering: use cases and metadata (Ch. 5, 7).** The book advocates capturing privacy as first-class requirements — *privacy use cases* (and misuse/abuse cases) and **privacy metadata**: every data element tagged with its purpose, legal basis, retention, sensitivity, and permitted recipients, carried as machine-readable annotation the access layer can enforce. This is the missing enforcement mechanism behind purpose limitation: a purpose written in a table is documentation; a purpose tag attached to the column and checked at the data-access layer is a control.

- **Consent, notice, and the individual's participation rights (Ch. 4, 6).** The book treats consent as an engineered artifact with lifecycle state (granted, scoped, withdrawn, expired), and notice/transparency as a system output, not a static privacy policy — the individual must be able to *see* what is processed and *act* (access, correct, delete). For this repo's B2B posture the book clarifies the controller/processor split the privacy-design skill already gestures at: the customer is controller of in-document PII; consent and participation for that data are the controller's relationship, not the processor's.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Build a PII lifecycle-control map, not just an inventory.** For every row in the Personal Data Inventory, add five columns — the control at collect, use, retain, disclose, and destroy. Example for extracted PII entities: collect = extract types+counts only (never raw text); use = purpose tag `compliance-detection`, ABAC-gated; retain = 90-day TTL on the partition; disclose = no third parties (contractual); destroy = scheduled partition drop, deletion itself audited. A row with a blank stage is an unguarded lifecycle transition.

- **Attach privacy metadata to the schema and enforce it at the pgx data-access layer.** Give each PII column a purpose tag, legal basis, sensitivity tier, and retention. Enforce purpose limitation as code: a query issued under purpose `security-audit` cannot read a column tagged purpose `compliance-detection`. This turns the privacy-design skill's Purpose Limitation table from documentation into a runtime check, matching compliance-design's "every behaviour has an automated check" bar.

- **Make the safe representation the only representable one (the minimisation type).** Model extracted findings as a type constructible *only* from `(EntityType, Count)` with no field and no constructor path that accepts raw text — the Go analogue of the book's structural-minimisation argument. "Logging raw PII" then has no data source to draw from; it is a compile-time impossibility, not a code-review catch. This hardens the privacy-design skill's "Storing what you promised not to" anti-pattern into a structural guarantee.

- **Map every compliance control back to its FIPP before mapping it forward to a regulation.** In compliance-design's coverage matrix, add a FIPP column. One "purpose limitation" FIPP row then unifies GDPR Art 5(1)(b), CCPA §1798.100(b), and SOC 2 Privacy criteria as three expressions of one principle — collapsing duplicate controls and revealing genuine gaps where no control serves a FIPP at all.

- **Write privacy use cases and misuse cases alongside functional ones.** For each feature touching PII, author a privacy use case (what data, what purpose, what basis, what retention) and its misuse case (re-identification via quasi-identifiers, purpose creep, over-retention). Feed the misuse cases to the threat-modeling skill so privacy harms (re-identification, linkage, secondary use) sit beside STRIDE security threats rather than being invisible to it.

- **Engineer consent and erasure as stateful artifacts with provable execution.** Model consent with explicit lifecycle state and scope; model retention as an enforced TTL/partition-drop job whose execution is itself written to the audit log. A documented 90-day retention with no scheduled deletion proves nothing — the book's accountability FIPP demands the deletion be demonstrable evidence, aligning with compliance-design's tamper-evident evidence pipeline.

- **Run a lightweight DPIA/privacy-risk model in the Design phase, keyed to the lifecycle.** Score each PII category on identifiability, sensitivity, volume, and lifecycle exposure; the highest-scoring transitions (e.g., disclose-stage for sensitive extracted entities) get the deepest review. This gives privacy-design's DPIA-trigger table a risk *model* rather than a yes/no trigger list.

## Key Terminology

- **PII lifecycle** — the five stages every item of personal data passes through (collect, use, retain, disclose, destroy), each requiring its own control. A precise, citable name for the structure the privacy-design inventory currently lacks; worth adding to `glossary-management` alongside the existing privacy terms.
- **Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs)** — the foundational set (notice, choice/consent, access/participation, integrity/security, enforcement/accountability, plus collection/purpose/use limitation) from which GDPR, CCPA, and SOC 2 Privacy criteria all descend. The common substrate a compliance matrix should map to before mapping to any single regulation.
- **Structural data minimisation** — designing the schema so the safe representation is the *only* representable one (types-and-counts, never raw text), making a privacy violation impossible rather than merely prohibited. The named generalisation of this repo's "file contents never stored" constraint.
- **Privacy metadata** — per-data-element machine-readable annotation (purpose, legal basis, retention, sensitivity, permitted recipients) carried with the data and enforced at the access layer. The enforcement mechanism behind purpose limitation.
- **Purpose limitation** — the FIPP/GDPR principle that data collected for one purpose may not be used for another without a new basis; distinct from access control (who may read) in that it constrains *why* a read is permitted.
- **Security vs privacy** — security protects data that exists; privacy governs whether/why the data should exist and be used. Worth a glossary note because several skills conflate the two.

## Caveats

- **The book is from 2014, pre-GDPR (2018) and pre-CCPA (2020).** Its regulatory references are the pre-GDPR landscape (Directive 95/46/EC, US sectoral laws, FTC FIPPs). The *principles* it teaches — FIPPs, PbD, the PII lifecycle — are exactly what GDPR and CCPA later codified, so they map forward cleanly; but do not cite this book for specific GDPR article numbers or CCPA sections (those post-date it). Ground article-level claims in the regulations themselves, as the compliance-design skill already does.
- **PbD's seven principles are Ann Cavoukian's, not original to this book.** The book teaches and operationalises them; attributing the seven principles *to* Dennedy/Fox/Finneran would be inaccurate. Likewise the FIPPs are an OECD/FTC lineage the book synthesises rather than invents.
- **The book is deliberately discipline-and-process oriented, not code-first, and language-agnostic.** It offers no Go, no pgx, no Linkerd — every stack-specific application above (the minimisation type, the pgx purpose-tag enforcement, the audited partition-drop) is this document's extrapolation of the book's principles onto this repo, not content literally present in the text.
- **This repo's physical per-tenant isolation and "never store file contents" posture already satisfy several of the book's harder-won recommendations.** The book argues for minimisation against systems that collect everything by default; this repo starts from a stronger baseline, so its techniques here reinforce and formalise an existing strength rather than closing an isolation gap the repo does not have.
- **Chapter references are approximate.** The chapter attributions above are my best approximation of the book's structure, not verified against the physical text — treat them as signposts, not citations to check page numbers against.

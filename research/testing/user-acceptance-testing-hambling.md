# User Acceptance Testing: A Step-by-Step Guide
**Authors:** Brian Hambling and Pauline van Goethem (BCS, The Chartered Institute for IT)
**Domain:** Testing (acceptance testing, the business side of the release decision)
**Also covered here, labelled separately:** the ISTQB acceptance-testing taxonomy (Certified Tester Foundation Level syllabus and glossary), as a **standard**, not as the book's content.

This file sits next to `agile-testing-crispin-gregory.md` (where UAT lives in the quadrants: Q3) and `explore-it-hendrickson.md` (how exploratory sessions run). It does not repeat them. It also relies on `../requirements-and-user-stories/lean-agile-atdd-pugh.md` for the pre-code side: ATDD is agreement *before* coding, UAT is acceptance *after* deployment. This file covers what neither of those books does: **who decides to accept a system, against what, with what evidence, and what happens when it is accepted with known defects.**

## Core Concepts

**From the book (concept-level recall; see Caveats):**

- **UAT is written for the business, not for testers.** The book's audience is business users and their managers who are asked to "do UAT" with no testing background. Its premise is that UAT belongs to the business that will live with the system. Its job is not to find as many bugs as possible. Its job is to give the business enough confidence to decide whether to accept the system into operational use. Finding defects is a side effect. The output that matters is the decision.
- **UAT owns its own test basis: business requirements and business processes, not the technical specification.** Earlier test levels (component, integration, system) are built from design documents and functional specifications. UAT is built from what the business said it needed and from how the business actually works. That means requirements, business process descriptions, and the day-to-day scenarios users run. A UAT test that just re-runs system-test cases with a user at the keyboard adds no new information. (`uat-plan` already says this; see Applies To.)
- **Acceptance criteria are agreed up front, as part of planning, by the people who will accept.** The book treats acceptance criteria as something to settle before execution starts, as the yardstick. They are not decided afterwards from whatever results came in. An acceptance decision made without criteria agreed in advance is a negotiation, not an acceptance.
- **The process is a sequence a non-tester can follow.** The book's "step-by-step" structure, as I recall it: understand what UAT is for → plan it (scope, schedule, people, criteria) → establish the test basis → design tests from business scenarios → prepare the environment and data → execute and record results → manage defects (log, triage, re-test) → report → reach the acceptance decision. The order matters. Each step consumes the output of the one before it.
- **Business scenarios over isolated functions.** UAT tests are designed around end-to-end business scenarios: a realistic piece of work a user does, crossing several functions. They are not designed one screen or one function at a time. The scenario is the unit a business user can judge as "yes, I can do my job with this."
- **Roles: business representatives execute, a UAT manager/lead coordinates, and someone with business authority accepts.** The book separates the people who run tests (business users, sometimes helped by testers), the person who plans and runs the UAT effort (a UAT manager or lead), and the person or body who formally accepts (a business sponsor or system owner). Development and supplier staff support but do not judge.
- **Environment and data realism are a precondition, not a nicety.** A UAT environment that does not resemble production produces results the business cannot rely on. Representative data matters as much as the environment. (`uat-plan`'s Environment section already covers this well.)
- **Risk decides how much testing is enough.** UAT time is always limited. The book directs that effort go where business risk is highest: the processes that matter most and would hurt most if they failed. It does not aim at even coverage.
- **Accepting with known defects is normal, and it has to be explicit.** Not every defect must be fixed before go-live. The business can accept a system with known defects if each one is understood, rated, given a workaround where needed, and scheduled for a fix. What must not happen is an *implicit* acceptance, where defects are simply left open and the system goes live anyway.

**From the ISTQB standard (labelled as standard, higher confidence than the book-specific points above):**

- **Acceptance testing is a test level** whose objectives are to establish confidence, validate fitness for use, and confirm the system meets the needs of users and other stakeholders. Finding defects is *not* its primary objective. A significant defect found at acceptance is treated as a project risk signal.
- **The five acceptance-testing forms** (CTFL syllabus):
  | Form | Checks against | Typically performed by | Typical content |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | **User acceptance testing (UAT)** | Fitness for use by intended users, in a real or simulated operational environment | Intended users | Business processes performed end to end |
  | **Operational acceptance testing (OAT)** | Readiness of the system to be *operated* and supported | Operations / system administration staff | Backup and restore; install, uninstall, upgrade; disaster recovery; user management; maintenance tasks; data load and migration; security vulnerability checks; performance |
  | **Contractual acceptance testing** | The acceptance criteria written into a contract for custom-developed software | Users or independent testers | Criteria defined when the contract was agreed |
  | **Regulatory acceptance testing** | Adherence to laws and regulations (government, legal, safety) | Users or independent testers, sometimes with regulators witnessing or auditing the results | Regulation-derived requirements, with retained evidence |
  | **Alpha and beta testing** | Feedback from potential or existing users before general release | Alpha: at the developer's site. Beta: at users' own sites | Real use in the user's own context |
- **Levels of test independence** (standard): no independence (authors test their own work) → independent people within the team → an independent test team within the organisation → independent testers from the business or user community → external independent testers. ISTQB is explicit that more independence finds different defects and brings different biases. It also has costs: isolation from the team, and slower feedback. Independence complements familiarity; it does not replace it.
- **Entry and exit criteria** (standard): entry criteria (in agile language, a Definition of Ready) say when an activity may start. Exit criteria (a Definition of Done) say when it may stop. Typical exit criteria are planned tests run, coverage reached, the number and severity of open defects below an agreed level, and residual risk evaluated. The last item means exit criteria are about **evaluated residual risk**, not only about zero counts.
- **Test completion report** (standard): at the end of a test level, a report summarises what was tested, deviations from plan, open defects and their risk, and residual risk. It is the evidence the acceptance decision is made against.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Pick the acceptance forms before writing the plan.** For each release, decide which of UAT / OAT / contractual / regulatory / alpha-beta apply. Each one that applies gets its own scope, its own executor, and its own line in the sign-off checklist. A compliance-sold product almost always needs regulatory acceptance. A hosted multi-tenant product almost always needs OAT. A contracted design partner may have contractual criteria.
- **Derive UAT tests from business scenarios, then trace them to requirements.** Start from "a compliance officer prepares for an audit" or "an office manager closes the day's lunch orders", not from the screen list. Then confirm every in-scope requirement is touched by at least one scenario. The two directions together give both realism and coverage.
- **Agree acceptance criteria and the defect bar in the plan, signed before execution.** This includes what severity of open defect is tolerable, and under what conditions. Changing them after results arrive is recorded as a decision in its own right. It is never a silent edit.
- **Rate every defect twice: severity (impact on the business) and priority (urgency of fix).** Triage decides fix-now, fix-later-with-workaround, or accept-as-is. A workaround is part of the defect record, written so a business user could follow it.
- **Re-test and regression-check every fix before it counts as closed.** "Fixed" means the failing scenario was re-run and passed, and the surrounding scenarios were re-checked. It does not mean "deployed".
- **Record results with a status set wider than pass/fail.** Pass, Fail, **Blocked** (could not run because of an environment problem or an earlier defect), and **Not run**. A blocked scenario is not a pass. A UAT report that hides blocked scenarios overstates readiness.
- **Close with a written report, then the decision.** Planned vs. executed, results, open defects with ratings and workarounds, residual risks, and a recommendation. The acceptance decision cites the report. It is not a separate impression.
- **Conditional acceptance = known defects + workaround + owner + date + re-test.** This is the standard shape of an acceptance with concessions. Each condition names the defect, its business impact, the workaround in the meantime, who fixes it, by when, and how the fix will be re-verified. ("Concession" is the quality-management term, from ISO 9001-style nonconformity handling, for knowingly releasing something that does not fully conform. Use it as a concept, not as a cited book term.)
- **Get independence from something other than headcount when people are scarce** (my synthesis from ISTQB's independence levels; neither source discusses a solo operator directly):
  - *Independence in time.* Acceptance criteria and UAT scenarios are written and frozen *before* the build is seen. In this pipeline they come from Ideate, which already happened. The executor then cannot quietly bend the test to fit what was built.
  - *Independence of the test basis.* The executor tests against criteria someone else, or an earlier self, agreed. They do not test against their memory of what they meant.
  - *Evidence anyone can audit.* Screenshots or a recording per scenario, tester, date, environment and digest. A later reviewer can then re-judge the result without trusting the executor.
  - *Borrowed independence.* Even a 30-minute session with one real intended user, a colleague, or a friend running two scenarios cold gives a second pair of eyes that no amount of self-testing gives.
  - *The agent is never the independent party.* The agent wrote the scenarios and facilitates the session. It cannot also be the check on them.
  - *Declare what you could not get.* State the independence level achieved, in ISTQB terms, in the sign-off ("no independence: product owner executed and accepted"). This turns a hidden weakness into a recorded, reviewable exception.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **No new agent.** Acceptance remains `requirements-analyst`'s decision to compile. The other forms' *execution* already has owners (`platform-engineer` for OAT, `security-engineer` for compliance evidence).
- **Probably no new skill either.** The acceptance-type selector is about 25 lines and fits in `uat-plan` (as a `references/acceptance-types.md` if it grows). An `operational-acceptance` skill would mostly restate `disaster-recovery-plan` and `runbook-authoring`. It is worth reconsidering only if enterprise dogfooding shows OAT needs its own plan artifact. If so, it would be a platform-engineer skill producing an `operational-acceptance-record`, consumed by the sign-off.
- **A `uat-completion-report` section, not a new artifact.** The ISTQB test completion report maps onto what `uat-plan`'s Day 4 "compile results" already does. Make it an explicit section of the UAT plan (or of the sign-off's evidence block) so lean products do not gain a new required file.

## Key Terminology

Candidates to check against `skills/glossary-management/`:

- **User Acceptance Testing (UAT)**, **Operational Acceptance Testing (OAT)**, **Contractual Acceptance Testing**, **Regulatory Acceptance Testing**, **Alpha Testing / Beta Testing**: ISTQB terms. The repo uses only the first and the last pair.
- **Acceptance Criteria** (already used), with the ISTQB sense made explicit: the criteria the accepting party uses to decide.
- **Test Basis**: what tests are derived from. For UAT, that is business requirements and processes.
- **Entry Criteria / Exit Criteria**: already used in `uat-plan`. They map to Definition of Ready / Done.
- **Residual Risk**: the risk knowingly accepted at sign-off. It is absent today.
- **Test Independence** (and its levels): absent. It is needed to name the solo-operator exception precisely.
- **Workaround**, **Severity vs. Priority**, **Re-test vs. Regression Test** (ISTQB "confirmation testing" vs "regression testing").
- **Concession / Conditional Acceptance**: the repo already says "conditional sign-off". Keep that as the canonical term and note "acceptance with concessions" as its synonym in the glossary, not in artifacts, to respect the no-synonyms rule.

## Caveats

- **I know this book less well than the other research files in this folder, and say so plainly.** I am confident about the book's existence, its authors, its BCS publisher, its business-user audience, and its overall thrust: UAT as a business-owned activity; test basis from business requirements and processes; scenario-based design; planning with agreed acceptance criteria; environment and data preparation; execution with defect management; reporting; the acceptance decision. I am **not** confident of its chapter structure, its exact role titles (I use "UAT manager/lead" and "business sponsor" generically), any specific template or checklist it prints, or the edition details. I have attributed nothing to it by page or quote, and I invented no statistics. Treat the "From the book" bullets as concept-level grounding. Where a skill change rests on a specific mechanism, the change leans on the **ISTQB standard**, which I recall with higher confidence and which is labelled as such throughout.
- **ISTQB material is a standard, not this book.** The five acceptance forms, the OAT content list, the independence levels, entry/exit criteria and the test completion report come from the ISTQB Certified Tester Foundation Level syllabus and glossary (v3.1 and v4.0 are broadly consistent on these points). ISTQB also publishes a Foundation Level *Specialist* syllabus on Acceptance Testing (aimed at business analysts and testers). I recall that it exists but not its detail, so nothing here is drawn from it. Before a skill cites ISTQB in `sources:`, confirm the wording against the syllabus text. ISTQB is not a `research/` book, so the citation belongs in prose ("ISTQB CTFL"), not in `sources:`.
- **"Concession" is quality-management vocabulary (ISO 9001-style nonconformity handling), not a term I can attribute to this book.** It is used here as a concept only.
- **The solo-operator guidance is my synthesis, not either source's.** Both the book and ISTQB assume an organisation with separate business, supplier and operations people. Neither discusses a single product owner who is also the only tester and the only accepting party. The "independence without headcount" techniques are reasoned from ISTQB's independence levels and from the owner's decision. They are not advice either source gives. The honest summary is that a solo sign-off has **no independence** in ISTQB terms. The techniques reduce the bias, they do not remove it, and the exception record exists so the reader knows that.
- **The book is pre-continuous-delivery in posture.** It assumes UAT as a phase before go-live on a dedicated environment, often in a supplier/customer relationship. This repo's UAT runs *after* deployment, on a canary tenant, as part of progressive delivery (`uat-plan/SKILL.md:32`). The book's process steps carry over. Its implicit "go-live happens after UAT" does not: here the sign-off widens a rollout that has already started. The skills already handle that mapping correctly, and nothing in this file should undo it.
- **Regulatory acceptance is framework-specific.** Neither the book nor ISTQB tells you *which* regulations apply. That comes from `compliance-design` per product. Do not let the selector hard-code SOC 2 (the same defect as W1-7 in `beta-program-design`'s validator).
- **Do not cite this file for exploratory testing or the quadrants.** Those belong to `explore-it-hendrickson.md` and `agile-testing-crispin-gregory.md`.

## Core Concepts

- **A Bounded Context is the boundary of one model and its language, nothing more by definition (Evans Ch. 14).** Evans introduces it to answer one problem: on a large project several models are in play, and when their boundaries are left implicit, code built on one model is quietly combined with code built on another, and both get worse. The Bounded Context states where a model applies and where its terms mean exactly one thing. Evans does say that team organisation, code base and database schema should line up with the boundary. That is advice for keeping a model whole, not part of what a context is. Khononov states the consequence plainly: a Bounded Context is a linguistic and model-consistency boundary; team ownership and deployment are separate choices, and they often line up with it but don't define it.
- **Subdomains belong to the problem space; Bounded Contexts belong to the solution space (Vernon, IDDD Ch. 2; *Distilled* Ch. 2–3).** A subdomain is a part of what the business does and exists whether or not software is built. A Bounded Context is a modelling decision the team makes. Vernon's goal is one subdomain per Bounded Context, and he grants that legacy systems often pack several subdomains into one context. Khononov agrees: subdomains are *discovered* and Bounded Contexts are *designed*. The two can map one-to-one, but nothing forces them to. One Core subdomain may span two contexts, and one context may hold a Core subdomain and a Generic one.
- **The Ubiquitous Language is ubiquitous only inside its Bounded Context (Evans Ch. 2 and Ch. 14; Khononov Ch. 2–3).** Evans's Ch. 2 claim is that the team uses one language based on the model in speech, in writing, in diagrams and in code, and that a change in the language *is* a change to the model. Khononov adds that the language belongs to the business, not to the engineers: it should have no technical jargon, no ambiguous terms and no synonyms. Once a term has to mean two things, the fix is two contexts, not a broader definition. Neither book treats a word list as the language.
- **A glossary is a record of the language, not the language (Khononov Ch. 2; Evans Ch. 2).** Khononov suggests a wiki glossary kept by the whole team, and warns that it captures the nouns. The behaviour (what happens, in what order, under which rules) has to live in the model, in Gherkin-style scenarios and in the code. Evans's view is close to this: documents support the spoken language and the code. They do not replace it, and a document that stops matching the code is worse than none. The skill-level consequence is to keep the per-context language with the product's model (D047 decision 9) rather than in one central vocabulary.
- **The language is found by talking the model through, not by naming things at a desk (Evans Ch. 2; Khononov Ch. 2).** Evans describes "modeling out loud": say candidate sentences about the domain, notice when one sounds wrong or clumsy, and treat that as a sign the model is wrong. Khononov's discovery rules are to ask the domain expert, question assumptions, and treat confusion over a word as the first clue that a boundary is near.
- **Evans names two subdomain types; Vernon and Khononov name three (Evans Ch. 15; Vernon IDDD Ch. 2; Khononov Ch. 1).** Evans's *Distillation* chapter sets the Core Domain against Generic Subdomains, and treats everything else as supporting material without a formal third type. Vernon makes Core / Supporting / Generic the standard triad. Khononov gives each type testable criteria:
  - **Core:** it gives competitive advantage, so it is complex (a simple Core is easy to copy and so gives no advantage), it changes often, and it is built in-house by the strongest people.
  - **Generic:** it is complex but already solved (everyone needs it the same way), so buy or adopt it.
  - **Supporting:** it is simple (often CRUD or ETL), gives no advantage, and is needed anyway. Build it cheaply in-house, or outsource it.
- **Classification decides investment, build-or-buy and how rich the patterns are (Evans Ch. 15; Khononov Ch. 1 and Ch. 10).** Evans: keep the Core small, put the best developers on it, and don't spend them on Generic parts. His Generic options are an off-the-shelf product, a published model, outsourcing, or an in-house build kept to the minimum. Khononov links the type to the business-logic pattern (already in `learning-ddd-khononov.md:8-14`) and to who builds it. Khononov Ch. 11 adds that types change over time (a Core capability can become Generic once the market catches up), so a classification has a date on it.
- **Subdomains are found by starting coarse and splitting until each piece has one type (Khononov Ch. 1).** Start from the company's departments or business capabilities. Split a capability into finer subdomains while the parts would get different types; stop when further splitting yields only sets of use cases of the same type. This is the procedure the classification step needs, not only the labels.
- **Distillation tools make the Core visible (Evans Ch. 15).**
  - **Domain Vision Statement:** about one page saying what the Core Domain is and what value it brings, written early and revised. It describes the model's value, not the product's market position.
  - **Highlighted Core:** a short distillation document, or flags on the model's elements, so anyone can tell which parts are Core.
  - **Cohesive Mechanism:** a complex, purely computational part moved out of the Core behind an interface.
  - **Segregated Core** and **Abstract Core:** refactorings for a Core tangled in an existing model, already covered in `subdomain-distillation/references/classification-techniques.md:116-141`.
- **A Context Map shows how things are today, including the mess (Evans Ch. 14; Vernon IDDD Ch. 3).** Evans: describe the points of contact between models, name each context, and draw the map as things exist, not as hoped. Vernon repeats "map what is" and keeps a target map apart from the current one. Each line between contexts shows which side is upstream (its changes flow down) and the relationship pattern on that line.
- **The relationship patterns, their sources, and when each fits:**
  - **Shared Kernel (Evans 2003):** two contexts share an explicit, small subset of the model and change it only by agreement, with shared tests. Khononov: worth it when duplicating the model costs more than coordinating, and natural when *one* team owns both contexts.
  - **Customer/Supplier Development Teams (Evans 2003):** the upstream team commits to the downstream team's needs. Evans has the downstream write automated acceptance tests that run in the upstream's suite, an early form of Consumer-Driven Contracts.
  - **Conformist (Evans 2003):** the downstream takes the upstream model as it is, because the upstream has no reason to accommodate it. Evans counts it as a legitimate, cheap choice when the upstream model is good enough. It isn't a failure.
  - **Anticorruption Layer (Evans 2003, built from facades, adapters and translators):** the downstream translates the upstream model into its own language. Khononov: use it when the downstream is Core, or the upstream model is messy or legacy, or the upstream contract changes often.
  - **Open Host Service (Evans 2003):** the upstream publishes a protocol for all consumers instead of one translator per consumer. Khononov: it separates the upstream's internal model from its public one and can serve several versions at once.
  - **Published Language (Evans 2003):** a documented shared exchange language, often paired with OHS. Khononov folds it into OHS rather than listing it apart.
  - **Separate Ways (Evans 2003):** no integration, because integrating would cost more than it returns. Khononov adds two reasons: the teams cannot communicate, or the capability is Generic and each side can use its own copy.
  - **Partnership (absent from the 2003 book; in Vernon IDDD Ch. 3 and Evans's later *DDD Reference*):** two teams whose contexts succeed or fail together plan their changes together. Khononov: it needs teams that communicate well and work in step, and it suits a single team badly because there is no second party.
  - **Big Ball of Mud (Foote and Yoder's 1997 term; added to the map vocabulary by Vernon and the *DDD Reference*):** a region with no coherent model. Draw a boundary round it, don't extend it, and reach it through an ACL.
- **Khononov groups the patterns by who has to cooperate (Khononov Ch. 4).** *Cooperation* covers Partnership and Shared Kernel. *Customer–Supplier* covers Conformist, ACL and OHS, chosen by where the power sits. *Separate Ways* stands alone. He also spells out what each pattern assumes about the teams. In an agent-built product with one owner, the team axis collapses for internal contexts, so the choice there rests on model quality and how often things change. The power reasoning still applies in full to external systems and vendors.
- **Contexts are not microservices, and are not meant to be as small as possible (Khononov Ch. 3, Ch. 10, Ch. 14).** Khononov: every microservice is a Bounded Context, but a Bounded Context need not be a microservice. How large a context should be depends on keeping its model consistent, not on a size target. His design heuristics say to start with wider boundaries, especially round Core subdomains, whose model is the least stable, and to split once the model settles; splitting early and wrongly costs far more than splitting late. One team may own several contexts, but each context has exactly one owning team.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Run the subdomain step before drawing contexts.** After Event Storming Big Picture (D047 decision 14), list business capabilities, split coarse to fine until each piece has one type, and classify each with Khononov's three tests (competitive advantage? complex? solved elsewhere?). Record a date and the evidence for each label.
- **Test "it's hard, so it's Core" against advantage.** A complex capability with no competitive advantage is Generic if a solution exists and Supporting if not. A capability that is simple is not Core, whatever its importance; in that case look for the real Core elsewhere.
- **Write a one-page Domain Vision Statement for the Core** as part of the classification artifact, kept separate from the product vision (`vision-statement`). It names what the Core model does that nothing off-the-shelf does.
- **Find contexts by language first.** Look for words that change meaning, and confirm with data ownership. Use team ownership only as a constraint (one team per context) where teams exist.
- **Start contexts wide.** When unsure whether a line is real, keep one context and record the candidate split with what would trigger it.
- **Draw the current map before the target map,** and mark each line's upstream side and its one pattern.
- **Choose a pattern in a fixed order.** First ask who has to cooperate (Khononov's three groups). Then ask whether the upstream model is good enough to adopt: if yes, Conformist; if no, ACL. Last, ask how many consumers there are: many means OHS with a Published Language.
- **For a candidate Shared Kernel, weigh duplication against coordination.** Keep the kernel small, list its members, and give it shared tests.
- **Keep each context's language with the product's model:** terms, definitions, banned synonyms and homonyms across contexts, plus at least one scenario per key term that shows the behaviour behind it.

## Key Terminology

- **Problem Space / Solution Space** — Vernon's split: subdomains describe the business (problem); Bounded Contexts are the team's modelling decision (solution).
- **Core Domain** — the subdomain that gives competitive advantage; complex, changing, built in-house by the strongest people.
- **Supporting Subdomain** — necessary, specific to the business, simple, no advantage; built cheaply or outsourced.
- **Generic Subdomain** — complex but solved the same way everywhere; bought or adopted.
- **Upstream / Downstream** — on a Context Map, the side whose changes affect the other (upstream) and the side affected (downstream).
- **Customer/Supplier** — the upstream team commits to meeting the downstream team's agreed needs, checked by the downstream's tests.
- **Conformist** — the downstream adopts the upstream model without translation.
- **Open Host Service** — the upstream's published protocol for all consumers, separate from its internal model.
- **Published Language** — a documented exchange format used across contexts, often with an OHS.
- **Partnership** — two teams that plan changes to their linked contexts together.
- **Separate Ways** — a deliberate decision not to integrate two contexts.
- **Big Ball of Mud** — a region with no coherent model, fenced off and reached through an ACL.
- **Domain Vision Statement** — a one-page statement of the Core Domain and its value (not the product vision).
- **Highlighted Core** — a short document or set of flags marking which model elements are Core.
- **Cohesive Mechanism** — a complex computational part moved out of the Core behind an interface.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** Bounded Context as a model and language boundary; the Context Map drawn as-is; the seven 2003 patterns and their intent; Evans's Core Domain and Generic Subdomain treatment and his distillation tools; Vernon's problem-space/solution-space split and three subdomain types; Khononov's subdomain criteria, his three pattern groups, "a microservice is a context but not the reverse", and his start-wide heuristic.
- **Uncertain:** chapter numbers, which vary across printings, and the exact chapter holding each Khononov heuristic (Ch. 3, 10 and 14 are best estimates). Ubiquitous Language is Evans Ch. 2, which sits in **Part I**, not Part II as the brief assumed. It is not certain whether Partnership and Big Ball of Mud first entered the vocabulary in Vernon's IDDD (2013) or in Evans's talks before it; both are in IDDD Ch. 3 and the *DDD Reference* (2015). That Evans names no formal "Supporting" type is recalled with medium confidence.
- **Unverified attribution:** the "would you outsource it?" test credited to Vernon in `classification-techniques.md:13`. Khononov clearly pairs outsourcing with Supporting. Check *Distilled* before keeping the attribution.
- **No quotations or page numbers.** Everything is paraphrased from working knowledge of the books, not a fresh re-read.
- **Team-based reasoning needs translating.** Most of the pattern reasoning assumes several human teams. In this plugin one agent models every internal context, so the team axis mostly applies to external systems; the Applies To rows say where.

## Core Concepts

- **A role-spectrum subtitle, not a single-role book.** The subtitle names four distinct roles/stages — Business Analyst, Product Owner, Product Manager, "Future Leaders" — as if on a single continuum rather than four unrelated jobs. That framing choice (worth taking seriously even without recalling the interior content) implies the book's organizing structure is likely a progression: BA as the entry/analysis-heavy role, PO as the backlog-and-delivery-facing role, PM as the strategy/outcome-facing role, and "future leader" as a further horizon beyond individual-contributor PM work (e.g., leading multiple product lines or a PM team). This is a genuinely different organizing axis than any book already in this cluster — Cohn and Lee teach story *craft*, Patton teaches story *mapping*, and this title (by its subtitle alone) teaches role *progression and boundary*.
- **Stories as pedagogical device, likely distinct from "user stories" as an agile artifact — a distinction worth stating explicitly rather than assuming.** The title's "with Stories" most plausibly refers to narrative case studies or vignettes used to *teach* PM/BA/PO practice (a common pattern in "learn X through stories/case studies" business books) — not necessarily the Cohn/Connextra "As a / I want / so that" artifact this repo already documents thoroughly via `user-story-writing`. I am not certain which sense the title intends, and it could plausibly be both (case-study narratives that themselves illustrate how to write good user stories). Any future skill or reader citing this book should verify which sense applies before assuming it duplicates Cohn's territory — see Caveats.
- **BA/PO/PM as genuinely distinct disciplines with different primary outputs — a widely-taught industry framing this subtitle invokes, independent of this book's specific treatment.** Conventional practice across the broader literature (IIBA's BABOK for the BA discipline, Scrum Guide for the PO role, and the general product-management literature for the PM role) draws these lines roughly as: a **Business Analyst** elicits, structures, and validates requirements and process detail, often bridging business stakeholders and a delivery team, with a primary output resembling this repo's Functional Requirements Document; a **Product Owner** owns the backlog for a single delivery team — prioritization, story readiness, acceptance criteria, day-to-day trade-off decisions — with a primary output resembling this repo's user story backlog, MoSCoW prioritisation, and acceptance criteria; a **Product Manager** owns market-facing strategy, vision, business outcomes, and cross-team prioritization, with a primary output resembling this repo's vision statement, OKRs, and roadmap (Strategy phase artifacts, not Ideate phase). A book with this exact subtitle almost certainly teaches some version of this same three-way split, likely through the lens of "here's a story about a day where a BA, a PO, and a PM each handled the same situation differently."
- **Role confusion and handoff friction as a recurring real-world theme.** Books that explicitly name multiple adjacent roles in a subtitle (rather than picking one) are typically responding to a genuine, common pain point: in practice, organizations blur BA/PO/PM boundaries constantly — a PO doing BA-style elicitation because no BA exists, a PM writing acceptance criteria because the PO role hasn't been staffed, a BA making prioritization calls that should be a PO's or PM's. A book aimed at "BAs, POs, PMs, and future leaders" together is plausibly teaching not just each role individually but the *handoffs and boundary disputes* between them — which is directly relevant to how this repo has already made a deliberate choice about where those boundaries sit (see Applies To below).
- **"Future Leaders" as an explicit career-progression frame.** Naming this as a fourth audience segment (distinct from the three named roles) suggests the book frames BA → PO → PM as a plausible career ladder, with a final chapter or section addressing what changes when someone moves from doing product work individually to leading people who do it. This has essentially no direct application to this repo (a single Han Solo operator with no team to lead), but is worth naming as content this repo should not attempt to extract guidance from — see Caveats.

## Actionable Techniques

Given the confidence limitations above, these are framed as **techniques the book plausibly teaches, based on its subtitle's role-spectrum framing** — not verified extractions:

- **Name which of the three roles (BA / PO / PM) is producing a given artifact, and check the artifact's shape against that role's typical primary output** — a BA-shaped artifact (a requirements document, a process map) should not silently be doing a PO's job (prioritizing a backlog) or a PM's job (making a business-outcome trade-off), and vice versa.
- **When a single person or agent is filling more than one of these roles, make the role-switch explicit rather than silent** — the plausible core discipline a book teaching all three roles together would emphasize is knowing *which hat you're wearing* at a given moment, since the reasoning, audience, and output differ by role even when the same person produces all three.
- **Use a short narrative case (a paragraph, not a bullet list) to illustrate a role boundary dispute before resolving it with a rule** — consistent with the book's apparent case-study/story-driven pedagogy, and structurally similar to how this repo's own `example-mapping` skill uses concrete scenarios (green cards) to surface a rule, rather than stating the rule abstractly first.
- **Treat "future leader" content as a distinct, later-stage concern from the individual-contributor BA/PO/PM content** — if this book is ever consulted further, separate what it teaches about *doing* the work (BA/PO/PM) from what it teaches about *leading people who do the work* (future leaders), since only the former has any plausible application to this repo's current, single-operator context.

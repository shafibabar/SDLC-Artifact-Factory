# Software Requirements (3rd ed.), with Mastering the Requirements Process (Volere)
**Authors:** Karl Wiegers and Joy Beatty (*Software Requirements*, 3rd ed., Microsoft Press); Suzanne Robertson and James Robertson (*Mastering the Requirements Process*, 3rd ed., the Volere method)
**Domain:** Requirements & User Stories (business analysis, requirements engineering)

## Core Concepts

- **Requirements come in three levels and several types, and mixing them up causes most requirements defects.** Wiegers and Beatty's model has three levels. **Business requirements** say why the organisation is building the product: objectives, success measures, the business opportunity. **User requirements** say what users must be able to do with it, written as use cases, user stories or event-response tables. **Functional requirements** say what the software must do so users can do those tasks. Alongside the levels sit other types that are not "levels": **business rules** (policies, regulations, formulas; these are not software requirements themselves, but they are the source of many), **quality attributes** (how well the product does things), **constraints** (limits on the design or build choices), **external interface requirements** (connections to users, other systems, hardware and communications) and **data requirements** (data definitions, data that must be kept, reports). A **feature** is a set of related functional requirements that gives the user a capability, which is a convenient unit for planning releases. The model lines up with Hathaway's four levels (`it-requirements-lean-agile-hathaway.md:7`): Hathaway's Stakeholder requirement is roughly Wiegers' user requirement, and her Solution requirement covers Wiegers' functional requirements plus quality attributes. Wiegers has no separate "transition" level. Instead, the book has a chapter on enhancement and replacement projects that covers migrating data and replacing an old system, and Volere's template gives migration its own section (below). This repo needs **one** taxonomy, not two side by side.
- **Classify every piece of input before you write anything.** Wiegers and Beatty tell the analyst to sort raw customer input into buckets: business requirement, user requirement, business rule, functional requirement, quality attribute, external interface, constraint, data requirement, and **solution idea**. The solution-idea bucket is the important one. "I want a dropdown" is recorded as a solution idea, and the analyst then asks what need it serves. This is the same discipline as Hathaway's "a want is not a requirement", but it comes as a concrete sorting step an agent can follow.
- **The vision and scope document holds the business requirements and draws the boundary.** In Wiegers' outline it has three parts: business requirements (background, opportunity, objectives, success metrics, a vision statement, business risks, assumptions and dependencies), **scope and limitations** (major features, what goes in the first release and what goes in later ones, and explicit **limitations and exclusions**), and business context (stakeholder profiles, project priorities, deployment considerations). The book also uses context diagrams, ecosystem maps, feature trees and event lists to show scope. In this repo, the Strategy artifacts already cover most of this (vision, OKRs, GTM, competitive analysis). The one part with no clear home is **scope per release, including exclusions**. Today that lands in the FRD's one-line Scope Statement.
- **User classes and product champions.** Users are grouped into **user classes** by how they use the product: how often, which features, what privileges, how expert they are. Each class is marked **favoured** (the product is built for them), **disfavoured** (the product should keep them out, e.g. an attacker or an unauthorised user) or **ignored**. Each important class gets a **product champion**: one real person who speaks for that class and makes decisions on its behalf. User classes are lighter and come earlier than personas. A persona is a picture of one member of a class. For a product run by one person, the product owner is the champion for every class by default, unless they name a design partner. That needs to be said explicitly.
- **Choose elicitation techniques to fit the project, not in a fixed order.** The book's techniques are interviews, workshops, focus groups, observation, questionnaires, system interface analysis, user interface analysis and document analysis. It gives guidance (in table form in the 3rd edition) on which suit which kind of project, for example document and interface analysis for replacing an existing system, observation for tacit work steps, and questionnaires for a large, spread-out user base. Elicitation is planned, and it goes back and forth with analysis; it is not done once. Volere calls this **trawling** for requirements, with an even wider set of techniques (apprenticing, scenarios, brainstorming, prototypes, and studying the current work).
- **Use cases and user stories are both ways to write user requirements; neither replaces functional requirements where detail matters.** Wiegers and Beatty prefer usage-centred elicitation. A use case has an actor, preconditions, a normal flow, alternative flows, exceptions and postconditions, and the alternative and exception flows are where many functional requirements come from. They treat user stories as a lighter form of the same level and accept them on agile projects, provided the detail (acceptance tests, functional detail where needed) is written down somewhere before it is built. Volere uses **business events** to set scope: the **business use case** is the work's response to an outside event, and the **product use case** is the part of that response the product carries out.
- **Good requirements have defined characteristics, checked one requirement at a time and across the whole set.** Each requirement should be **complete, correct, feasible, necessary, prioritised, unambiguous and verifiable**. The set as a whole should be **complete, consistent, modifiable and traceable**. The book gives a list of words that cause ambiguity, which the analyst should look for and replace: "user-friendly", "fast", "flexible", "robust", "support", "minimise", "and/or", "etc.", "as appropriate", "should". It also allows functional requirements to be written from the system's point of view ("The system shall…") or the user's ("The user shall be able to…"), as long as one style is used consistently. Unknowns are not hidden. They are marked **TBD**, collected on a TBD list, and resolved before the set is baselined. These characteristics are a fuller version of the repo's current Quality Criteria table.
- **Business rules have their own taxonomy and their own catalogue.** Wiegers and Beatty sort business rules into **facts**, **constraints**, **action enablers**, **inferences** and **computations**. They keep them in a business-rules catalogue with an ID and a source, so that many functional requirements can trace back to one rule. Rules exist apart from any one product. A regulation such as data residency stays a rule even when the software changes.
- **Quality attributes are written as measurable targets, ranked, and checked for trade-offs.** Wiegers and Beatty split quality attributes into **external** ones that users notice (availability, installability, integrity, interoperability, performance, reliability, robustness, safety, security, usability) and **internal** ones that developers notice (efficiency, modifiability, portability, reusability, scalability, verifiability). Their advice has four parts. First, don't try to specify every attribute: rank them and specify the few that matter. Second, write them with Tom Gilb's **Planguage** keywords: *Tag*, *Ambition*, *Scale* (the unit of measure), *Meter* (how it is measured), *Must* (the minimum acceptable), *Plan* (the target), *Stretch* and *Wish*. Third, draw an **attribute trade-off matrix** that shows which attributes help or hurt each other (more security usually costs some usability and performance, for example). Fourth, turn quality attributes into the derived functional requirements, constraints and design choices they imply. Volere's non-functional types are similar but grouped differently: look and feel, usability and humanity, performance, operational and environmental, maintainability and support, security, cultural, and compliance (legal and standards).
- **Volere's fit criterion: every requirement gets a measurable test of success, including functional ones.** In Volere, each requirement has a **description** (the intent, in the user's words), a **rationale** (why it exists) and a **fit criterion**. The fit criterion is a measurable statement of what a solution must do to satisfy the requirement, precise enough that a tester can say pass or fail. The Robertsons' test: if you cannot write a fit criterion, the requirement is not yet understood or is ambiguous. The fit criterion comes from the rationale, so a "fast" requirement turns into "fast enough for what?".
- **The snow card (requirement shell) is a fixed set of fields for every atomic requirement.** The Volere shell holds: requirement number, requirement type (its section in the Volere template), the event or use case it comes from, description, rationale, originator (source), fit criterion, **customer satisfaction** and **customer dissatisfaction** scores (1–5: how pleased stakeholders are if it is delivered, how unhappy if it is not), priority, conflicts (other requirements it clashes with), supporting materials, and history. The two satisfaction scores are close to Kano's idea that a missing basic feature hurts much more than it is worth when present (`user-stories-applied-cohn.md`'s Kano note).
- **The quality gateway: every requirement is checked before it enters the specification.** Volere checks each requirement as it is written, not all at once at the end. The checks: it has a fit criterion; it is relevant to the scope; it is complete and traceable to its source; it uses the project's defined terms consistently; it is solution-free unless it is a real constraint (no gold-plating); it does not conflict with other requirements; and it is feasible. For a pipeline where an agent writes requirements one after another, this is a better fit than one review at the end.
- **Volere's template puts things the repo does not cover today into named sections.** Its top-level sections are Project Drivers (purpose, client, customer, stakeholders, users), Project Constraints (mandated constraints, naming conventions and definitions, relevant facts and assumptions), Functional Requirements (scope of the work, business data model and data dictionary, scope of the product, functional requirements), Non-functional Requirements, and Project Issues. Project Issues covers open issues, off-the-shelf solutions, new problems, tasks, **migration to the new product**, risks, costs, user documentation and training, the **waiting room** (requirements kept for a later release), and ideas for solutions. Two of these are missing from this repo. The waiting room gives deferred items an honest place to go instead of being dropped. Migration and training is Hathaway's transition requirements under another name.
- **Prioritise with methods that weigh value against cost and risk.** Wiegers and Beatty describe several methods. **In or out** is a simple yes or no for this release. **Pairwise comparison and ranking** puts items in order. A **three-level scale** sorts items into high, medium and low. **MoSCoW** sorts items into Must, Should, Could and Won't. The **$100 method** gives stakeholders an imaginary budget to spread across items. Their own **value, cost and risk model** scores each feature on four things: the benefit of having it, the penalty of not having it, its relative cost and its relative risk. The scores are weighted, and features are ranked by value divided by cost plus risk. They do not treat prioritisation as a one-time step. A rough ranking early on is normal, and it is refined as cost and risk become clearer. This supports the product owner's decision: an early provisional priority, which MoSCoW finalises later.
- **Validate requirements with reviews, inspections, prototypes and tests written from them.** Wiegers' background is peer reviews. The book covers informal reviews and formal **inspections** with set roles (author, moderator, reader, recorder), a defect checklist, and a rule to review early and in small pieces. It also recommends writing **conceptual tests** from the requirements before any code exists, which finds ambiguity and gaps cheaply, and agreeing acceptance criteria with users. This is close to example mapping and ATDD (Pugh), but the book frames it as validating the *requirements*, not testing the product.
- **Once agreed, requirements are baselined, and changes go through a controlled process.** A **baseline** is the agreed set of requirements for a release, placed under version control. Each requirement carries attributes, most importantly a **status** (proposed, approved, implemented, verified, deleted, rejected), plus its origin, owner and release. A change to the baseline goes through a **change control** process: a change request, an **impact analysis** (what it affects, how much work), and a decision by whoever has authority (a change control board on large projects, the product owner on small ones). **Traceability** runs both ways. Backward, each requirement goes to its source (a business objective, user requirement or rule). Forward, it goes to the design, code and tests. Volatility (how often requirements change) is tracked as a risk signal.
- **On agile projects, requirements work still happens; it moves to just in time.** Wiegers and Beatty's chapter on agile projects says the same requirements activities happen, but they are spread out over time. High-level scope and business requirements come first. User requirements are kept as a prioritised backlog of stories. Detail and acceptance tests are added shortly before each item is built. Some documents, including the vision and scope, business rules, quality attributes and anything needed for regulators, are still kept and maintained. The Robertsons make the same point with three project shapes: **rabbit** (small, fast, iterative, documents kept to a minimum), **horse** (medium-sized, some formality) and **elephant** (large, long-lived, often regulated or contracted, full specification). How formal the requirements are should match the shape of the project.

## Actionable Techniques

- **Input classification pass (Wiegers).** Before drafting, the agent puts every candidate statement from the Strategy artifacts into one of nine buckets, including *solution idea*. A solution idea goes through Hathaway's why-chain until it states a need. It is never copied into the FRD as a requirement.
- **User-class table with favoured, disfavoured and ignored classes, and a named champion.** At the top of the FRD, one row per user class, cross-referenced to `user-persona`. A disfavoured class (for example an unauthorised tenant user) is often where security and isolation requirements come from that nobody would otherwise write down.
- **Requirement shell, scaled by profile (Volere snow card).** Every requirement has these fields: ID, type, source (originator or event), description, **rationale**, **fit criterion**, provisional priority, status, conflicts. At enterprise scale, add the customer satisfaction and dissatisfaction scores and a history entry.
- **Fit criterion in place of "Test:".** Write the measurable pass condition first. The verification method (integration test, usability test, review) is a separate field after it. A requirement with no fit criterion goes on the TBD list, not into the baselined set.
- **Ambiguity sweep.** Search each draft for the book's ambiguous words ("fast", "user-friendly", "support", "flexible", "etc.", "and/or", "as appropriate", "should") and the repo's own warnings (`requirements-analysis/SKILL.md:149`). Each hit is replaced with a number and a condition, or moved to the TBD list.
- **Quality gateway per requirement (Volere).** Check each requirement against the gateway (fit criterion present, in scope, traced, uses glossary terms, solution-free or a real constraint, no conflicts) as soon as it is written. Record what it fails.
- **Business-rules catalogue.** List rules with an ID, a type (fact, constraint, action enabler, inference, computation) and a source. Functional requirements and NFRs cite the rule ID instead of restating the rule.
- **Ranked quality attributes before writing NFRs.** Do a short pairwise ranking of the candidate attributes for this product. Only the top-ranked ones get full Planguage-style entries, and the rest get "no requirement, because …". This puts the lean-profile rule ("only the NFRs that change a design decision", `phase-requirements.json:150`) into practice.
- **Planguage-style NFR entries, scaled by profile.** Lean: *Scale* + *Meter* + *Must*. Standard: add *Plan*. Enterprise: add *Stretch* and per-tier targets. Having a separate *Must* (the minimum acceptable) and *Plan* (the target) is the clearest way to let targets scale without making every product aim for the top tier.
- **Attribute trade-off matrix.** List the chosen attributes against each other and mark where one helps or hurts another (+ or −). Each "−" pair where both have targets is a conflict to send to `enterprise-architect`. This is the grounding W1-13 asks for.
- **Derived-requirement pass.** For each NFR, write down the functional requirements it implies (e.g. a security NFR implies "lock the account after N failed attempts") and send them back to the FRD, so they are not left out of the backlog.
- **Value, penalty, cost and risk scores as the provisional priority.** At FRD time, score benefit and penalty from the traced source. Use relative 1–9 scores for cost and risk, filled in when sizes exist. MoSCoW at the end of Ideate turns the ranking into Must, Should, Could and Won't. The "penalty" score is the same idea as Hathaway's necessity test, expressed as a number.
- **TBD list and baseline.** The FRD's Open Questions table becomes the TBD list, and every TBD is tied to the requirement it blocks. When the product owner approves the document, it becomes the baseline. Any later change is recorded with an impact note: which stories, acceptance criteria, NFRs and tests it affects.
- **Waiting room.** Requirements that are found but deferred go in a named section with their reason, instead of being deleted. MoSCoW's Won't list draws from it.
- **Conceptual tests during review.** Before the FRD goes to the product owner, the agent writes one or two quick tests for each high-priority requirement. A requirement that no test can be written for is sent back for rework.

- **Business Rule** (fact, constraint, action enabler, inference, computation), **Quality Attribute**, **Constraint**, **External Interface Requirement**, **Data Requirement**.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** the three levels and the other requirement types; classifying customer input, including "solution idea"; the outline of the vision and scope document; user classes, favoured and disfavoured classes, and product champions; the characteristics of good requirements and the ambiguous-word warnings; TBDs; the business-rule taxonomy; the split between external and internal quality attributes, Planguage, and the trade-off matrix; the list of prioritisation methods (in/out, pairwise, three-level, MoSCoW, $100, value-cost-risk) and the benefit/penalty/cost/risk structure of Wiegers' model; inspections and conceptual tests; baselines, status attributes, change control, impact analysis and two-way traceability; Volere's fit criterion, snow card fields, quality gateway, the template's top-level sections, the waiting room, and business events with business and product use cases.
- **Less certain:** the exact weights and arithmetic in Wiegers' prioritisation spreadsheet (only the structure is given here); the exact contents of the elicitation-technique table; the order and wording of every Volere template section; the rabbit / horse / elephant metaphor (I believe it is from the Robertsons' 3rd edition, but check the source before quoting it); and whether either book discusses Kano by name (the satisfaction/dissatisfaction link above is my interpretation). No chapter numbers, page numbers or direct quotations are claimed. Book titles, authors and editions are as above. I am fairly but not completely sure of the publication years (about 2013 for Wiegers and Beatty, about 2012 for Volere), so they are left out.
- **Neither book assumes an AI analyst or a solo owner.** Workshops, focus groups, inspections with four roles, and change control boards assume a team. In this pipeline they turn into the agent applying the check to itself and the product owner approving at the gate. Self-review by the agent that wrote the requirements is weaker than a real inspection, so state that as a known limitation instead of claiming the inspection happened.
- **These are heavyweight texts, and they must be scaled.** Applying the full Volere shell or a full SRS to a lean product contradicts both books' own advice to fit the formality to the project, and also `phase-requirements.json` (the FRD is skipped at lean scale). Use the profile column in Applies To as the guard.
- **DORA delivery targets and test coverage figures do not come from either book.** Calling them "process requirements" is my reading of Wiegers' separation of product and project requirements. The figures themselves still need checking against `dora-metrics` and *Accelerate* (W1-11).
- **Planguage is Tom Gilb's notation**, which Wiegers and Beatty adopt and teach. If a skill cites it, credit Gilb as its origin and this file as the source.

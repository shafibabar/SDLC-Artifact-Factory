# Impact Mapping: Making a Big Impact with Software Products and Projects
**Authors:** Gojko Adzic (with supporting material from Gojko Adzic and David Evans, *Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories*)
**Domain:** Requirements & Discovery (outcome-based planning)

## Core Concepts

- **An impact map is a planning tool for choosing what to build, not a requirements document.** Adzic presents impact mapping as a lightweight, collaborative way to plan a product or project milestone. It is a mind map that ties the scope of delivery to a business goal through the people whose behaviour has to change. Its job is to expose the *assumptions* behind a plan so the team can test them and change course. It is not there to make a fixed scope look justified. He frames it as a cure for two kinds of waste: building software that is delivered as specified but changes nothing, and long backlogs where nobody can say why an item is there. The technique grew out of the "effect mapping" work of Mijo Balic and Ingrid Ottersten (Adzic credits them), and Adzic popularised it under the name *impact mapping*. This repo's `skills/impact-mapping/SKILL.md:25-35` already has the right purpose ("why are we building this?"). It explains that purpose through Cagan's feature factory rather than through Adzic's own framing: the map as a picture of assumptions to test.
- **Four levels, each answering one question.** **Why**: the goal, the reason we are doing this at all. **Who**: the actors whose behaviour can produce or prevent the goal. **How**: the impacts, meaning how those actors' behaviour should change. **What**: the deliverables, meaning what the organisation could do to support those impacts. The direction is the point. The map is built from the centre (the goal) outward, and every leaf deliverable must trace back to the goal along one path. The skill states the four levels accurately (`SKILL.md:37-52`, `references/impact-map-levels.md:1-36`), so this part needs no correction.
- **The goal must be measurable, and Adzic borrows the measurement discipline from Tom Gilb.** A goal like "improve onboarding" cannot be tested. Adzic recommends saying *what* is measured (a scale), *how* it is measured (a meter), and what counts as success, ideally with a current benchmark and a target. He also allows a constraint, meaning a level the metric must not fall below. A goal should also describe the problem being solved, not the solution. "Build a mobile app" is a solution, and the real goal is the business change the app was supposed to bring. The skill has the "measurable" and "outcome, not output" rules (`SKILL.md:100`, `references/impact-map-levels.md:41-64`). It lacks the scale/meter/benchmark/target structure. That matters here because `okr-authoring` already requires metric, baseline, target and owner on every Key Result (`okr-authoring/SKILL.md:134`), and the impact map should carry those fields through rather than restate the goal in prose.
- **Actors include more than users: indirect actors, and actors who can obstruct.** The Who level asks *who* can produce the desired effect, *who* can obstruct it, *who* the users of the product are, and *who* will be affected by it. Actors can be individuals, roles or groups, as long as their behaviour can be observed. Obstructing actors matter as much as helpful ones, because removing an obstacle is often the cheapest path to the goal. The skill's four actor types (primary / secondary / off-stage / blocker, `references/impact-map-levels.md:66-91`) and its "at least one blocking actor" quality rule (`SKILL.md:103`) are faithful to this, and that discipline is a real strength of the current skill.
- **Impacts are behaviour changes, not features, and they include behaviour you want to stop.** This is the level Adzic treats as the heart of the technique and the one teams most often skip. An impact says how an actor's behaviour should change: what they start doing, do more or less often, do faster, do differently, or stop doing. Impacts can be negative. The map can name behaviour that hurts the goal, such as actors churning or support calls rising, so that deliverables can be aimed at preventing it. The skill's phrasing formula ("starts / stops / does more / does less / does differently ... instead of the current behaviour", `references/impact-map-levels.md:127-130`) captures this well. The "behaviour you want to prevent" type is present only for blockers (`:82-83`), not for primary actors whose current behaviour works against the goal.
- **Deliverables are options, not commitments.** Adzic is clear that the What level holds *possible* deliverables. They are options, and many are expected never to be built. Deliverables are not limited to software: training, a process change, marketing, a partnership or a manual workaround can each cause an impact more cheaply than a feature. The skill gets the "hypotheses, droppable" stance right (`references/impact-map-levels.md:141-170`) but lists deliverables as "features, content, processes, or configuration options" (`:145`). That covers part of the range. It never tells the analyst to consider a non-software deliverable first, so an agent will tend to fill the What level with features alone.
- **Choose a path, deliver the smallest thing, measure, then re-plan.** The prioritisation step in the book is choosing *one path* through the map: one goal, then the most promising actor, then the impact most likely to move the goal, then the cheapest deliverable likely to cause that impact. That is the shortest path to the goal. The team delivers it, measures whether the behaviour actually changed, and goes back to the map. If the impact did not happen, the deliverable was a wrong guess and another option on the same impact gets tried. If it did happen, the team checks whether the goal moved, and if not, the assumption linking impact to goal was wrong. This loop is what makes the map a roadmap tool: milestones are expressed as impacts to achieve, not features to finish. The skill has the pruning steps (`references/impact-map-levels.md:172-196`), and its "stop as soon as the impact is achieved" rule (`:168-170`) is Adzic's. What it lacks is the *measure and re-plan* half. Nothing in the skill or its template records how the impact will be observed, or what happens to the map after the first deliverable ships.
- **Impact maps sit above user stories, and stories should describe behaviour changes too.** In *Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories*, Adzic and Evans push the same idea down to story level. Stories should describe a behaviour change, ideally a quantified one. Backlogs should be hierarchical (goal → impact → deliverable → story) rather than flat. And they distinguish a team's **zone of control** (what it ships) from its **sphere of influence** (how actors behave), with the outside world beyond both. The key point is that deliverables sit inside the zone of control, while impacts sit in the sphere of influence: the team can make them more likely but cannot guarantee them. This is a sharper version of the skill's litmus test ("could the team achieve this entirely on its own?", `references/impact-map-levels.md:58-61`). The book also places impact maps *before* story maps. The impact map picks which deliverable to pursue, and a story map then breaks that deliverable into a releasable journey.
- **Impact mapping is a facilitated group exercise and it should stay small.** Adzic describes building a map with business sponsors and delivery people together, timeboxed, at a whiteboard or in a mind-mapping tool. The facilitator keeps the room on the current level and refuses deliverables until goal, actors and impacts exist. A useful map is small: one goal, a handful of actors, a few impacts each, and deliverables only as deep as is needed to choose the next step. A map that grows to hundreds of nodes has become a work-breakdown structure and lost its point. The skill says nothing about facilitation or map size. Under this repo's single-agent-then-approval model the session cannot happen literally (the same adaptation `user-story-mapping-patton-economy.md` names for story mapping). The adaptation should be named rather than silently dropped.
- **Common mistakes Adzic warns about.** The ones I recall with confidence: expressing the goal as a solution or a feature; goals that cannot be measured; impacts written as software features ("use the dashboard") rather than behaviour; treating the map as a complete plan where every deliverable is committed (a disguised work-breakdown structure); maps that are too big or too detailed; and starting from a list of deliverables and drawing goals afterward to justify them. The skill's anti-patterns (`SKILL.md:107-127`) cover all of these except "too big / too detailed".

## Actionable Techniques

- **Copy the Key Result's measurement into the goal, field by field.** The goal section should hold metric (scale), how it is measured (meter), baseline (benchmark), target and deadline, plus an optional floor (constraint), all taken from the linked KR in `okr-authoring`. If the KR lacks any of these, send it back to `okr-authoring` rather than invent a number in the map.
- **For every actor, ask the four Who questions in order:** who can produce the effect, who can obstruct it, who uses the product, and who is affected by it. Record at least one obstructing actor, or state explicitly why none exists.
- **Write impacts as behaviour changes and give each one an observable signal.** Use the existing formula (`references/impact-map-levels.md:127-130`), then add a line saying how the change would be observed ("we would see it in ___"). An impact with no observable signal cannot be tested when its deliverable ships. Include at least one *prevent* impact where current behaviour harms the goal.
- **At What, consider a non-software option first.** For each priority impact, ask "what is the cheapest thing, not necessarily software, that might cause this?" before listing features. Combine this with the candidate-spread step from W1-41 (`unleash-creativity-with-ai-bier.md:5`): generate two to three times more candidates than will be kept, in two passes, the second under a constraint such as "no new code" or "one week".
- **Choose one path and mark the assumptions on it.** Instead of a MoSCoW-style priority column, mark the one selected path (actor → impact → deliverable), the assumption each link rests on ("if the Steward sees a progress meter, she connects the remaining sources"), and how that assumption will be checked. Every other branch stays as an option, not as a "Could".
- **Record a re-plan trigger.** For the selected path, write down what would make the team go back to the map: the impact signal not moving within a set time, or the impact moving while the goal does not. This makes the map a living roadmap input instead of a one-time scope document.
- **Keep the map small enough to read at once.** One goal per map; the skill's single-goal rule (`references/impact-map-levels.md:55-56`) is right. Detail deliverables only under impacts the team might choose next, and leave deeper branches as one-line placeholders.

## Key Terminology

Checked against `skills/glossary-management/references/ubiquitous-language.md`:

- **Impact Mapping**: already present (`:258`) with a sound definition. No change is needed beyond optionally adding "a planning technique whose deliverables are options to test, not commitments".
- **Impact**: used in the skill to mean a behaviour change in an actor. Not a glossary entry. It is worth registering, because "impact" in everyday product language means "effect or value", and a definition would stop "impact" from being read as "benefit of a feature".
- **Actor**: the skill's term for a Who-level person or group. Check against any existing persona/role terms before adding, so the glossary does not hold two words for the same thing.
- **Deliverable**: Adzic's What level. Note that it means *option*, not *committed scope*.
- **Selected path**: proposed replacement for the skill's "MVP scope" (see Applies To). Add it only if the skill adopts it.
- **Zone of control / sphere of influence**: Adzic and Evans' distinction. Add it only if the skill body uses it.

## Caveats

- **Confident:** the four levels and their questions; measurable goals and the Gilb-style scale/meter/target borrowing; obstructing actors; impacts as behaviour changes, including negative ones; deliverables as options that include non-software work; choosing one path and iterating on evidence; impact maps as a planning and roadmap tool that precedes story maps; facilitation as a collaborative, timeboxed exercise; and the main mistakes listed above. The effect-mapping origin (Balic and Ottersten) is something I am fairly confident Adzic credits, but verify it before citing it in a skill.
- **Less certain:** the exact wording of Adzic's four Who questions (the substance is right; the phrasing is mine); whether "benchmark" and "constraint" are presented in the book with exactly those labels or summarised from Gilb more loosely; the precise list and names of the book's "common mistakes" (I recall the substance, not the chapter structure); and which *Fifty Quick Ideas* chapters carry the zone-of-control and hierarchical-backlog ideas. I am confident those ideas are in that book, but not of their titles.
- **No page numbers, quotes or statistics are given**, deliberately. Nothing above is a direct quotation.
- **The book is from 2012 and is short.** It is a method, not a tooling guide. Mind-mapping tools and workshop logistics it mentions are not recommendations for this repo.
- **Don't attribute to Adzic:** the feature factory and four product risks (Cagan), the walking skeleton (Cockburn, via Patton), MoSCoW (DSDM), or the 0.7 OKR grading convention (Google/Doerr). The skill currently attributes these correctly. Keep it that way when merging this file's material.
- **The live workshop cannot happen literally** under the single-agent-then-approval model. The confirm-before-deliverables stop proposed above is an adaptation, not Adzic's practice. Name it as such in the skill.

# The DSDM Agile Project Framework (handbook), with DSDM Atern before it
**Authors:** Agile Business Consortium (formerly the DSDM Consortium). The MoSCoW acronym is credited to Dai Clegg. The framework was published as *DSDM Atern* (2007) and later as the *DSDM Agile Project Framework* (AgilePF, 2014 onward), and is available as an online handbook.
**Domain:** Requirements & User Stories (prioritisation, timeboxing, agile project delivery)

## Core Concepts

- **Fix time, cost and quality. Let the features vary.** DSDM's central idea is the "inverted triangle". A traditional project fixes the features and lets time and cost overrun. DSDM fixes time, cost and the agreed quality level at the start, and treats the features as the thing that changes. So the deadline is never the variable. When work runs late, lower-priority features are dropped, and the date and the quality bar stay where they were. MoSCoW and timeboxing exist to make this work: timeboxing fixes the time, and MoSCoW decides in advance which features give way. The current skill already states this framing (`moscow-prioritization/references/moscow-rubric.md:146-155`) but has no source for it.

- **The eight principles.** (1) **Focus on the business need**: every decision is judged by whether it serves the business goal, and the project delivers what the business needs when it needs it. (2) **Deliver on time**: the deadline is protected, and the project is planned in timeboxes. (3) **Collaborate**: business and technical people work together as one team, and the business roles are actively involved. (4) **Never compromise quality**: the quality level is agreed at the start and is not something to trade for features. Testing is continuous and built in. (5) **Build incrementally from firm foundations**: do "enough design up front" (EDUF) to set a stable base, and no more, then deliver in increments. (6) **Develop iteratively**: expect change, and use feedback to converge on the right solution. (7) **Communicate continuously and clearly**: through facilitated workshops, daily stand-ups, models and prototypes rather than long documents. (8) **Demonstrate control**: make plans and progress visible, measure delivery by the products delivered rather than by activity, and manage proactively. Principles 2 and 4 are the reason MoSCoW exists. If neither the date nor quality can move, the only thing left to cut is scope, and the cut has to be agreed before it is needed.

- **MoSCoW, as DSDM defines it.** In DSDM, MoSCoW is not a ranking of importance. It is a statement of what is **guaranteed** within a fixed timeframe, and what is contingency.
  - **Must Have.** These are the requirements the project guarantees to deliver. DSDM expands "MUST" as the **Minimum Usable SubseT**. The defining test is **"what happens if this requirement is not delivered?"** If the honest answer is "cancel the project, because there is no point delivering on the target date without it", it is a Must. The handbook gives these signals: without it there is no point delivering on the date; it is a legal or regulatory requirement; the solution would be unsafe without it; or the solution would not be viable without it. The equally important corollary is that **if there is any workaround, even a manual or painful one, it is not a Must**. It is a Should or a Could.
  - **Should Have.** Important but not vital. Leaving it out may hurt, but the solution is still viable. There may be a workaround, such as managing expectations, some inefficiency, an existing solution or paperwork. Should and Could are told apart by **how much pain** leaving it out would cause, measured as business value lost or the number of people affected.
  - **Could Have.** Wanted or desirable, but less important than a Should. Leaving it out has a smaller impact. The **Coulds are the main contingency**: they are the first thing dropped if the timebox or project runs late.
  - **Won't Have this time.** Requirements the business and the team have agreed will **not** be delivered in this timeframe. They are recorded because writing them down clarifies scope and manages expectations. They may be reconsidered for a later increment. "This time" is part of the name: it means "not now", not "never".

- **"Minimum Usable" means usable, not just minimal.** The Must set is not any collection of necessary items. It must together make a solution that works for its purpose. A set of Musts with a gap in the middle (a step the user cannot complete) is not a usable subset, so it does not meet DSDM's own definition. This is the bridge to Patton's walking skeleton (`user-story-mapping-patton-economy.md:7`). Both describe the smallest thing that works end-to-end. DSDM states it as a priority category, and Patton states it as a shape on the map. That is why the owner's decision to align Musts with the story map's MVP slice is consistent with DSDM, not only a convenience for this plugin.

- **The effort guidance: Musts no more than about 60%, Coulds about 20%.** DSDM recommends that Must Haves take **no more than about 60% of the effort** for a project, increment or timebox, and that Could Haves make up **about 20%**. Shoulds fill the rest. The purpose is **contingency**. Estimates are always wrong, and the Coulds (then the Shoulds) are the buffer that lets the Musts arrive on time when they are. A timebox loaded with 100% Musts has no buffer, so the first surprise breaks the deadline, and principle 2 says the deadline does not move. DSDM also says this percentage should be **adjusted for how far the estimates can be trusted**. A team with a new technology, an unfamiliar domain or no estimating track record should hold a *lower* Must percentage. A higher Must percentage is acceptable only when the estimates are reliable, the work is well understood and the team is established. So 60% is a ceiling on risk, not a target to fill.

- **Priorities are set at three levels and reviewed throughout.** MoSCoW is applied at the **project** level (in Foundations, against the high-level Prioritised Requirements List), at the **increment or release** level, and at the **timebox** level. The same requirement can have different priorities at different levels. A project-level Must may be a Could in the first increment if it is planned for a later one. When a Must requirement is broken down, its parts do not all inherit Must. Some parts of a Must feature are Shoulds or Coulds within the timebox. Priorities are reviewed at each timebox and increment boundary, and agreed with the business roles, not set by the team on its own.

- **Agree the rules before the work starts.** The handbook advises the business and the team to agree, before any prioritisation, what each category means for this project, who has the final say on priority (the business roles), how disputes are escalated, and what happens to Shoulds and Coulds when time runs short. Agreeing these first stops the categories being re-argued under deadline pressure.

- **The Prioritised Requirements List (PRL).** This is DSDM's single list of requirements, each with a MoSCoW priority. It is created at a high level in Feasibility, baselined at Foundations (at a level of detail that fixes scope without fixing every detail), and then refined and re-prioritised through Evolutionary Development. Detail is added just in time, in the timebox where an item is built. Other key products include the Terms of Reference, Business Case, Solution Architecture Definition, Development Approach Definition, Delivery Plan, Timebox Plan and Timebox Review Record, and the Benefits Assessment.

- **Timeboxing.** A timebox is a fixed period, usually a few weeks, with an agreed set of objectives and an end date that does not move. AgilePF describes a **structured timebox** with stages: Kick-off, Investigation, Refinement, Consolidation, Close-out. There is also a lighter free-format option. Daily stand-ups run inside it. Timebox plans are kept at timebox, increment and project level. If the timebox is running late, scope is reduced by dropping Coulds, then Shoulds, and the end date stays fixed. A timebox that ends without its Musts is a signal to re-plan, not to extend.

- **The lifecycle.** AgilePF: Pre-Project → Feasibility → Foundations → Evolutionary Development (iterated in timeboxes, repeated per increment) → Deployment → Post-Project. Atern named the middle phases differently (Exploration and Engineering), which AgilePF merged into Evolutionary Development. Foundations is where "enough design up front" happens: a baselined PRL, the solution architecture and the delivery plan.

- **The roles.** DSDM separates roles by interest. **Project-level roles**: Business Sponsor (owns the business case and the budget), **Business Visionary** (owns the vision and the business interpretation of the requirements, and has the final say on priority at project level), Technical Coordinator (technical authority and design coherence), Project Manager (plans and coordinates at a high level and does not command the team), Business Analyst (facilitates and records requirements; does not own them). **Solution development team**: Team Leader, **Business Ambassador** (the day-to-day voice of the business inside the team, who decides priority and acceptance at timebox level), Solution Developer, Solution Tester. **Supporting roles**: Business Advisor, Technical Advisor, Workshop Facilitator, DSDM Coach. The point that matters for this plugin is that **priority belongs to the business roles**. The analyst facilitates and records the decision but does not make it.

- **Facilitated workshops and modelling.** DSDM's core practices are MoSCoW, timeboxing, facilitated workshops (an independent facilitator, a set goal, the right people present), modelling, and iterative development. MoSCoW decisions are normally made in a workshop with the business roles present.

## Actionable Techniques

- **The "what happens if it is not delivered?" test, with the workaround clause.** For every candidate Must, ask what happens if it is not there on the date. It is a Must only if the answer is "there is no point delivering", "it is illegal", "it is unsafe" or "it is not viable". Then ask: "Is there any workaround, even a manual or painful one?" If there is, it is not a Must. The workaround question is the part most often skipped, and it removes most false Musts.

- **The usability test on the Must set as a whole.** After the item-by-item test, check the Must set as one thing: can the primary user complete the core journey end-to-end using only the Musts? If not, either a missing step needs a Must, or the release goal is wrong. In this plugin, this check is the walking-skeleton test from `story-mapping`.

- **Prioritise at the right level, and decompose priorities.** Assign release-level MoSCoW to stories. When an epic is a Must, do not mark all its stories Must. Run the test on each story. Some stories under a Must epic are Shoulds or Coulds (for example, edge-case handling or a second input format).

- **A prioritisation agreement before the bins.** Write a short header before any categorisation: what the constraint is, who decides (the product owner), what happens to Shoulds and Coulds if the release is under pressure, and how a disputed Must is escalated. In this plugin, the product owner holds both the Business Visionary and the Business Ambassador role.

- **Contingency check, sized to estimate confidence.** Check that the release has real contingency. There must be Shoulds and Coulds that could be dropped without breaking the Must set. Hold a larger buffer when confidence is lower. The section below says how to do this without person-weeks.

- **Won't Have this time, with a target.** Every Won't is recorded with its reason and the increment where it will be looked at again. It is re-examined at the next release's MoSCoW pass.

- **Re-prioritise at every boundary.** Re-run MoSCoW at each release (and, in Implement, at each build iteration) against that boundary's own constraint.

### Can the 60% guidance be expressed without person-week estimates?

The percentage is a means. The goal is: **the Musts must still arrive on the fixed date and quality when estimates turn out wrong, so the release must hold scope that can be dropped.** In this plugin, AI agents do the building, so there is no team capacity in person-weeks, and no skill produces such estimates (W1-9). There are three ways to handle it:

1. **Relative size.** Epics already carry a relative size of S/M/L/XL (`epic-definition/references/epic-format-and-criteria.md:81`), and stories have a Size column (`user-story-writing/SKILL.md:197`). Map the sizes to weights (for example S=1, M=2, L=3, XL=5) and report the Must share of the total weight. This keeps DSDM's ratio and needs no new estimating step. Its weakness is that the weights are made up and the ratio looks more precise than it is.
2. **Count-based ceiling.** Musts are no more than about 60% of the stories in the release. This is simple, but it is misleading when story sizes differ widely, so it is weaker than option 1.
3. **Drop the ratio and keep its purpose as structural checks.** (a) The Must set passes the Minimum Usable SubseT test and matches the walking skeleton. (b) The release holds a non-empty Should and Could tier that could be dropped, and it is listed in the order it would be cut. (c) Each Must has passed the workaround clause.

**Recommendation: drop the 60% rule as a gate, as the owner decided, and use option 3 as the replacement.** DSDM itself says the Must percentage should fall when estimates are less reliable, and an agent-built product has no estimating track record. A numeric ceiling would be false precision. Option 1 can stay as an **optional, report-only signal** at enterprise scale ("Musts are about X% of the release's relative size"). It is a prompt to look harder, never a pass/fail threshold. In an agent pipeline, the binding constraints are the release date set by the product owner, the model and token budget, and how much the product owner can review. The constraint statement should name these, not team capacity.

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

None needed. DSDM's MoSCoW content fits inside `moscow-prioritization`. A `references/dsdm-moscow.md` would hold the definitions, the levels and the reasoning for contingency. `templates/` would hold the updated backlog template, with a prioritisation-agreement header and a cut-order list. `resources/` would hold the checks run before handoff. Timeboxing, the DSDM lifecycle and the role model do not need their own skill. This plugin's phases, gates and product-owner approvals already do that job, and a timeboxing skill would duplicate `roadmap-authoring` and the phase-gate rules. If the Implement phase later plans builds in fixed iterations, timebox-level MoSCoW belongs as a short section in whichever skill plans those iterations, not as a new skill.

## Key Terminology

Candidates to check against `skills/glossary-management/` (which today has a User Story Mapping entry but no MoSCoW or Minimum Usable SubseT entry):

- **MoSCoW**: Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, Won't Have this time. A prioritisation of what is guaranteed and what is contingency within a fixed timeframe (DSDM; acronym by Dai Clegg).
- **Minimum Usable SubseT (MUST)**: the guaranteed set of requirements that together give a usable solution. Equal to the walking skeleton at release level in this plugin.
- **Won't Have this time**: agreed as out of this timeframe, recorded, and looked at again later. Use DSDM's wording rather than "Will not have".
- **Contingency**: the Shoulds and Coulds that can be dropped to protect the date and quality.
- **Prioritised Requirements List (PRL)**: DSDM's prioritised requirements list, the equivalent of this plugin's prioritised backlog.
- **Timebox**: a fixed period with fixed objectives and an end date that does not move.
- **Business Visionary / Business Ambassador**: the business roles that own priority. In this plugin, the product owner holds both.
- Keep apart: **MoSCoW Must** (a release-level guarantee) vs **Planguage Must** (an NFR minimum threshold, see the Wiegers file) vs **Kano Must-be** (a satisfaction class). These are three different meanings of "must", and the glossary needs to separate them.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** the inverted triangle (fixed time, cost and quality; variable features); the eight principles and what each means; the MoSCoW definitions, including Must = Minimum Usable SubseT, the "what happens if it is not delivered / cancel the project" test, the workaround clause, Should vs Could by degree of pain, and "Won't Have this time"; the guidance of no more than about 60% Must effort and about 20% Coulds, and that it exists for contingency; that the Must percentage should fall as estimate confidence falls; prioritisation at project, increment and timebox level; decomposing a Must's parts into different priorities; the role names and that business roles own priority; the PRL and its baselining at Foundations; timeboxing with a fixed end date and scope as the variable; and the AgilePF lifecycle phase names.
- **Less certain:** the exact names and order of the structured-timebox stages (I believe Kick-off, Investigation, Refinement, Consolidation, Close-out in AgilePF); whether the 20% figure applies only to Coulds or is quoted as a Should/Could split (most material I recall says Coulds about 20%, with Shoulds filling the rest); the exact Atern phase names (Exploration and Engineering) and how they map into Evolutionary Development; the full list of DSDM products and of the "instrumental success factors", which are not used here; and the exact publication years. **Atern vs AgilePF:** the MoSCoW definitions and the 60% guidance are, as far as I recall, the same in both. The main AgilePF changes were the lifecycle naming and a lighter, more general framing, but check the current online handbook before quoting wording. No page numbers, chapter numbers or direct quotations are claimed. The quoted phrases above are DSDM's standard terms, not passages.
- **The 60% recommendation is this file's interpretation, not DSDM's.** DSDM assumes a human team with estimates. Dropping the ratio as a gate in favour of structural checks adapts the framework to an agent-built product and follows the owner's decision. The adaptation is named here so it is not lost. If the plugin ever records real build effort (for example model time or tokens per story), a measured version of the ratio could come back.
- **Aligning Musts with the walking skeleton is a reading of "usable".** DSDM does not mention story maps. The alignment rests on "Minimum *Usable* SubseT" meaning a working end-to-end whole. That is sound, but it adds one rule DSDM does not state: legal, safety or regulatory Musts that are not on the skeleton must be added to it.
- **Do not credit Hathaway, Cohn or Patton with MoSCoW, and do not credit DSDM with Kano or the walking skeleton.** MoSCoW and timeboxing are DSDM's. The walking skeleton is Cockburn's term, as used by Patton. Kano is Noriaki Kano's, as used by Olsen.

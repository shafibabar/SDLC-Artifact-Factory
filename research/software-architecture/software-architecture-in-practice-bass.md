# Software Architecture in Practice (4th ed.), with the ISO/IEC 25010 product-quality model
**Authors:** Len Bass, Paul Clements, Rick Kazman (*Software Architecture in Practice*, 4th ed., SEI Series in Software Engineering, Addison-Wesley). ISO/IEC 25010 is an international **standard**, not a book: *Systems and software Quality Requirements and Evaluation (SQuaRE): product quality model*, first published 2011 and revised in 2023.
**Domain:** Software Architecture (quality attributes, architecturally significant requirements, architecture evaluation)

This file covers what the SEI book adds to how this repo writes and hands off non-functional requirements. It builds on two existing files and does not repeat them. `requirements-and-user-stories/software-requirements-wiegers-beatty.md` covers Planguage (Scale, Meter, Must, Plan, Stretch), ranking attributes and the attribute trade-off matrix. `fundamentals-of-software-architecture-richards-ford.md` covers architecture characteristics as competing forces and fitness functions. `documenting-software-architectures-clements.md` (the same SEI group) covers views and documentation packages. This book supplies the missing pieces: a **standard format for one NFR** (the quality-attribute scenario), a **way to choose which NFRs matter** (the utility tree), **design responses per attribute** (tactics) and a **way to check a design against them** (ATAM and its lighter versions). ISO/IEC 25010 supplies the **category list**.

## Core Concepts

- **A quality attribute is a measurable or testable property of a system, judged by how well the system satisfies its stakeholders' needs.** The book's core claim is that architecture exists mostly to achieve quality attributes. Functionality alone does not decide structure: almost any structure can deliver the features, but only some structures deliver them with the required availability, modifiability or performance. So quality attributes, not features, are what drive architectural design. The book also warns against two common faults in how people talk about qualities. First, an attribute name alone ("the system must be modifiable") cannot be tested, because every system is modifiable with respect to some changes and not others. Second, the attribute communities use different words for the same thing (an "event" in performance is an "attack" in security and a "failure" in availability). The scenario format below exists to fix both.
- **The quality-attribute scenario: the standard form for one testable NFR.** A scenario has six parts:
  - **Source of stimulus:** who or what causes the event (an end user, an attacker, an internal component, a developer, the hardware).
  - **Stimulus:** the event that arrives (a request, a crash, an attack, a change request, a deploy).
  - **Environment:** the conditions at the time (normal operation, peak load, degraded mode after a node loss, development time, startup).
  - **Artifact:** the part of the system that is affected (the whole system, one service, a data store, the user interface, the deployment pipeline).
  - **Response:** what the system does about the stimulus (processes it, detects and masks the fault, blocks the attacker and logs it, makes the change without side effects).
  - **Response measure:** the number that makes the response testable (latency at a percentile, downtime in minutes, time to detect, effort in person-days, percentage of attacks blocked).

  The environment and source fields are what make a target falsifiable. "P95 under 200 ms" with no environment is the "targets without load context" anti-pattern this repo already names (`nfr-specification/references/nfr-antipatterns-and-output.md:32-40`). In the scenario format the environment field is part of the shape, so leaving it out is visible.
- **General scenarios vs concrete scenarios.** A **general scenario** is system-independent: a template for one attribute that lists the possible values of each of the six parts (for availability: sources such as internal or external; stimuli such as omission, crash, incorrect timing or incorrect response; responses such as detect, log, notify, recover, degrade). A **concrete scenario** is the system-specific instance written for one product: "An unexpected crash of one classification worker during normal operation is detected, its work is re-queued, and no scan result is lost; throughput recovers within 2 minutes." The book uses general scenarios as checklists for eliciting concrete ones. For this repo, general scenarios are knowledge (a skill's `references/`), and writing concrete ones for a given product is the agent's job.
- **The attributes the 4th edition covers, one chapter each:** **availability, deployability, energy efficiency, integrability, modifiability, performance, safety, security, testability and usability**, plus a chapter on "other" quality attributes (among them variability, portability, development distributability, scalability and elasticity, mobility, and monitorability or observability; the exact list is less certain). Compared with the 3rd edition (2012), the 4th edition (2021) adds **deployability**, **energy efficiency**, **safety** and **integrability** as full chapters; integrability takes over from the 3rd edition's interoperability chapter. Each chapter follows the same pattern: what the attribute means, its general scenario, its tactics, and (in the 4th edition) a tactics-based questionnaire and related patterns.
- **Deployability is a quality attribute of the architecture, and it is the one closest to DORA.** The 4th edition treats deployability as a property of the *system*: how predictably, quickly and cheaply a change can be moved into production, and rolled back if it fails. Its general scenario has a stimulus such as "a new increment or bug fix is ready", an artifact such as one service or the whole system, and response measures such as time to deploy, cost, and number or percentage of failed deployments. Its tactics include managing the pipeline (scaling rollouts gradually, rolling back, scripting deployment commands) and managing the deployed system (managing service interactions so old and new versions can coexist, packaging dependencies with the service, feature toggles). This is what lets the book separate "can this architecture be deployed independently and rolled back safely" (a product property) from "how often does this team choose to ship" (a team and process outcome).
- **Tactics: the design responses for one attribute.** A tactic is a design decision that affects how the system responds to the stimulus in a scenario. Patterns bundle several tactics. The book's tactic families, roughly:
  - **Availability:** *detect faults* (ping/echo, heartbeat, monitor, timestamp, sanity checking, condition monitoring, voting, exception detection, self-test); *recover from faults* (active, passive or cold redundancy, rollback, retry, exception handling, graceful degradation, reconfiguration, software upgrade; then shadow operation, state resynchronisation, escalating restart); *prevent faults* (removal from service, transactions, predictive models, exception prevention).
  - **Performance:** *control resource demand* (manage the work request or sampling rate, limit event response, prioritise events, reduce computational overhead, bound execution times, increase efficiency); *manage resources* (increase resources, introduce concurrency, keep multiple copies of computations or data, bound queue sizes, schedule resources).
  - **Modifiability:** increase cohesion (split a module, redistribute responsibilities); reduce coupling (encapsulate, use an intermediary, restrict dependencies, abstract common services); defer binding (configuration, runtime registration, plug-ins).
  - **Security:** detect attacks; resist attacks (identify, authenticate and authorise actors, limit access and exposure, encrypt data, separate entities, validate input); react to attacks (revoke access, restrict login, inform actors); recover (audit trail, non-repudiation).
  - **Testability:** control and observe system state (specialised test interfaces, record and playback, localise state, abstract data sources, sandboxing, executable assertions); limit complexity (structural and behavioural).
  - **Usability:** support user initiative (cancel, undo, pause and resume, aggregate); support system initiative (keep a model of the task, the user and the system).
  - **Deployability, integrability, energy efficiency and safety** each get their own families in the 4th edition (for safety: avoiding unsafe states, detecting them, containing them through redundancy, barriers and limiting consequences, and recovering).

  Tactics belong at Design, not Ideate. An NFR states the scenario and its response measure; choosing a tactic to meet it is an architecture decision for `enterprise-architect`.
- **Architecturally significant requirements (ASRs) and the utility tree.** An ASR is a requirement that has a deep effect on the architecture: the architecture would be noticeably different without it. Most ASRs are quality-attribute requirements. The book's sources for ASRs are the requirements documents (which often lack them or state them vaguely), stakeholder interviews and workshops, and the business goals (the 4th edition has a method for eliciting quality attributes from business goals, PALM). The **utility tree** organises them. The root is "utility" (overall goodness of the system). The next level is the quality attributes; below each is a refinement (for performance: latency, throughput); the leaves are concrete scenarios. Each leaf is rated on two scales, usually **High / Medium / Low**: its **business value** (how much it matters if it is not met) and its **architectural impact or difficulty** (how hard it is to achieve, how much it shapes the design). Leaves rated (H, H) are the ones the design must address first; (L, L) leaves rarely need deep analysis. This is the book's answer to "which NFRs matter", and it is stronger than ranking attributes alone because it ranks *scenarios*, the unit that will actually be tested.
- **The Quality Attribute Workshop (QAW): eliciting scenarios before an architecture exists.** A facilitated, stakeholder-centred workshop: the business or mission is presented, then the architectural plan, then the architectural drivers are identified; stakeholders brainstorm scenarios, consolidate duplicates, vote on priority, and refine the top ones into the six-part form. Its outputs are a prioritised scenario list and a better shared understanding. In this repo there is one stakeholder (the product owner) and one analyst (an agent), so the workshop becomes the agent drafting scenarios from the upstream artifacts and the product owner voting at the gate.
- **ATAM and lighter evaluation methods: checking a design against its scenarios.** The **Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method** is the SEI's scenario-based architecture evaluation. In outline: present the method, present the business drivers, present the architecture, identify the architectural approaches, build the utility tree, analyse the approaches against the highest-priority scenarios, brainstorm and prioritise more scenarios with a wider group, analyse again, and present results. Its outputs are named precisely:
  - **Risks:** architectural decisions that may lead to an unmet quality attribute.
  - **Non-risks:** decisions judged safe, with the reason.
  - **Sensitivity points:** a property of one or more components that is critical for achieving one particular response (for example, the size of a connection pool sets latency under load).
  - **Trade-off points:** a property that is a sensitivity point for **more than one** attribute, in opposite directions (for example, the level of encryption improves security but costs latency). Trade-off points are where design decisions must be made explicitly.
  - **Risk themes:** groups of risks that point to a systemic weakness, tied back to the business goals they threaten.

  A full ATAM takes a team several days and a large group of stakeholders. The book also describes a **Lightweight Architecture Evaluation**: an in-house, much shorter version (about a day) run by the team itself, using the same utility tree and scenario analysis. The 4th edition also includes a tactics-based questionnaire per attribute, which a designer can run against a design alone ("does the system use heartbeat or ping/echo to detect a failed component?").
- **Trade-offs are the normal case, and the scenario format makes them visible.** Because every tactic has side effects on other attributes, the book treats trade-off analysis as the centre of architecture work. This is the same point Richards and Ford make, with sharper vocabulary: a **trade-off point** is something specific in a design, not a general feeling that "security and usability conflict". Wiegers' attribute trade-off matrix (attribute against attribute) works at requirements time; ATAM's sensitivity and trade-off points work at design time against specific decisions. They are two halves of the same check.
- **ISO/IEC 25010 as the category model.** The 2011 standard defines a **product quality model** of eight characteristics, each with sub-characteristics, and a separate **quality-in-use model** (effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, freedom from risk, context coverage):
  - **Functional suitability:** completeness, correctness, appropriateness.
  - **Performance efficiency:** time behaviour, resource utilisation, capacity.
  - **Compatibility:** co-existence, interoperability.
  - **Usability:** appropriateness recognisability, learnability, operability, user error protection, user interface aesthetics, accessibility.
  - **Reliability:** maturity, availability, fault tolerance, recoverability.
  - **Security:** confidentiality, integrity, non-repudiation, accountability, authenticity.
  - **Maintainability:** modularity, reusability, analysability, modifiability, testability.
  - **Portability:** adaptability, installability, replaceability.

  The **2023 revision** has **nine** characteristics. It adds **safety** (with sub-characteristics such as operational constraint, risk identification, fail safe, hazard warning and safe integration), renames usability to **interaction capability** (adding, among others, inclusivity, user assistance, self-descriptiveness and user engagement in place of aesthetics), renames portability to **flexibility** (adding **scalability**), renames reliability's maturity to **faultlessness**, and adds **resistance** under security. As I recall, the quality-in-use model was moved out of 25010 into a separate part of the series. The standard gives names and definitions; it does **not** give target values, measurement formulas (those are in ISO/IEC 25023) or a format for writing a requirement. That is why it pairs with the SEI scenario: 25010 says *which* qualities to consider, the scenario says *how* to write one, the utility tree says *which few* to write in full.
- **How these resolve the DORA question: the four DORA metrics are not product quality attributes.** None of the eight (or nine) 25010 characteristics is "how often the team deploys" or "what share of deployments fail". Those numbers describe the performance of a **team and its delivery process** over a period, measured across many changes. They depend on team size, release policy and how much work there is, as much as on the system. The SEI model draws the line in a usable place:
  - What **is** a product quality attribute: **deployability** (can one service be built, deployed and rolled back on its own, with no manual steps and without taking the others down) and, under 25010, **modifiability** and **testability** under maintainability. These are properties the architecture has or lacks, and they can be written as scenarios with response measures ("a fix to the scanning service is deployed to production with no downtime and no change to other services; rollback completes within 10 minutes").
  - **Time to restore** is mostly an **availability** and **recoverability** response measure (detect, then recover), already close to RTO. It belongs in the availability scenario for a failure, not in a separate delivery category.
  - **Deployment frequency, lead time and change failure rate** as rates are **process targets**: the product owner's choices about how the team works, calibrated against `dora-metrics`' clusters. They are legitimate things to record, but they are not NFRs of the product, so they should not be mandatory for every service at every scale, and their tables belong in one place (`dora-metrics`), not two (W1-11, W1-12).

## Actionable Techniques

- **Write every NFR as a concrete scenario.** Required fields scale with the profile (see Applies To): at minimum *stimulus + environment + response measure*; in full, all six parts. Put the Planguage *Must* and *Plan* (from the Wiegers file) in the response measure: "response measure: P95 ≤ 300 ms (Must), ≤ 200 ms (Plan)". The scenario says what is being measured under what conditions; Planguage says how good is good enough.
- **Keep a general-scenario library per attribute in the skill's `references/`.** One short table per attribute, with typical sources, stimuli, environments, artifacts, responses and response measures. The agent picks values from it to draft concrete scenarios, the same way the book uses general scenarios as an elicitation checklist.
- **Use the ISO/IEC 25010 characteristics as the coverage checklist, not as a quota.** Walk all eight (or nine) characteristics and their sub-characteristics. For each one, either derive at least one candidate scenario or decline it with a one-line reason ("portability: single cloud, no requirement"). Then keep only the ranked ones (next step). This replaces "address all 10 categories" with a standard-backed checklist.
- **Build a utility tree and rate each leaf (business value, architectural impact) as H, M or L.** Fully specify the (H, *) leaves; record (L, L) leaves in one line or drop them. Every leaf rated H on architectural impact is an ASR and goes into the Architecture Handoff. This turns the handoff's current fixed list of "strongest constraint types" (`SKILL.md:195-202`) into a rule that works for any product.
- **Scenario-driven conflict check before handoff.** For each pair of H-rated scenarios, ask whether meeting one makes the other harder (availability with cross-region failover vs data that may never leave one region; low latency vs encryption and audit on every call). Record each clash as a *candidate trade-off point* for `enterprise-architect`. This is the W1-13 fix, phrased in ATAM's vocabulary so the Design phase picks it up directly.
- **Separate product quality attributes from process targets in the document.** One section for product NFRs (25010 characteristics plus deployability, written as scenarios). One section, clearly labelled, for delivery and engineering-process targets (DORA rates, test coverage, CI duration, review gate), each one citing `dora-metrics` or the owning skill for its numbers and marked optional at lean scale.
- **Hand tactics to the architect, not to the NFR.** The NFR stops at the response measure. The handoff entry may list the *tactic families* that usually serve that scenario (redundancy and failover for an availability scenario, concurrency and caching for a latency one) as pointers, but choosing among them is Design-phase reasoning and an ADR.
- **At Design, run a lightweight evaluation against the utility tree.** After `container-diagram` and the first ADRs, `enterprise-architect` walks the top scenarios against the design and records risks, non-risks, sensitivity points and trade-off points. At lean scale, this is the tactics questionnaire for the two or three top scenarios. At enterprise scale, it is a one-day Lightweight Architecture Evaluation with the product owner, with risk themes reported at the Design gate.
- **Trace scenarios to tests.** Every scenario's response measure is the pass condition of a Quality-phase test (load test, chaos or failover drill, security test, usability test). The NFR ID stays the traceability key, as the skill already requires (`references/nfr-antipatterns-and-output.md:58-64`).

## Candidate New Skills/Agents

- **`architecture-evaluation` (candidate skill, owner `enterprise-architect`, phase design, domain architecture).** The knowledge for a scenario-based check of a design: the utility tree format, the lightweight evaluation steps, the definitions of risk, non-risk, sensitivity point, trade-off point and risk theme, the per-attribute tactics catalogue, and the tactics questionnaires, with an output template for an evaluation record. Its folder content would be: `references/tactics-<attribute>.md` (one per attribute), `templates/evaluation-record.md`, `resources/evaluation-checklist.md` and `examples/` (lean: a questionnaire on two scenarios; enterprise: a one-day evaluation with risk themes). Applying it to a given design is the agent's reasoning, which keeps the Skill = expertise rule. This is the one piece that has no home today: `enterprise-architect` has no skill that checks a design against the NFRs it reads first. Before creating it, check whether the tactics catalogue fits inside an existing skill instead (`integration-design` already covers some availability tactics such as retry, timeout and circuit breaker).
- **No new agent.** The quality-attribute workshop becomes a step `requirements-analyst` already owns (drafting scenarios, with the product owner voting at the gate), and evaluation belongs to `enterprise-architect`.
- **Explicitly not proposed:** a full ATAM skill. A multi-day evaluation with an outside team and a large stakeholder group does not fit a solo-operator, frugal plugin. The lightweight evaluation keeps the method's outputs at a fraction of the cost.

## Key Terminology

Candidates to check against `skills/glossary-management/`:

- **Quality Attribute** (the SEI term) and **Quality Characteristic / Sub-characteristic** (the ISO/IEC 25010 terms). Both overlap with Richards and Ford's **Architecture Characteristic** and Wiegers' **Quality Attribute**. The glossary should pick one primary term (quality attribute is the most widely used) and list the others as synonyms, so the NFR skill and architecture skills don't compete.
- **Quality-Attribute Scenario** with its six parts: **Source of Stimulus, Stimulus, Environment, Artifact, Response, Response Measure**. Note that *artifact* here means the part of the system affected, which clashes with this repo's use of "artifact" for a document the plugin produces. The glossary must say so, or rename the field in templates (for example "affected part").
- **General Scenario / Concrete Scenario.**
- **Tactic** (a design decision affecting one attribute's response), as distinct from a **Pattern** (a bundle of tactics). This repo already uses "tactical" for DDD tactical patterns; "quality-attribute tactic" keeps them apart.
- **Architecturally Significant Requirement (ASR)** and **Utility Tree**, with its two ratings (**business value**, **architectural impact**).
- **Risk, Non-risk, Sensitivity Point, Trade-off Point, Risk Theme** (ATAM outputs).
- **ATAM, Lightweight Architecture Evaluation, Quality Attribute Workshop (QAW).**
- **Deployability** as a product quality attribute, kept distinct from the DORA **delivery performance** metrics (already proposed for the glossary by the Accelerate file).
- **Process Target**, if adopted: a team or delivery-process target (DORA rates, coverage, CI duration) kept separate from product NFRs.

## Caveats

- **Recalled with confidence:** the six-part scenario and the general vs concrete distinction; the claim that quality attributes, not functionality, drive architecture; the tactic families for availability, performance, modifiability, security, testability and usability; the 4th edition's addition of deployability, energy efficiency, safety and integrability chapters; ASRs and the utility tree with its two H/M/L ratings; the QAW's purpose and broad steps; ATAM's outputs (risks, non-risks, sensitivity points, trade-off points, risk themes) and its broad step sequence; the existence of a lighter, in-house evaluation method; ISO/IEC 25010:2011's eight characteristics and their sub-characteristics; and the 2023 revision adding safety for nine characteristics.
- **Less certain:** the exact contents of the 4th edition's "other quality attributes" chapter; the full tactic lists for deployability, integrability, energy efficiency and safety (the families above are approximate); whether the 4th edition still calls the lighter method "Lightweight Architecture Evaluation" and how many hours it budgets; exactly where PALM appears; and the exact 2023 sub-characteristic names and the fate of the quality-in-use model. Check these against the book and the published standard before any skill quotes them. No chapter numbers, page numbers or direct quotations are given, and none should be added from this file.
- **Edition differences matter for citation.** The 3rd edition (2012) has interoperability instead of integrability and no deployability, energy efficiency or safety chapters. A skill that cites "Bass et al." for deployability must cite the 4th edition. Likewise, a skill must say whether it means 25010:2011 (eight characteristics) or 25010:2023 (nine); the names differ (usability vs interaction capability, portability vs flexibility).
- **ISO/IEC 25010 is a paid standard.** Its characteristic names and short definitions are widely reproduced, but the full text is sold by ISO. Under the repo's frugality rule, skills should use the names and their own short definitions, not copy the standard's text.
- **The DORA resolution is this file's reading, not a claim made by either source.** Neither the SEI book nor ISO/IEC 25010 discusses the DORA metrics. That they are process targets rather than product quality attributes follows from 25010 having no characteristic for them and from the SEI book treating deployability as a property of the system. It does not settle W1-11: which DORA cluster numbers are correct still has to be checked against *Accelerate* itself.
- **Both sources assume teams and stakeholder groups.** QAWs and ATAM rely on many stakeholders voting and an outside evaluation team. Here the product owner is the only stakeholder and the agent that wrote the scenarios also drafts the evaluation, so the evaluation is a self-check. Say that in the artifact instead of presenting it as an independent review.
- **Do not over-apply at lean scale.** Six-part scenarios for every characteristic, a full utility tree and a formal evaluation for a 20-person office lunch app would contradict both the book's advice to focus on the few ASRs that matter and `phase-requirements.json:150`. The profile row in Applies To is the guard.
